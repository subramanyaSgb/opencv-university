# 10.7 Colour detection with inRange

<Definition term="Colour detection">marking every pixel whose colour lies inside a chosen range, then turning the marked pixels into objects. In OpenCV the core step is `cv2.inRange`, usually on an HSV image.</Definition>

This chapter puts Module 10 to work: white balance (10.6), the right colour space (10.2), and a range test, followed by the clean-up steps that make the result robust enough for a production line.

## 1. An analogy

Sorting coloured beads with a sieve made of three filters: one lets through only a band of hues, one only beads that are colourful enough, one only beads that are bright enough. A bead must pass all three. Then you count the clumps that came through and ignore the dust.

## 2. The pipeline

<Flow

`cv2.inRange(src, lower, upper)` sets a pixel to **255 if every channel is within [lower, upper]** (inclusive), else 0. On HSV, the three conditions are: hue in a band, saturation at least a minimum, value at least a minimum.

## 3. Tune it

<InRangeLab caption="A shaded scene with orange, red, crimson and green caps, cardboard and a specular highlight. Tune the HSV range and watch the mask and the share of each object that is detected." />

Things to try:

- **orange** preset: the orange cap is found except its highlight; the cardboard is not (lower saturation). Lower S min to 60: the cardboard starts to appear.
- **red (no wrap)**: the red cap is found, the crimson one not. **red (wraps)**: both.
- Raise **V min** to 150: the darker right side of the scene drops out (shading).

## 4. Choosing the limits

1. **Collect samples**: draw ROIs on good examples of the colour under production conditions, on several parts and positions.
2. **Measure**: H, S, V statistics in each ROI (percentiles, not min/max, to ignore outliers).
3. **Set the hue band** a few units wider than the measured range; check that other colours stay outside.
4. **Set S min and V min** to exclude greys, highlights and shadows: these are the most important limits.
5. **Check on bad and neighbouring colours** (cardboard vs orange, crimson vs red).
6. **Fix lighting and white balance** before tuning; ranges tuned under one light do not survive another.

## 5. Making it robust

| Problem | Remedy |
| --- | --- |
| Noisy, speckled mask | blur before converting; morphological opening |
| Holes in objects (highlights, texture) | morphological closing; fill contours |
| Objects touching | separate with distance transform / watershed (27.4) |
| Small false regions | reject components below a minimum area |
| Shading across the part | rely on H and S; keep V min low; flat-field the light (15.3) |
| Colour drift over time | fixed light and white balance, periodic checks of a reference |
| Two similar colours | Lab and ΔE to reference colours (10.3), or a classifier (36.x) |

## 6. Industrial example

A capping machine must verify that each bottle has a cap of the ordered colour (orange, red or green) at 300 bottles per minute. The station uses a diffuse LED ring and manual white balance on a grey tile. For each frame: Gaussian blur 5 × 5, HSV conversion, one inRange per expected colour (red with the two-range wrap), opening and closing with a 5 × 5 ellipse, and connected components with a minimum area of 2000 pixels in the cap ROI. The decision is "exactly one component of the ordered colour, centred within ±15 pixels of the expected cap position". Brown cardboard dividers that once caused false "orange" detections are excluded by S ≥ 120; the reflections on glossy caps are filled by the closing step.

## Code

Three examples: inRange from scratch, a complete detection pipeline, and setting limits from a sample region.

### 1. From scratch: what inRange does

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

hsv = cv2.cvtColor(cv2.imread("sample-color.png"), cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)
lo, hi = np.array([10, 120, 60]), np.array([22, 255, 255])

ours = np.all((hsv >= lo) & (hsv <= hi), axis=2).astype(np.uint8) * 255    # from scratch: all three channels in range
theirs = cv2.inRange(hsv, lo, hi)
print("identical:", np.array_equal(ours, theirs), "| dtype", theirs.dtype, "| values", np.unique(theirs).tolist(), "| pixels", cv2.countNonZero(theirs))
```

Output:

```text
identical: True | dtype uint8 | values [0, 255] | pixels 5901
```

- Both limits are **inclusive**. The result is a single-channel uint8 mask with values 0 and 255.

### 2. OpenCV: a complete red detector

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
img = cv2.imread("sample-color.png")
cv2.rectangle(img, (250, 15), (299, 54), (60, 20, 200), -1)       # crimson square (hue near 173)
shade = np.linspace(1.0, 0.45, img.shape[1])[None, :, None]
img = np.clip(img * shade + rng.normal(0, 6, img.shape), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)   # uneven light + noise

hsv = cv2.cvtColor(cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (5, 5), 0), cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)   # 1. smooth noise, convert
red = cv2.bitwise_or(cv2.inRange(hsv, (0, 120, 50), (8, 255, 255)),     # 2. red: two hue ranges
                     cv2.inRange(hsv, (170, 120, 50), (179, 255, 255)))
k = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (5, 5))
red = cv2.morphologyEx(cv2.morphologyEx(red, cv2.MORPH_OPEN, k), cv2.MORPH_CLOSE, k)   # 3. clean the mask
n, labels, stats, cents = cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(red)          # 4. objects
for i in range(1, n):
    x, y, w, h, area = stats[i]
    if area < 200:                                                        # 5. ignore small specks
        continue
    print(f"red object: area {area} px, box ({x}, {y}, {w}, {h}), centre ({cents[i][0]:.1f}, {cents[i][1]:.1f})")
```

Output:

```text
red object: area 1982 px, box (250, 15, 50, 40), centre (274.6, 34.4)
red object: area 5782 px, box (28, 53, 85, 86), centre (69.9, 95.0)
```

- Both the pure red circle (H ≈ 0) and the crimson square (H ≈ 173) are found, despite shading to 45 % and noise.
- The circle's area (5782) is a little larger than its true 5525 pixels: the blur spreads its edge by a pixel or two. For accurate sizes, measure on the unblurred image inside the detected region.

### 3. OpenCV: limits from a sample region

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

roi = hsv[168:183, 30:290]                                         # a sample region on the orange bar
h, s, v = (roi[..., i].ravel() for i in range(3))
lo = (int(np.percentile(h, 1)) - 3, int(np.percentile(s, 1)) - 30, int(np.percentile(v, 1)) - 30)
hi = (int(np.percentile(h, 99)) + 3, 255, 255)
print("H, S, V 1st-99th percentile:", [int(np.percentile(c, 1)) for c in (h, s, v)], "to", [int(np.percentile(c, 99)) for c in (h, s, v)])
print("chosen range:", lo, "to", hi)
orange = cv2.inRange(hsv, lo, hi)
print("orange pixels:", cv2.countNonZero(orange), "| inside the red objects:", cv2.countNonZero(cv2.bitwise_and(orange, red)))
```

Output:

```text
H, S, V 1st-99th percentile: [14, 210, 128] to [16, 221, 240]
chosen range: (11, 180, 98) to (19, 255, 255)
orange pixels: 5821 | inside the red objects: 0
```

- The measured hue band is narrow (14–16); a margin of ±3 keeps it robust without touching red.
- S and V lower limits get a larger margin (30) because they vary more with lighting; the upper limits stay at 255. The 5821 detected pixels are the bar minus some of its darkest, noisiest right-hand end, where V falls below 98: lower the V limit if the whole bar must be found.

## OpenCV API notes

**Range test**: `cv2.inRange(src, lowerb, upperb)`: works on any number of channels; bounds as tuples, arrays or scalars; inclusive; output uint8 0/255.

**Combine masks**: `cv2.bitwise_or`, `cv2.bitwise_and`, `cv2.bitwise_not`.

**Clean**: `cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (k, k))`, `cv2.morphologyEx(mask, cv2.MORPH_OPEN / MORPH_CLOSE, kernel)` (23.2).

**Objects**: `cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(mask)` → number of labels, label image, stats (x, y, w, h, area), centroids; `cv2.findContours` for shapes (24.1).

**Mean colour of a detection**: `cv2.mean(img, mask=component_mask)`.

## Where it fails

- **Changing light colour** without white balance shifts all hues.
- **Neutral and dark colours** (black, grey, white, brown) have poor hue; use V and S, or Lab.
- **Similar hues** (orange vs brown, red vs crimson vs magenta) need careful S limits or a better colour space.
- **Mixed pixels** at edges have intermediate colours: object sizes shrink or grow with the limits.
- **Glossy surfaces**: highlights are white, reflections take the colour of surroundings.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **inRange on BGR** values for "red" (works only under fixed light and fails with shading).
- **Forgetting the second red range**.
- **Tuning ranges on one image** and deploying.
- **Min/max instead of percentiles** when measuring samples (one outlier ruins the range).
- **No minimum area**: noise specks counted as objects.

</Callout>

### From ranges to classifiers

An inRange box is the simplest colour classifier: an axis-aligned box in HSV. Real colour clusters are tilted ellipsoids (correlated H, S, V), so a box either misses parts of the cluster or includes neighbours. Better boundaries come from a Mahalanobis distance to the class mean (using the measured covariance), Gaussian mixture models, or a small classifier on (a\*, b\*) or (H, S) features (Part F). Back-projection of a colour histogram (12.5) is another classic alternative.

### Speed

`cv2.cvtColor` to HSV and `cv2.inRange` are both vectorised and multi-threaded; on a 5 MP image they take a few milliseconds each on a modern CPU. For a fixed colour range on 8-bit BGR input, the HSV conversion plus range test can also be precomputed as a 256³-entry lookup table (16 MB) mapping BGR directly to "in range" (TODO: verify speed benefit on the target CPU before using it).

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A colour detector works perfectly during the day shift and produces false detections at night. What do you investigate?

<Solution>

Ambient light: daylight through windows changes the light colour and intensity (the station is not shielded), so white balance and V/S levels differ between shifts. Also check auto exposure / auto white balance in the camera, lamp temperature (LED output depends on temperature), and whether night-shift parts come from a different batch. The fix is usually optical: shield the station, use its own constant light, fix camera settings, and log a reference patch to detect drift.

</Solution>
