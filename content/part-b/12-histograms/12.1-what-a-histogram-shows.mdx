# 12.1 What a histogram shows

<Definition term="Histogram">a table (or bar chart) of how many pixels have each grey value. It forgets where the pixels are and keeps only how often each value occurs, which makes it the quickest summary of an image's brightness and contrast.</Definition>

The histogram is the most used diagnostic in image processing. One glance tells you whether an image is dark or bright, has low or high contrast, is clipped, or contains two populations (object and background) that a threshold could separate. Many operations in this module and the next (equalization, thresholds) are computed from it.

## 1. An analogy

Sorting a class by height into bins of 5 cm and counting each bin. The chart tells you the average height, the spread, whether there are two groups (children and teachers), without saying who stands where. A histogram does the same with pixels and grey values.

## 2. Building a histogram

<Flow
  steps={[
    { label: "Image: one grey value per pixel", tone: "data" },
    { label: "For each value 0…255, count the pixels with that value", tone: "process" },
    { label: "Histogram h[v]; cumulative c[v] = h[0] + … + h[v]", tone: "result" },
  ]}
/>

- **h[v]**: number of pixels with value v; Σ h = number of pixels.
- **Normalised histogram** p[v] = h[v] / N: the probability of value v.
- **Cumulative histogram** c[v]: how many pixels are at or below v. Its normalised form rises from 0 to 1; where it crosses 0.5 is the **median**, 0.01 and 0.99 give the 1st and 99th percentiles (11.4).
- **Bins**: several values per bar (for example 32 bins of 8 values) give a smoother, less noisy histogram.

## 3. Reading histograms

| Histogram shape | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass on the left | dark, underexposed | longer exposure, more light, gain |
| Mass on the right | bright, overexposed | shorter exposure, less light |
| Narrow peak | low contrast | better lighting; stretch (11.4) |
| Spike at 0 or 255 | clipping: information lost | change exposure; never accept for measurement |
| Two separated peaks | two populations (part and background) | a global threshold will work (13.2) |
| Gaps (comb shape) | values were stretched digitally; levels missing | stretch less, or capture with more bits |
| Very wide, noisy | noise or high gain | more light, lower gain |

The histogram says nothing about **where** values are: a dark object on a bright background and a checkerboard can have identical histograms. Use it together with the image.

## 4. Histograms in inspection

- **Exposure monitoring**: log the percentage of pixels at 0 and 255 and the 1st/99th percentiles on every frame; alarm when they drift (lamp ageing, dirty lens, wrong camera setting).
- **Region statistics**: histograms of a mask (a part's ROI) for its mean, spread, and contamination by background.
- **Quick classification**: comparing histograms (`cv2.compareHist`) of a region with a reference, e.g. to check the right material or a missing coating.
- **Threshold selection**: Otsu (13.3) and others pick a threshold from the histogram alone.

## 5. Industrial example

A battery-cell weld inspection logs, for every image, the 1st and 99th percentile of the weld ROI and the fraction of clipped pixels. Over three weeks the 99th percentile crept from 210 to 245 and clipped pixels appeared: a reflector in the lighting had loosened and was focusing more light on the weld. The defect detection had not yet failed, but its margin was shrinking. The histogram trend found the problem before it caused false rejects; a five-minute maintenance fix restored the original values.

## Code

Two examples: a histogram and its statistics from scratch, and `cv2.calcHist` for exposure checks, masks and comparisons.

### 1. From scratch: counting and statistics

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
h_np = np.bincount(img.ravel(), minlength=256)                    # from scratch: count each value 0..255
h_cv = cv2.calcHist([img], [0], None, [256], [0, 256]).ravel()     # OpenCV: float32 counts
print("same counts:", np.array_equal(h_np, h_cv.astype(int)), "| total", int(h_np.sum()), "=", img.size)

c = np.cumsum(h_np) / img.size                                     # cumulative fraction
median = int(np.searchsorted(c, 0.5))
p1, p99 = int(np.searchsorted(c, 0.01)), int(np.searchsorted(c, 0.99))
mean = float((np.arange(256) * h_np).sum() / img.size)
print(f"from the histogram: mean {mean:.1f} | median {median} | 1 % {p1} | 99 % {p99}")
print(f"from the image:     mean {img.mean():.1f} | median {np.median(img):.0f} | 1 % {np.percentile(img, 1):.0f} | 99 % {np.percentile(img, 99):.0f}")
```

Output:

```text
same counts: True | total 64000 = 64000
from the histogram: mean 120.3 | median 117 | 1 % 6 | 99 % 252
from the image:     mean 120.3 | median 117 | 1 % 6 | 99 % 252
```

- 256 counts are enough to get the mean, median and percentiles: no need to sort millions of pixels. On large images this is much faster than `np.percentile`.
- `np.bincount` is the NumPy histogram for small non-negative integers.

### 2. OpenCV: exposure checks, masks and comparisons

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

def report(name, im):
    h = cv2.calcHist([im], [0], None, [256], [0, 256]).ravel()
    n = im.size
    print(f"{name:20s}: range {im.min():3d}..{im.max():3d} | at 0: {h[0] / n:5.1%} | at 255: {h[255] / n:5.1%} | levels used {int(np.count_nonzero(h)):3d}")

good = cv2.imread("sample-plate-good.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
poor = cv2.imread("sample-plate-poor.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
gain = cv2.imread("sample-noise-dark-gain.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
for name, im in [("plate, good light", good), ("plate, poor light", poor), ("dark scene, high gain", gain)]:
    report(name, im)

h_good = cv2.calcHist([good], [0], None, [32], [0, 256])          # 32 bins of 8 values each
h_poor = cv2.calcHist([poor], [0], None, [32], [0, 256])
print("histogram correlation good vs poor:", round(float(cv2.compareHist(h_good, h_poor, cv2.HISTCMP_CORREL)), 3))
mask = np.zeros_like(good); mask[50:150, 100:220] = 255            # histogram of a region only
print("ROI histogram pixels:", int(cv2.calcHist([good], [0], mask, [256], [0, 256]).sum()))
```

Output:

```text
plate, good light   : range  39..217 | at 0:  0.0% | at 255:  0.0% | levels used  61
plate, poor light   : range  69..183 | at 0:  0.0% | at 255:  0.0% | levels used 111
dark scene, high gain: range  10..255 | at 0:  0.0% | at 255:  0.0% | levels used 244
histogram correlation good vs poor: -0.157
ROI histogram pixels: 12000
```

- The "good" plate uses few levels (61) but they are spread far apart: two well-separated populations. The "poor" plate uses more levels in a narrow band: low contrast.
- The high-gain image uses almost all 256 levels: noise fills the histogram.
- `cv2.compareHist` with `HISTCMP_CORREL` gives 1 for identical shapes; here the two lighting conditions produce clearly different histograms (−0.16).

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.calcHist(images, channels, mask, histSize, ranges)`**: lists for each argument; `ranges=[0, 256]` (upper bound exclusive); returns float32 of shape (bins, 1). Several channels → multi-dimensional histograms (12.6).

**Comparison**: `cv2.compareHist(h1, h2, method)` with `HISTCMP_CORREL`, `HISTCMP_CHISQR`, `HISTCMP_INTERSECT`, `HISTCMP_BHATTACHARYYA`; normalise first (`cv2.normalize`) when images differ in size.

**NumPy**: `np.bincount(img.ravel(), minlength=256)`, `np.histogram(img, bins, range)`; **matplotlib**: `plt.hist(img.ravel(), 256, (0, 256))` or `plt.plot(h)` (6.5).

## Where it fails

- **No spatial information**: identical histograms for very different images; a small defect barely changes the histogram of a large image.
- **Bin choice**: too many bins on few pixels → noisy; too few → peaks merge.
- **Mixed regions**: the histogram of an ROI that includes background mixes populations.
- **Colour**: three separate channel histograms lose the joint colour information (12.6).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **`ranges=[0, 255]`** instead of `[0, 256]` (value 255 is then excluded).
- **Passing the image instead of a list** (`cv2.calcHist(img, …)` instead of `[img]`).
- **Comparing histograms of different-size images** without normalising.
- **Judging exposure from the mean only** (clipping can hide behind a normal mean).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The histogram as a probability density

The normalised histogram estimates the probability density of grey values. Image statistics computed from it (mean, variance, entropy H = −Σ p log₂ p) are the basis of thresholding methods (Otsu uses class means and variances, 13.3; entropy methods use H, 13.4) and of contrast measures. With few pixels, smoothing the histogram (kernel density estimation) gives a more stable density [1].

### Fast histograms

Histograms of sliding windows (for local thresholds or median filters) can be updated incrementally: moving the window one pixel removes one column and adds another, costing O(window height) instead of O(window area). Huang's and Perreault–Hébert's median filters are built on this idea (18.3) [2].

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Compute the entropy H = −Σ p log₂ p (in bits) of the three plate/scene images used above. Which has the most "information" in its grey levels?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

for name, im in [("good", good), ("poor", poor), ("high gain", gain)]:
    p = np.bincount(im.ravel(), minlength=256) / im.size
    p = p[p > 0]
    print(f"{name:9s}: entropy {-(p * np.log2(p)).sum():.2f} bits")
```

Output:

```text
good     : entropy 4.19 bits
poor     : entropy 6.12 bits
high gain: entropy 5.11 bits
```

Entropy measures how evenly the levels are used, not how useful they are: the low-contrast plate scores highest because noise spreads its values over many levels, while the well-lit plate (two clean populations) scores lowest. A high entropy is not the same as a good image.

</Solution>
