# 12.6 2D histograms

<Definition term="2D histogram">a count of pixels for every combination of two values, for example (hue, saturation) of a colour image, or (value in image A, value in image B) for two images. It is a grid instead of a row of bars.</Definition>

A 1D histogram answers "how many pixels have this value?". A 2D histogram answers "how many pixels have this pair of values?". It shows which values occur **together**, which is exactly what is lost when two channels are looked at separately. It is the model inside back-projection (12.5), a map of an image's colours, and, between two images, the basis of mutual information.

## 1. An analogy

A table of a class's heights tells you little about weights, and a table of weights little about heights. A scatter plot of height against weight shows that tall pupils are heavier, and spots the one who is not. A 2D histogram is that scatter plot with the points counted in squares.

For `sample-caps.png` with 30 hue bins × 32 saturation bins, 225 of the 960 cells are used. Each object forms a compact cluster at its own (H, S); the grey belt forms a long smear:

| Object | Pixels | Peak cell (H, S) | Cells used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Belt (grey) | 12 229 | (6, 16) | 182 |
| Orange cap | 797 | (12, 224) | 24 |
| Cardboard | 943 | (12, 128) | 18 |
| Red cap | 709 | (0, 216) | 6 |
| Crimson cap | 709 | (168, 224) | 7 |
| Green cap | 613 | (60, 192) | 9 |

- **Orange cap and cardboard share the hue cell 12** and differ only in saturation. A hue histogram alone shows one peak for both; the 2D histogram shows two clusters.
- **The belt spreads over almost every hue** at low saturation: grey has no reliable hue (10.2), so its noise scatters it along the hue axis.
- **Red and crimson** sit at opposite ends of the hue axis (0 and 168): hue wraps around, so they are neighbours in reality.

## 3. Display: log scale

In most images a few cells hold most pixels: here the belt cells hold 76 % of the image. Shown linearly, everything else is invisible. Plotting **log(1 + count)** compresses the large counts (11.2) so that small clusters show up. This is for viewing only; the counts themselves are not changed.

## 4. Two images: the joint histogram

Put the value of a pixel in image A on one axis and the value of the **same pixel** in image B on the other. For the two cameras of 12.4 (`sample-scene.png` and `sample-scene-camb.png`) the counts lie along a narrow curve: the camera B response. If B is shifted by a few pixels, the pairs no longer match and the cloud spreads out.

**Mutual information** (MI) measures how concentrated the joint histogram is compared with what independent images would give. With 64 × 64 bins:

| Shift of image B | 0 px | 1 px | 2 px | 5 px | 10 px |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MI (bits) | 4.15 | 2.63 | 2.54 | 2.39 | 2.23 |
| Correlation | 0.987 | 0.944 | 0.908 | 0.832 | 0.719 |

MI is highest when the images are aligned, even though their grey values are related by a non-linear curve. This is why MI is the standard similarity measure for aligning images from different sensors (for example CT and MRI).

## 5. Industrial example

A food sorter sets up colour classes for nuts: good (light brown), burnt (dark brown), mouldy (greenish grey). The engineer collects pixels from labelled samples and views their H × S histograms side by side. Good and burnt nuts overlap in hue but separate in saturation and value; mouldy ones form a small cluster at low saturation. The 2D view shows which channel pairs separate the classes before any classifier is trained, and the class histograms themselves become back-projection models (12.5).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A 2D histogram has 30 × 32 bins. How many numbers does it hold, and how many would two separate 1D histograms with the same bins hold?

<Solution>

**960** cells; separately **30 + 32 = 62**. The extra cells store which hue goes with which saturation.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Two objects have identical hue histograms and identical saturation histograms. Can their H × S histograms differ?

<Solution>

**Yes.** Example: object A has (orange, high S) and (red, low S); object B has (orange, low S) and (red, high S). The marginals are the same, the pairs are not.

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: an H × S histogram with OpenCV and NumPy, then the joint histogram of two images and mutual information.

### 1. Hue–saturation histogram

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-caps.png")
ids = cv2.imread("sample-caps-ids.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
hsv = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)

h = cv2.calcHist([hsv], [0, 1], None, [30, 32], [0, 180, 0, 256])            # shape (30, 32), float32
n, _, _ = np.histogram2d(hsv[..., 0].ravel(), hsv[..., 1].ravel(), bins=[30, 32], range=[[0, 180], [0, 256]])
print("same as np.histogram2d:", np.array_equal(h, n.astype(np.float32)), "| total", int(h.sum()), "| non-empty", int((h > 0).sum()))

names = ["belt", "orange", "red", "crimson", "green", "cardboard"]
for o, name in enumerate(names):
    m = (ids == o).astype(np.uint8)
    ho = cv2.calcHist([hsv], [0, 1], m, [30, 32], [0, 180, 0, 256])          # histogram of one object (mask)
    hb, sb = np.unravel_index(ho.argmax(), ho.shape)
    print(f"{name:9s} {int(m.sum()):5d} px, peak cell H {hb * 6:3d} S {sb * 8:3d}, {int((ho > 0).sum())} cells")

print("hue-only histogram: orange and cardboard peaks in the same bin:", int(cv2.calcHist([hsv], [0], (ids == 1).astype(np.uint8), [30], [0, 180]).argmax()),
      int(cv2.calcHist([hsv], [0], (ids == 5).astype(np.uint8), [30], [0, 180]).argmax()))
```

Output:

```text
same as np.histogram2d: True | total 16000 | non-empty 225
belt      12229 px, peak cell H   6 S  16, 182 cells
orange      797 px, peak cell H  12 S 224, 24 cells
red         709 px, peak cell H   0 S 216, 6 cells
crimson     709 px, peak cell H 168 S 224, 7 cells
green       613 px, peak cell H  60 S 192, 9 cells
cardboard   943 px, peak cell H  12 S 128, 18 cells
hue-only histogram: orange and cardboard peaks in the same bin: 2 2
```

- Rows are hue bins, columns saturation bins: `h[hb, sb]`. The bin's lower edge is `hb · 180 / 30` and `sb · 256 / 32`.
- The mask argument gives a per-object histogram without cutting the object out.

### 2. Joint histogram and mutual information

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

a = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
b = cv2.imread("sample-scene-camb.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)

def mutual_information(x, y, bins=64):
    j = cv2.calcHist([x, y], [0, 1], None, [bins, bins], [0, 256, 0, 256])   # channel 0 of x, channel 0 of y
    p = j / j.sum()
    px, py = p.sum(1, keepdims=True), p.sum(0, keepdims=True)
    nz = p > 0
    return float((p[nz] * np.log2(p[nz] / (px @ py)[nz])).sum())

for shift in (0, 1, 2, 5, 10):
    bs = np.roll(b, shift, axis=1)                                             # misalign by `shift` columns
    print(f"shift {shift:2d} px: MI {mutual_information(a, bs):.2f} bits, correlation {np.corrcoef(a.ravel(), bs.ravel())[0, 1]:.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
shift  0 px: MI 4.15 bits, correlation 0.987
shift  1 px: MI 2.63 bits, correlation 0.944
shift  2 px: MI 2.54 bits, correlation 0.908
shift  5 px: MI 2.39 bits, correlation 0.832
shift 10 px: MI 2.23 bits, correlation 0.719
```

- `cv2.calcHist([x, y], [0, 1], ...)` treats the list of images as one stack of channels: channel 0 is x, channel 1 is y.
- MI drops sharply after a 1-pixel shift: the joint histogram is very sensitive to alignment, which makes it a good target for registration.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.calcHist([hsv], [0, 1], mask, [hBins, sBins], [0, 180, 0, 256])`**: returns a float32 array of shape (hBins, sBins).

**Several images**: `cv2.calcHist([img1, img2], [0, 1], ...)` counts pairs across images (channels are numbered across the list).

**NumPy**: `np.histogram2d(x, y, bins, range)`; note that NumPy's last bin includes the upper edge, OpenCV's ranges exclude it.

**Display**: `np.log1p(h)` then `cv2.normalize(..., 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX)` and `cv2.resize(..., interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)` to enlarge the cells; or `plt.imshow(np.log1p(h), aspect="auto")`.

**3D**: three channels work the same way (`[0, 1, 2]`, three bin counts), but the bins multiply quickly: 32³ = 32 768 cells.

## Where it fails

- **Too many bins for the data**: most cells empty, clusters break into noise. Fewer bins or smoothing the histogram.
- **Hue wrap-around**: clusters near H = 0 and H = 179 look far apart.
- **Low saturation**: hue is undefined; grey pixels smear along the hue axis (mask them out with S > threshold).
- **Joint histograms need alignment**: they compare pixel pairs; a shift destroys the structure (which is also their use).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Reading rows and columns the wrong way round** (rows follow the first channel in the list).
- **Showing a linear-scale 2D histogram** of a natural image: one bright cell, everything else black.
- **Using 0–256 for 8-bit hue**: half of the hue bins stay empty.
- **Comparing histograms of different sizes** without normalising (divide by the total).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Mutual information

With p(a, b) the normalised joint histogram and p(a), p(b) its marginals, MI = Σ p(a, b) · log₂ (p(a, b) / (p(a) · p(b))). It is 0 for independent images and maximal when one image determines the other, whatever the (even non-monotonic) relation between their grey values. Viola and Wells, and independently Collignon, Maes et al., introduced MI for multi-modal image registration in 1995 [2, 3].

### Histogram comparison

`cv2.compareHist(h1, h2, method)` compares two histograms of the same shape, 1D or 2D: correlation, chi-square, intersection, Bhattacharyya distance, Kullback–Leibler divergence. Colour histograms compared this way were an early image-retrieval technique (Swain and Ballard, 1991; 12.5).

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Recompute the H × S histogram of `sample-caps.png` with a mask that keeps only pixels with S > 60. How many cells are non-empty now, and what fraction of the pixels is kept?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

mask = (hsv[..., 1] > 60).astype(np.uint8)
hm = cv2.calcHist([hsv], [0, 1], mask, [30, 32], [0, 180, 0, 256])
print(f"non-empty cells: {int((hm > 0).sum())} | pixels kept: {mask.mean():.0%}")
```

Output:

```text
non-empty cells: 40 | pixels kept: 23%
```

Removing the low-saturation pixels removes the belt smear: 225 cells shrink to 40, essentially the five object clusters, which makes the histogram far easier to read (and a better back-projection model).

</Solution>
