# 5.7 Eigenvectors and SVD

<Definition term="Eigenvector">a direction that a matrix only stretches, without turning it. The stretch factor is the eigenvalue. The SVD finds such special directions for any matrix.</Definition>

Which way does a part point? How elongated is it? What is the "main content" of an image? How do you solve the homography equations when there are more than enough points? All of these come down to finding the special directions of a matrix.

## 1. An analogy

Stretching a rubber sheet with a circle drawn on it. The circle becomes an ellipse. Along the ellipse's long and short axes, points only moved straight outwards or inwards; everywhere else they also slid sideways. Those two axis directions are the **eigenvectors**; how much they stretched are the **eigenvalues**.

## 2. Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

For a square matrix M, an eigenvector v and its eigenvalue λ satisfy

<Equation terms={[{ value: "M v" }, { op: "=", value: "λ v", result: true }]} />

- A scale matrix [[2, 0], [0, 1]]: eigenvectors (1, 0) with λ = 2 and (0, 1) with λ = 1.
- A rotation (not 0° or 180°): no real eigenvectors, every direction turns.
- A **symmetric** matrix (like a covariance matrix): always two real, **perpendicular** eigenvectors. This is the case used most in image processing.

## 3. Orientation of a part: PCA

Take all pixel positions (x, y) of a part (a mask from thresholding). Their **covariance matrix** (5.4, in 2-D) is

<Equation terms={[{ value: "C" }, { op: "=", value: "[[var(x), cov(x, y)], [cov(x, y), var(y)]]", result: true }]} />

Its eigenvector with the larger eigenvalue points along the part's **long axis**; the eigenvalues are the variances along the long and short axes. This is **principal component analysis** (PCA) in two dimensions. For an ellipse-shaped part drawn at 30° *(Code example 1)*:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Covariance C | [[1592.5, 784.8], [784.8, 684.9]] |
| Eigenvalues | 2045.3 and 232.2 |
| Major axis direction | (−0.866, −0.5): 29.98° |
| Elongation √(λ1/λ2) | 2.97 (the drawn ellipse is 90 × 30: ratio 3) |

`cv2.moments` gives the same orientation from the **central moments** μ20, μ02, μ11 (which are n × the covariance entries): θ = ½ atan2(2μ11, μ20 − μ02) = 29.98°.

An eigenvector's **sign** is arbitrary: (−0.866, −0.5) and (0.866, 0.5) are the same axis. Orientation from PCA is defined modulo 180°.

## 5. The SVD: any matrix, rotate–scale–rotate

Only square matrices have eigenvectors, and only symmetric ones have nice perpendicular ones. The **singular value decomposition** works for **every** matrix, of any shape:

<Equation terms={[{ value: "A" }, { op: "=", value: "U · Σ · Vᵀ", result: true }]} />

- **V**: perpendicular input directions; **U**: perpendicular output directions (rotations or reflections).
- **Σ**: diagonal, the **singular values** σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ … ≥ 0: how much each direction is stretched.

Every linear map is "rotate, stretch along the axes, rotate" (5.2). The singular values tell how "strong" each part of the matrix is.

## 6. Low-rank approximation of an image

A 200 × 320 image is a matrix (5.1). Its SVD writes it as a sum of simple "rank-1" images (one column pattern × one row pattern), ordered by importance. Keeping only the k largest *(Code example 2)*:

| Rank k | Numbers stored | Share of the pixels | PSNR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 521 | 0.8 % | 14.6 dB |
| 5 | 2 605 | 4.1 % | 23.4 dB |
| 20 | 10 420 | 16.3 % | 29.7 dB |
| 50 | 26 050 | 40.7 % | 34.0 dB |

As compression this loses to JPEG (4.3), but the idea, "keep the strongest components, drop the weak ones (mostly noise)", is central to PCA, denoising, background modelling and face recognition (eigenfaces).

## 7. Solving A h = 0 with the SVD

Many vision problems become "find h with A h = 0, h ≠ 0" (h only defined up to scale): the homography (5.3), the fundamental matrix, camera calibration. With noisy data there is no exact solution; the best h (minimising ‖A h‖ with ‖h‖ = 1) is the **right singular vector of the smallest singular value**: the last row of Vᵀ.

For the homography of 5.3, 4 point pairs give 8 equations for the 9 entries of H. The SVD solution, scaled so that h33 = 1, equals `cv2.getPerspectiveTransform` exactly *(Code example 3)*. With more points, the same code gives the least-squares homography (the DLT, 5.3).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of [[4, 0], [0, 1]]?

<Solution>

λ = **4** with (1, 0) and λ = **1** with (0, 1): a diagonal matrix stretches along the axes.

</Solution>

**Q2.** A part's covariance eigenvalues are 900 and 100. Elongation?

<Solution>

√(900 / 100) = **3**: the part is about three times as long as it is wide.

</Solution>

**Q3.** The singular values of a 100 × 100 image drop to almost zero after the 10th. What does that say?

<Solution>

The image is almost exactly a sum of 10 rank-1 patterns: very simple structure (for example smooth gradients and stripes). It could be stored with about 10 × 201 numbers instead of 10 000.

</Solution>

## Code

Three examples: orientation by PCA and moments, low-rank approximation, and the homography by SVD. Copy the sample images next to the script.

### 1. From scratch and OpenCV: orientation and elongation of a part

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

mask = np.zeros((200, 320), np.uint8)
cv2.ellipse(mask, (160, 100), (90, 30), 30, 0, 360, 255, -1)       # an elongated part, rotated 30 deg
ys, xs = np.nonzero(mask)
pts = np.column_stack([xs, ys]).astype(float)
C = np.cov(pts, rowvar=False, bias=True)                          # 2 x 2 covariance of the pixel positions
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)                                    # eigenvalues ascending, eigenvectors in columns
major = vecs[:, 1]
print("covariance:\n", np.round(C, 1))
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(vals, 1), "| major axis direction:", np.round(major, 3))
print(f"orientation {np.degrees(np.arctan2(major[1], major[0])) % 180:.2f} deg, elongation sqrt(l1/l2) = {np.sqrt(vals[1] / vals[0]):.2f}")
m = cv2.moments(mask, binaryImage=True)
theta = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * m["mu11"], m["mu20"] - m["mu02"]))
print(f"from cv2.moments: orientation {theta % 180:.2f} deg")
```

Output:

```text
covariance:
 [[1592.5  784.8]
 [ 784.8  684.9]]
eigenvalues: [ 232.2 2045.3] | major axis direction: [-0.866 -0.5  ]
orientation 29.98 deg, elongation sqrt(l1/l2) = 2.97
from cv2.moments: orientation 29.98 deg
```

- `np.nonzero(mask)` returns (rows, cols) = (y, x): note the order when building points.
- `np.cov(…, rowvar=False, bias=True)`: each row is one point; `bias=True` divides by n like the moments.
- `np.linalg.eigh` is for symmetric matrices: eigenvalues in **ascending** order, eigenvectors as **columns**.
- `% 180` folds the angle into 0–180°, since v and −v are the same axis. In image coordinates (y down) a positive angle is clockwise on screen.

### 2. NumPy: SVD and low-rank approximation of an image

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE).astype(float)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(img, full_matrices=False)                 # img = U @ diag(S) @ Vt
print("shapes:", U.shape, S.shape, Vt.shape, "| largest singular values:", np.round(S[:4]).astype(int))
for k in [1, 5, 20, 50]:
    approx = (U[:, :k] * S[:k]) @ Vt[:k]                          # keep the k strongest components
    stored = k * (img.shape[0] + img.shape[1] + 1)
    psnr = cv2.PSNR(img, np.clip(approx, 0, 255))
    print(f"rank {k:2d}: {stored:6d} numbers ({100 * stored / img.size:5.1f} % of the pixels), PSNR {psnr:5.1f} dB")
```

Output:

```text
shapes: (200, 200) (200,) (200, 320) | largest singular values: [33039  9925  3877  2852]
rank  1:    521 numbers (  0.8 % of the pixels), PSNR  14.6 dB
rank  5:   2605 numbers (  4.1 % of the pixels), PSNR  23.4 dB
rank 20:  10420 numbers ( 16.3 % of the pixels), PSNR  29.7 dB
rank 50:  26050 numbers ( 40.7 % of the pixels), PSNR  34.0 dB
```

- `full_matrices=False` gives the compact SVD: U is 200 × 200, Vt 200 × 320 (not 320 × 320).
- `U[:, :k] * S[:k]` scales each of the first k columns by its singular value (broadcasting), then `@ Vt[:k]` rebuilds the image.
- Rank k needs k columns of U, k rows of Vᵀ and k singular values: k · (200 + 320 + 1) numbers.

### 3. NumPy: the homography as the null space of A

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

src = np.float32([[70, 40], [270, 62], [255, 170], [52, 140]])
dst = np.float32([[0, 0], [240, 0], [240, 120], [0, 120]])
rows = []
for (x, y), (u, v) in zip(src, dst):                              # 2 equations per point pair: A h = 0
    rows.append([x, y, 1, 0, 0, 0, -u * x, -u * y, -u])
    rows.append([0, 0, 0, x, y, 1, -v * x, -v * y, -v])
A = np.array(rows, float)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
h = Vt[-1]                                                        # right singular vector of the smallest singular value
H = (h / h[-1]).reshape(3, 3)
print("A:", A.shape, "-> 8 equations, 9 unknowns: h is the direction A cannot see (its null space)")
print("check A @ h = 0:", np.allclose(A @ h, 0, atol=1e-6))
print("H by SVD equals getPerspectiveTransform:", np.allclose(H, cv2.getPerspectiveTransform(src, dst)))
```

Output:

```text
A: (8, 9) -> 8 equations, 9 unknowns: h is the direction A cannot see (its null space)
check A @ h = 0: True
H by SVD equals getPerspectiveTransform: True
```

- Each pair gives u = (h1 x + h2 y + h3)/(h7 x + h8 y + h9) and the same for v; multiplying out gives the two linear rows.
- `Vt[-1]` is the last row of Vᵀ: the unit vector that A shrinks most (to 0 here).
- With noisy points and more than 4 pairs, normalise the coordinates first for numerical stability (Hartley normalisation, 5.3 Go deeper).

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.moments(src, binaryImage)`**: spatial moments m00 (area), m10, m01 (centroid = m10/m00, m01/m00), central moments mu20, mu02, mu11 …, and normalised moments; `cv2.HuMoments` for rotation-invariant shape features (Part C).

**`cv2.PCACompute2(data, mean)`** → mean, eigenvectors, eigenvalues for rows of data (`float32`/`float64`); **`cv2.eigen(symmetric)`**, **`cv2.SVDecomp(src)`** → w, u, vt.

**`cv2.minAreaRect(points)`**: rotated bounding box with an angle (OpenCV's own angle convention, check its documentation); `cv2.fitEllipse` (5.6): another orientation estimate from the outline.

**NumPy**: `np.linalg.eigh` (symmetric), `np.linalg.eig` (general), `np.linalg.svd`.

## Where it fails

- **Round or square-ish parts**: λ1 ≈ λ2 and the orientation is undefined (a square has equal variance in all directions!). Use features (corners, notches) instead.
- **Stray pixels** far from the part pull the covariance strongly (squared distances, like least squares 5.6).
- **180° ambiguity**: PCA cannot tell head from tail; use an asymmetric feature (third moments, a hole position).
- **Large SVDs** are slow: a full SVD of a 5 MP image is far too slow for real-time; use truncated/randomised SVD or other methods.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Taking `vecs[0]` (a row) instead of `vecs[:, 0]` (a column)** from `eigh`.
- **Assuming eigenvalues come largest first**: `eigh` returns them ascending; `svd` returns singular values descending.
- **Swapping (row, col) and (x, y)** when building point arrays from `np.nonzero`.
- **Reporting PCA angles without checking elongation**.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Eigenvectors and SVD are related

The right singular vectors of A are the eigenvectors of AᵀA, and the singular values are the square roots of its eigenvalues. PCA on centred data X can therefore be done with the SVD of X directly, which is numerically better than forming XᵀX [1].

### Eckart–Young theorem

Keeping the k largest singular values gives the best possible rank-k approximation of a matrix in the least-squares sense: no other rank-k matrix is closer [2]. That is why the low-rank image in Section 6 is the best one can do with that many numbers in this form.

### Eigenvalues of the structure tensor

The 2 × 2 matrix of averaged gradient products (5.5 Go deeper) has two small eigenvalues on flat areas, one large on edges and two large at corners: the Harris and Shi–Tomasi detectors (Part F) threshold exactly these eigenvalues.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A part's central moments are μ20 = 500, μ02 = 500, μ11 = 300. Orientation?

<Solution>

θ = ½ atan2(2·300, 500 − 500) = ½ atan2(600, 0) = ½ · 90° = **45°**: equal spread in x and y but positively correlated, so the long axis is diagonal (down-right on screen, y down).

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Draw a filled rectangle 120 × 30 pixels rotated by −20° (use `cv2.boxPoints` and `cv2.fillPoly`), and measure its orientation and elongation with `cv2.moments`.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

mask = np.zeros((200, 320), np.uint8)
box = cv2.boxPoints(((160, 100), (120, 30), -20)).astype(np.int32)   # centre, (width, height), angle
cv2.fillPoly(mask, [box], 255)
m = cv2.moments(mask, binaryImage=True)
theta = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * m["mu11"], m["mu20"] - m["mu02"]))
C = np.array([[m["mu20"], m["mu11"]], [m["mu11"], m["mu02"]]]) / m["m00"]
l2, l1 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(C)
print(f"orientation {theta:.1f} deg, elongation {np.sqrt(l1 / l2):.2f} (120 / 30 = 4)")
```

Output:

```text
orientation -19.9 deg, elongation 3.95 (120 / 30 = 4)
```

The moments find the −20° rotation to within 0.1°. For a filled rectangle √(λ1/λ2) equals the side ratio (the variance of a uniform bar is length²/12); the small differences come from rounding the corners to whole pixels.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A robot must pick round washers, each with a small notch. PCA gives random angles. How do you find the orientation?

<Solution>

The washer itself has no long axis, so PCA is meaningless. Find the **notch** (for example the largest deviation of the outer contour from a fitted circle, 5.6) and take the angle of the vector from the centre to the notch (5.2). This also resolves the 180° ambiguity.

</Solution>
