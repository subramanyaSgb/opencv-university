# 5.8 Probability and Bayes

<Definition term="Bayes' rule">how to update a belief with evidence: the probability that a part is defective **given** an alarm depends on how good the detector is **and** on how common defects are in the first place.</Definition>

Every inspection system makes decisions under uncertainty: noise (5.4) overlaps good and bad parts. Probability gives the language to say how often it will be wrong, and Bayes' rule shows a trap that surprises almost everyone the first time.

## 1. An analogy

A smoke alarm in a kitchen. It beeps almost every time there is a fire, but also sometimes when you make toast. Fires are rare and toast is daily, so when it beeps, it is **almost always toast**, even though the alarm is "99 % reliable". Whether an alarm is believable depends on how rare the real event is.

## 2. Conditional probability

**P(A | B)** is "the probability of A, given that B is true". The two directions are different:

<VsTable
  left="P(alarm | defect)"
  right="P(defect | alarm)"
  rows={[
    ["How often the system finds a defect that is there", "How often an alarm is a real defect"],
    ["Detection rate, sensitivity, recall", "Precision, positive predictive value"],

/>

Confusing these two is the most common error in judging detectors.

## 3. Bayes' rule

<Equation terms={[{ value: "P(defect | alarm)" }, { op: "=", value: "P(alarm | defect) · P(defect) / P(alarm)", result: true }]} />

with P(alarm) = P(alarm | defect) P(defect) + P(alarm | good) P(good). **P(defect)** is the **prior**: the defect rate before looking.

## 4. The rare-defect trap

A detector finds 99 % of defects, with 2 % false alarms on good parts. Defects occur in 0.1 % of parts *(Code example 1)*:

<Flow
  steps={[
    { label: "100 000 parts", tone: "world" },
    { label: "100 defective → 99 alarms, 1 missed", tone: "data" },
    { label: "99 900 good → 1 998 false alarms", tone: "data" },
    { label: "2 097 alarms, only 99 real: P(defect | alarm) = 4.7 %", tone: "result" },
  ]}
/>

A "99 % accurate" detector, and **95 % of its alarms are false**. The many good parts produce more false alarms than the few defects produce true ones. Counting with **natural frequencies** (numbers of parts, as above) makes this obvious; percentages hide it.

## 5. The confusion matrix

| | Alarm | No alarm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Defective** | true positive (TP): caught | false negative (FN): missed, **escape** |
| **Good** | false positive (FP): false alarm, **scrap/re-check** | true negative (TN): passed |

- **Recall** (detection rate) = TP / (TP + FN); **false-alarm rate** = FP / (FP + TN).
- **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP) = P(defect | alarm).
- **Accuracy** = (TP + TN) / all: nearly useless for rare defects (a system that never alarms is 99.9 % "accurate" here).

## 7. Choosing a threshold

Good and defective patches have overlapping grey-value distributions (5.4). Wherever you put the threshold, some errors remain. With 1 % defects, good patches N(150, 8) and defect patches N(120, 10) *(Code example 2)*:

| Threshold | Caught | Missed | False alarms | Precision | Recall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Midpoint 135 | 931 | 69 | 3 103 | 0.23 | 0.93 |
| Bayes 124.9 | 694 | 306 | 72 | 0.91 | 0.69 |

The **Bayes threshold** is where P(defect) · p(x | defect) = P(good) · p(x | good): it minimises the **total** number of errors (378 vs 3 172). Because defects are rare, it moves towards the defect side.

But errors do not cost the same: a missed crack in a safety part may cost far more than re-checking a good part. Then weight each error by its cost; the threshold moves back to catch more defects. The best threshold is a business decision expressed in probabilities.

## 8. Combining evidence

Two **independent** checks multiply the evidence. In odds form, each alarm multiplies the odds by the **likelihood ratio** P(alarm | defect) / P(alarm | good) = 0.99 / 0.02 = 49.5 *(Code example 3)*:

| Evidence | P(defect) |
| --- | --- |
| Prior | 0.1 % |
| Camera A alarms | 4.7 % |
| Camera A and B alarm | 71.0 % |

This is why industrial systems often use a fast first check with many false alarms, followed by a slower second check (another camera angle, a different method, or a human) only on alarmed parts. Warning: two cameras looking at the same scratch with the same lighting are **not** independent.

## What you should remember from 5.8

<Takeaways>
<Takeaway n={1} title="Direction">P(alarm | defect) ≠ P(defect | alarm).</Takeaway>
<Takeaway n={2} title="Bayes">P(D | A) = P(A | D) P(D) / P(A); the prior P(D) matters.</Takeaway>
<Takeaway n={3} title="Rare defects">Most alarms are false unless the false-alarm rate is far below the defect rate.</Takeaway>
<Takeaway n={4} title="Metrics">Use recall and precision (and costs), not accuracy.</Takeaway>
<Takeaway n={5} title="Combine">Independent checks multiply odds by their likelihood ratios.</Takeaway>
</Takeaways>

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** 1000 parts, 10 defective. The detector catches 9 of them and raises 50 false alarms. Precision and recall?

<Solution>

Recall = 9 / 10 = **90 %**; precision = 9 / (9 + 50) ≈ **15 %**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** A system never raises an alarm. Defect rate 0.5 %. Its accuracy?

<Solution>

**99.5 %**, while catching nothing (recall 0). Accuracy is misleading for rare events.

</Solution>

**Q3.** Defect rate 1 %, detection 95 %, false alarms 1 %. P(defect | alarm)?

<Solution>

0.95 · 0.01 / (0.95 · 0.01 + 0.01 · 0.99) = 0.0095 / 0.0194 ≈ **49 %**.

</Solution>

## Code

Four examples: Bayes' rule with natural frequencies, the Bayes threshold for two classes, combining two checks, and what OpenCV's automatic (Otsu) threshold does with rare defects. No images needed.

### 1. From scratch: what does an alarm mean?

```python
p_d = 0.001                 # prior: 1 part in 1000 has the defect
sens = 0.99                 # P(alarm | defect): the detector finds 99 % of defects
fa = 0.02                   # P(alarm | good): 2 % false alarms
p_alarm = sens * p_d + fa * (1 - p_d)               # total probability of an alarm
post = sens * p_d / p_alarm                         # Bayes: P(defect | alarm)
print(f"P(alarm) = {p_alarm:.5f}, P(defect | alarm) = {post:.3f}")
n = 100_000
d = n * p_d
print(f"of {n:,} parts: {d:.0f} defective -> {d * sens:.0f} caught, {d * (1 - sens):.0f} missed; "
      f"{n - d:,.0f} good -> {(n - d) * fa:,.0f} false alarms")
print(f"so only {d * sens:.0f} of {d * sens + (n - d) * fa:,.0f} alarms are real defects")
```

Output:

```text
P(alarm) = 0.02097, P(defect | alarm) = 0.047
of 100,000 parts: 100 defective -> 99 caught, 1 missed; 99,900 good -> 1,998 false alarms
so only 99 of 2,097 alarms are real defects
```

- The denominator P(alarm) is the "law of total probability": alarms from defects plus alarms from good parts.
- The second half recomputes the same result as counts (natural frequencies), which is easier to explain to colleagues.

### 2. From scratch: midpoint vs Bayes threshold

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=14)
n_good, n_bad = 99_000, 1_000                        # prior 1 % defects
good = rng.normal(150, 8, n_good)                    # grey value of good surface patches
bad = rng.normal(120, 10, n_bad)                     # defect patches are darker
def gauss(x, m, s):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - m) / s) ** 2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
xs = np.arange(100, 160, 0.01)
bayes_t = xs[np.argmin(np.abs(gauss(xs, 120, 10) * 0.01 - gauss(xs, 150, 8) * 0.99))]   # where the two posteriors are equal
for name, t in [("midpoint 135", 135.0), (f"Bayes {bayes_t:.1f}", bayes_t)]:
    tp, fp = np.sum(bad < t), np.sum(good < t)
    fn = n_bad - tp
    print(f"threshold {name:12s}: caught {tp:4d}, missed {fn:3d}, false alarms {fp:5d}, "
          f"precision {tp / (tp + fp):.2f}, recall {tp / n_bad:.2f}, total errors {fp + fn}")
```

Output:

```text
threshold midpoint 135: caught  931, missed  69, false alarms  3103, precision 0.23, recall 0.93, total errors 3172
threshold Bayes 124.9 : caught  694, missed 306, false alarms    72, precision 0.91, recall 0.69, total errors 378
```

- `gauss` is the Gaussian density from 5.4; prior × density is proportional to the posterior of each class.
- The Bayes threshold is found numerically: the x where the two weighted densities cross.
- To weight costs, multiply each side by its error cost (e.g. a miss costs 20 × a false alarm) and find the new crossing (Exercise 2).

### 3. From scratch: two independent alarms

```python
prior_odds = 0.001 / 0.999
lr = 0.99 / 0.02                                     # likelihood ratio of one alarm: P(alarm|defect) / P(alarm|good)
for k, name in [(1, "camera A alarms"), (2, "camera A and B alarm (independent)")]:
    odds = prior_odds * lr ** k
    print(f"{name:36s}: P(defect) = {odds / (1 + odds):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
camera A alarms                     : P(defect) = 0.047
camera A and B alarm (independent)  : P(defect) = 0.710
```

- **Odds** = P / (1 − P). Bayes' rule in odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.
- Independence is an assumption; correlated checks give much less than this.

### 4. OpenCV: an automatic threshold ignores the prior

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=14)
good = rng.normal(150, 8, 99_000)
bad = rng.normal(120, 10, 1_000)
values = np.clip(np.concatenate([good, bad]), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8).reshape(1, -1)   # all patches as one "image" row
t, _ = cv2.threshold(values, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
tp, fp = np.sum(bad < t), np.sum(good < t)
print(f"Otsu threshold {t:.0f}: caught {tp}, missed {1000 - tp}, false alarms {fp}")
balanced = np.clip(np.concatenate([good[:1000], bad]), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8).reshape(1, -1)   # 50 % defects
t2, _ = cv2.threshold(balanced, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
print(f"Otsu on a balanced sample: threshold {t2:.0f}")
```

Output:

```text
Otsu threshold 148: caught 1000, missed 0, false alarms 39731
Otsu on a balanced sample: threshold 133
```

- `cv2.threshold(src, 0, 255, THRESH_BINARY + THRESH_OTSU)` ignores the given threshold and returns the one chosen by Otsu's method (Part C) as the first value.
- Otsu assumes two classes of comparable size. With 1 % defects it splits the **good** class in half: 40 % of good patches become "defects". With balanced data it lands near the midpoint (133).
- Automatic thresholds are useful when both classes are large (dark parts on a bright belt), not for finding rare defects.

## OpenCV API notes

Probability is mostly NumPy and plain Python, but OpenCV has probabilistic tools used later in the course:

- **`cv2.ml.NormalBayesClassifier`**: a Bayes classifier with Gaussian class models (Part H).
- **`cv2.calcBackProject`**: turns a colour histogram into a per-pixel likelihood map (Part B, colour detection).
- **`cv2.createBackgroundSubtractorMOG2`**: models each pixel as a mixture of Gaussians and classifies foreground vs background (Part G).
- **`cv2.threshold(…, cv2.THRESH_OTSU)`**: picks a threshold that best separates two classes in a histogram (Part C); unlike the Bayes threshold it ignores priors and costs.

## Where it fails

- **Unknown or changing priors**: the defect rate changes with material batches, tool wear and season; precision changes with it.
- **Non-independent evidence**: two checks on the same feature, or a model and its retrained copy, are correlated.
- **Wrong class models**: real distributions are rarely perfect Gaussians; estimate them from data (histograms, Part B).
- **Test sets with 50 % defects** give far too optimistic precision; evaluate at the real defect rate.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Reading "99 % detection" as "99 % of alarms are real"**.
- **Reporting accuracy** for a rare-defect detector.
- **Tuning thresholds on a balanced test set** and deploying on a line with 0.1 % defects.
- **Treating two views of the same defect as independent evidence**.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Bayes decision theory

Choosing the class with the highest posterior minimises the probability of error; weighting by costs minimises the expected cost (the "Bayes risk"). Every classifier, from a single threshold to a neural network, can be judged against this ideal [1].

### ROC and precision–recall curves

Sweeping the threshold traces a **ROC curve** (detection rate vs false-alarm rate) or a **precision–recall curve**. ROC curves do not depend on the defect rate; precision–recall curves do and are more honest for rare defects [2].

### Likelihoods from images

Histograms of good and defective examples (Part B) are empirical p(x | class). Combining them with priors gives pixel-wise posteriors: the basis of colour-based segmentation, background subtraction and naive Bayes classifiers.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

0.5 % of welds are bad. An inspection finds 90 % of bad welds and wrongly flags 5 % of good ones. Of 10 000 welds, how many alarms, how many real, and what is P(bad | alarm)?

<Solution>

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

In Code example 2, a missed defect costs 20 times as much as a false alarm. Find the cost-weighted threshold (where 20 · P(defect) · p(x | defect) = P(good) · p(x | good)) and print caught, missed and false alarms.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=14)
good = rng.normal(150, 8, 99_000)
bad = rng.normal(120, 10, 1_000)
def gauss(x, m, s):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - m) / s) ** 2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
xs = np.arange(100, 160, 0.01)
t = xs[np.argmin(np.abs(20 * gauss(xs, 120, 10) * 0.01 - gauss(xs, 150, 8) * 0.99))]
tp, fp = np.sum(bad < t), np.sum(good < t)
print(f"cost-weighted threshold {t:.1f}: caught {tp}, missed {1000 - tp}, false alarms {fp}, cost {20 * (1000 - tp) + fp}")
```

Output:

```text
cost-weighted threshold 132.0: caught 893, missed 107, false alarms 1212, cost 3352
```

With expensive misses the threshold moves up from 124.9 to 132.0: more false alarms, far fewer escapes. (With the plain Bayes threshold the cost would be 20 · 306 + 72 = 6192.)

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A supplier shows a defect detector with "98 % accuracy" measured on 500 good and 500 defective images. Your line has 0.2 % defects. What do you ask for before buying?

<Solution>

Ask for the detection rate (recall) and the false-alarm rate separately, and compute the precision at **your** defect rate: with, say, 98 % recall and 2 % false alarms, P(defect | alarm) = 0.98·0.002 / (0.98·0.002 + 0.02·0.998) ≈ **9 %**. Ask for a test on your own parts at a realistic defect rate, and for how the threshold can be tuned (costs of misses vs false alarms).

</Solution>
