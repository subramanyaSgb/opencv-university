# 3.1 Sampling and resolution

<Definition term="Sampling">measuring a continuous scene at a regular grid of points, one value per pixel.</Definition>

The real world is **continuous**: brightness changes smoothly from place to place, at every scale. A digital image is **discrete**: a finite grid of numbers. **Sampling** is the step between them, and **resolution** says how fine that grid is.

## 1. From smooth to grid

Imagine measuring the temperature along a road with a thermometer every 10 metres. You get a list of numbers, not the full temperature curve. Anything that happens **between** your measurement points is invisible to you.

A camera does the same with brightness: it measures it at a grid of **pixels**. Module 2 showed how; this chapter is about **what the grid can and cannot capture**.

## 2. A pixel is a small area, not a point

A camera pixel does not measure an infinitely small point. It collects light over its whole area (2.5), so its value is roughly the **average brightness over a small square** of the scene.

<PixelMatrix values={[[30, 30, 200, 200], [30, 115, 200, 200], [30, 30, 200, 200]]} highlight={[[1, 1]]} caption="The outlined pixel straddles a dark-to-bright edge: it records the average, 115." />

That is why edges are rarely a perfect jump from one value to another: pixels on the edge get **in-between** values.

## 3. A 1D example

A pattern of stripes whose brightness follows a wave with a **2 mm** period. Sample it at three different steps:

| Step (mm per pixel) | Samples over 4 mm | Values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.25 | 16 | 128 199 228 199 128 57 28 57 … |
| 0.5 | 8 | 128 228 128 28 128 228 128 28 |
| 1.0 | 4 | 128 128 128 128 |

*(From Code example 1.)* With a fine step we see the wave clearly. At 0.5 mm we still see it, just barely (2 samples per period). At 1 mm, every sample lands on the same point of the wave and the stripes **disappear completely**. Chapter 3.5 explains this exactly.

## 4. Resolution: three meanings

"Resolution" is used for three different things. Keep them apart:

<Glossary
  items={[
    { term: "Pixel count", desc: "How many pixels the image has.", example: "2448 × 2048 (5 MP)" },
    { term: "Spatial resolution", desc: "How much of the object one pixel covers (2.4).", example: "0.2 mm/px" },
    { term: "Optical resolution", desc: "The finest detail the lens can deliver to the sensor (2.2, 2.3).", example: "line pairs per mm" },
  ]}
/>

A 20 MP camera behind a blurry lens, or far from the object, can still have **poor spatial resolution** on the part you care about.

## 5. Line pairs

Optical people measure detail in **line pairs**: one dark line plus one bright line. To show a line pair you need at least **two pixels**: one for the dark line, one for the bright. So a sensor with 3.45 µm pixels can at best show 1 / (2 × 0.00345 mm) ≈ **145 line pairs per mm** on the sensor.

## 6. Coarser sampling loses detail

**Try it.** Sample the test chart with fewer and fewer pixels.

<SamplingLab caption="Each displayed block is one sample: the average of the scene area it covers. Turn on smoothing to see that interpolation hides the blocks but does not restore the bars." />

The thin bars go first: their contrast is 205 at half the pixels in each direction, 103 at a quarter and 52 at an eighth (Code example 2).

## 7. Enlarging does not add detail

Making an image **bigger** in software (upsampling) only **interpolates** between existing pixels. It can look smoother, but it cannot show anything that was not sampled. Code example 3 shrinks the chart 4× and enlarges it back with three methods: all three are far from the original (about 18 dB PSNR).

<Callout kind="mistake">

"We'll capture at low resolution and upscale later" does not work for inspection. The detail must be **captured**: right pixels, right optics, right distance.

</Callout>

## 9. Sampling in time

A video samples **time** as well as space. At 25 frames per second, anything that happens between two frames (40 ms apart) is missed. Fast rotating wheels can even appear to turn backwards: the **wagon-wheel effect**, a time version of the problem in section 3.

## 10. Memory and data

More samples cost more memory and processing:

| Resolution | 8-bit gray | 8-bit colour | 16-bit gray |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 640 × 480 | 0.31 MB | 0.92 MB | 0.61 MB |
| 1920 × 1080 | 2.07 MB | 6.22 MB | 4.15 MB |
| 2448 × 2048 | 5.01 MB | 15.04 MB | 10.03 MB |
| 4096 × 3000 | 12.29 MB | 36.86 MB | 24.58 MB |

*(Code example 4.)* At 30 frames per second, a 2448 × 2048 colour stream is about **450 MB/s**. Choose the resolution the task **needs**, not the highest available.

## 11. Choosing the sampling

The design rule from 2.4 still applies, now with a reason: the smallest feature you care about needs **several samples** across it, at least two and preferably three or more.

## 12. Industrial example

Cracks on a hot slab surface are about **0.5 mm** wide. A camera covering a 1600 mm wide slab with 2448 pixels gives 0.65 mm/px: a crack is **less than one pixel** wide and shows up only as a slightly darker pixel, if at all. Options: more pixels across (line-scan, 2.10), several cameras each covering part of the width, or accepting that only larger cracks will be found.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A pixel covers half of a black area (value 0) and half of a white area (value 240). What value does it record (ideally)?

<Solution>

The average: **120**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Stripes repeat every 1 mm. What is the largest sampling step that can still show them?

<Solution>

Two samples per period: **0.5 mm per pixel** (and in practice somewhat less).

</Solution>

**Q3.** A 4096 × 3000 camera sends 8-bit gray images at 20 fps. Data rate?

<Solution>

12.29 MB × 20 ≈ **246 MB/s**.

</Solution>

**Q4.** Why does a "4K" camera not guarantee that you can see 0.1 mm scratches?

<Solution>

Pixel count is not spatial resolution: the scratch's visibility depends on mm per pixel (field of view ÷ pixels), focus, lens quality and lighting.

</Solution>

## Code

Four experiments: sampling a 1D wave, what coarse sampling does to fine detail, why upsampling cannot restore it, and image sizes in memory.

### 1. From scratch: sampling a stripe pattern

```python
import numpy as np

# a continuous scene: brightness along a 10 mm line, b(x) = 128 + 100 sin(2 pi x / 2 mm)
def scene(x_mm):
    return 128 + 100 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * x_mm / 2.0)

for step in [0.25, 0.5, 1.0]:                      # sampling step (mm per pixel)
    x = np.arange(0, 4, step)
    print(f"step {step:4.2f} mm: {len(x)} samples ->", np.round(scene(x)).astype(int))
```

Output:

```text
step 0.25 mm: 16 samples -> [128 199 228 199 128  57  28  57 128 199 228 199 128  57  28  57]
step 0.50 mm: 8 samples -> [128 228 128  28 128 228 128  28]
step 1.00 mm: 4 samples -> [128 128 128 128]
```

- These are **point** samples (the value exactly at each x), the simplest model. Real pixels average over their width, which matters in 3.5.
- At a 1 mm step every sample falls on a zero crossing of the 2 mm wave: the pattern vanishes.

### 2. OpenCV: shrink the image, measure what survives

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-pinhole-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)   # 320 x 200
for factor in [2, 4, 8]:
    small = cv2.resize(img, None, fx=1 / factor, fy=1 / factor, interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)
    back = cv2.resize(small, (img.shape[1], img.shape[0]), interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)
    thin = back[40:90, 230:245]                   # the 3-px bars
    print(f"1/{factor}: {small.shape[1]} x {small.shape[0]} px, {small.size:6d} pixels, thin-bar contrast {int(thin.max()) - int(thin.min()):3d}")
```

Output:

```text
1/2: 160 x 100 px,  16000 pixels, thin-bar contrast 205
1/4: 80 x 50 px,   4000 pixels, thin-bar contrast 103
1/8: 40 x 25 px,   1000 pixels, thin-bar contrast  52
```

- `cv2.INTER_AREA` averages the source pixels that fall into each new pixel: a good model of a coarser sensor.
- `fx`, `fy` scale factors can replace the size argument (pass `None` as `dsize`).
- The contrast of the 3-pixel bars halves with each halving of the resolution, then they are gone.

### 3. OpenCV: enlarging cannot restore detail

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-pinhole-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
small = cv2.resize(img, (80, 50), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)          # 1/4 in each direction
for name, interp in [("nearest", cv2.INTER_NEAREST), ("bilinear", cv2.INTER_LINEAR), ("bicubic", cv2.INTER_CUBIC)]:
    big = cv2.resize(small, (320, 200), interpolation=interp)
    print(f"{name:8s}: PSNR vs original {cv2.PSNR(img, big):5.2f} dB")
```

Output:

```text
nearest : PSNR vs original 17.66 dB
bilinear: PSNR vs original 17.71 dB
bicubic : PSNR vs original 18.42 dB
```

- Bicubic looks smoothest and scores best, but all three are far from the original. The detail was lost at the shrinking step.
- Interpolation methods matter for geometry (Chapter 21); they cannot replace sampling.

### 4. From scratch: image sizes in memory

```python
import numpy as np

print("resolution    8-bit gray   8-bit colour   16-bit gray")
for w, h in [(640, 480), (1920, 1080), (2448, 2048), (4096, 3000)]:
    sizes = [w * h * ch * bits // 8 / 1e6 for bits, ch in [(8, 1), (8, 3), (16, 1)]]
    print(f"{w:4d} x {h:4d}  " + "  ".join(f"{s:9.2f} MB" for s in sizes))
```

Output:

```text
resolution    8-bit gray   8-bit colour   16-bit gray
 640 x  480       0.31 MB       0.92 MB       0.61 MB
1920 x 1080       2.07 MB       6.22 MB       4.15 MB
2448 x 2048       5.01 MB      15.04 MB      10.03 MB
4096 x 3000      12.29 MB      36.86 MB      24.58 MB
```

- Bytes = width × height × channels × bytes per value. In NumPy, `img.nbytes` gives it directly.
- These are uncompressed sizes; files on disk may be smaller (Module 4).

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.resize(src, dsize[, dst, fx, fy, interpolation])`**: `dsize = (width, height)`; or `dsize = None` with `fx`, `fy`. Interpolation:

- `INTER_AREA`: averages; best for **shrinking**.
- `INTER_NEAREST`: copies the nearest pixel; keeps exact values (labels, masks).
- `INTER_LINEAR` (default), `INTER_CUBIC`, `INTER_LANCZOS4`: smooth enlarging.

**`img.nbytes`, `img.shape`, `img.dtype`** tell you the memory size, dimensions and data type of any image (Chapter 6.1).

**`cv2.pyrDown` / `cv2.pyrUp`** halve or double the size with Gaussian smoothing (image pyramids, Chapter 21).

## Where it fails

- **The "pixel = area average" model** is approximate: pixels have gaps, microlenses and different sensitivity across their area.
- **Downsampling without averaging** (taking every n-th pixel, or `INTER_NEAREST` when shrinking) causes aliasing (3.5).
- **Rules of thumb** ("2–3 pixels per feature") depend on contrast and noise; low-contrast features need more.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Confusing megapixels with spatial resolution.**
- **Upscaling images and expecting new detail.**
- **Shrinking with `INTER_NEAREST`**: jagged results and aliasing; use `INTER_AREA`.
- **Passing (rows, cols) to `cv2.resize`**: it wants (width, height).
- **Choosing the maximum resolution by default**, then struggling with data rates and processing time.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Sampling as multiplication by a comb

Mathematically, point sampling multiplies the continuous image by a grid of impulses. In the frequency domain that copies the image's spectrum around multiples of the sampling frequency; if the copies overlap, aliasing results (3.5, Chapter 20) [1][2].

### Pixel aperture

Real pixels integrate over their area, which is a convolution with a small box before sampling. That box acts as a weak low-pass filter, reducing (but not removing) aliasing. Its size relative to the pixel pitch is the *fill factor* [2].

### Super-resolution

Combining several images shifted by fractions of a pixel, or using learned priors, can recover some detail beyond one frame's sampling. It relies on extra information (more frames or strong assumptions), not on interpolation alone [2].

</GoDeeper>

A 1D row of pixels covers a scene where the left 2.5 pixels are black (0) and the rest white (200). The pixels are 1 unit wide, starting at 0. What are the first four pixel values?

<Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Make a 1D signal of stripes with a period of 4 pixels (`np.tile([0, 0, 200, 200], 50)`). Shrink it by 2 and by 4 with `cv2.resize(..., interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)` and print the first values and the contrast (max − min) each time.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

row = np.tile(np.array([0, 0, 200, 200], np.uint8), 50)[None, :]   # 1 x 200
for f in [2, 4]:
    small = cv2.resize(row, (row.shape[1] // f, 1), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)
    print(f"1/{f}: {small[0, :6]}  contrast {int(small.max()) - int(small.min())}")
```

Output:

```text
1/2: [  0 200   0 200   0 200]  contrast 200
1/4: [100 100 100 100 100 100]  contrast 0
```

At 1/2 there are still 2 samples per period and the stripes survive. At 1/4 each new pixel averages a whole period: the stripes vanish into flat gray.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

A billet ID is painted with strokes 4 mm wide. The camera covers 1200 mm with 1920 pixels. How many pixels wide is a stroke? Is that enough for reliable OCR?

<Solution>

1200 / 1920 = 0.625 mm/px; 4 / 0.625 = **6.4 pixels** per stroke. That is comfortably above the 2–3 pixel minimum and usually enough for OCR, provided focus and contrast are good.

</Solution>
