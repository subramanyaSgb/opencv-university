# 3.5 Aliasing and Nyquist

<Definition term="Aliasing">a pattern too fine for the sampling grid showing up as a different, false, coarser pattern.</Definition>

In 3.1, stripes sampled too coarsely **vanished**. That was a lucky case. More often they turn into **something else**: wide false stripes, curved rings, a wheel that turns backwards. This false pattern is an **alias**: a disguise. The **Nyquist limit** tells you how fine you must sample to avoid it.

## 1. Two patterns, the same samples

A wave with **0.6 cycles per pixel** and a wave with **0.4 cycles per pixel** give **exactly the same pixel values** when sampled once per pixel (Code example 1):

<PixelMatrix values={[[255, 24, 167, 167, 24, 255, 24, 167]]} caption="Eight samples (shown as gray levels) of either wave. Once sampled, nothing tells them apart." />

The camera records the samples, not the wave. The finer pattern is **indistinguishable** from the coarser one: it has taken its **alias**.

## 2. The Nyquist limit

The rule (the sampling theorem, after Nyquist and Shannon):

<Callout kind="key">

To capture a repeating pattern, you need **more than 2 samples per period**. The highest frequency a grid can show is **half its sampling rate**: the **Nyquist limit**.

</Callout>

For pixels: at most **0.5 cycles per pixel**, i.e. a period of at least **2 pixels** (one dark, one bright: the line pair of 3.1).

## 3. Where the alias lands

A pattern above the limit is "folded back" below it:

```text
alias = | f − round(f / fs) × fs |
```

At 1 sample per pixel:

| Pattern (cycles/px) | Seen as (cycles/px) |
| --- | --- |
| 0.3 | 0.3 |
| 0.5 | 0.5 (limit) |
| 0.6 | **0.4** |
| 0.8 | **0.2** |
| 0.9 | **0.1** |
| 1.0 | **0.0** (flat!) |
| 1.2 | **0.2** |

*(Code example 2.)* A pattern **just** above the limit appears **slower**, the closer to 1.0 the slower; at exactly 1.0 cycle per pixel every sample hits the same phase and the pattern disappears (the 3.1 case).

## 5. Moiré: aliasing in 2D

In 2D, fine textures alias into **large curved or striped patterns**: **moiré**. You see it when photographing screens, fabrics, fences or fine meshes.

## 6. Jagged edges

A sharp, slanted edge contains detail at **all** frequencies, including ones above the limit. Sampled without blur, it becomes a **staircase** ("jaggies"). Drawing functions use **anti-aliasing** (`cv2.LINE_AA`) to soften this by giving edge pixels in-between values.

## 7. Aliasing in time

Video samples time (3.1). A wheel with spokes turning faster than half the frame rate (per spoke spacing) appears to turn **slowly, stop or turn backwards**: the **wagon-wheel effect**. A fan or roller that looks still in a video may in fact be spinning fast. Flicker banding in 2.9 is another time-sampling effect.

## 8. Prevention 1: blur before sampling

The cure is to remove detail finer than the Nyquist limit **before** sampling. This is an **anti-aliasing filter** (a low-pass filter):

<Flow direction="right" steps={["Scene with very fine detail", "Low-pass: blur slightly", "Sample", { label: "No false patterns", tone: "result" }]} />

In cameras, the lens blur, the pixel's own area (3.1) and sometimes an **optical low-pass filter** (2.7) do this. The cost: some sharpness.

## 9. Prevention 2: shrink images properly

Software has the same problem. Shrinking by **taking every n-th pixel** (`INTER_NEAREST`) is point sampling: it aliases. **Averaging** (`INTER_AREA`) or **blurring first** reduces it:

| Method (shrink 2×, stripes of period 3 px) | Strength of the false pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Every 2nd pixel (`INTER_NEAREST`) | 63.8 |
| Area average (`INTER_AREA`) | 31.9 |
| Gaussian blur, then every 2nd pixel | 2.7 |

*(Code example 3.)* Image pyramids (`cv2.pyrDown`) blur and then subsample for exactly this reason.

## 10. Prevention 3: sample finer

If the fine pattern **matters** (you need to see the mesh, the thread, the crack), blurring it away is not acceptable. Then you need **finer sampling**: more pixels on the object, a closer camera or a longer lens (2.4), so that the pattern is below the Nyquist limit.

## 11. Why aliasing is dangerous in inspection

Aliases look **real**. A false periodic pattern can be mistaken for a **defect** (or hide a real one), a measurement of a pitch or thread can come out wrong, and a texture classifier can see a different texture. Unlike noise, aliasing does not average away: the same scene gives the same false pattern every time.

## 12. Industrial example

A camera inspects **woven wire mesh** with a wire pitch of 0.5 mm, at 0.3 mm per pixel. The mesh frequency is 0.3 / 0.5 = **0.6 cycles per pixel**: above Nyquist. The image shows a false mesh with a pitch of 1 / 0.4 = 2.5 pixels (0.75 mm) and slow moiré bands. Measuring the pitch from this image gives a wrong answer with full confidence. The fix: at least 0.2 mm/px (pitch = 2.5 px, 0.4 c/px), better 0.1 mm/px.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** What is the Nyquist limit for a camera at 0.25 mm per pixel, in line pairs per mm?

<Solution>

4 pixels per mm → **2 line pairs per mm** (a period of 0.5 mm).

</Solution>

**Q2.** A pattern of 0.7 cycles per pixel is sampled once per pixel. What frequency appears?

<Solution>

|0.7 − 1| = **0.3 cycles per pixel**.

</Solution>

**Q3.** Why does `cv2.resize(..., interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)` cause more aliasing than `INTER_AREA` when shrinking?

<Solution>

Nearest takes single samples (point sampling) and keeps all the too-fine detail, which aliases; area averaging acts as a low-pass filter first.

</Solution>

**Q4.** A video at 25 fps shows a 4-spoke wheel standing still. Give one possible true speed.

<Solution>

Any speed where the wheel turns by a multiple of 90° between frames, e.g. 90° per frame = 25 × ¼ = **6.25 revolutions per second**.

</Solution>

## Code

Three experiments: two waves with identical samples, where aliases land, and shrinking stripes with and without anti-aliasing.

### 1. From scratch: two different waves, identical samples

```python
import numpy as np

n = np.arange(8)                                   # sample positions (pixels)
fine = np.cos(2 * np.pi * 0.6 * n)                 # 0.6 cycles per pixel: too fine
slow = np.cos(2 * np.pi * 0.4 * n)                 # 0.4 cycles per pixel
print("0.6 c/px samples:", np.round(fine, 3))
print("0.4 c/px samples:", np.round(slow, 3))
print("identical:", np.allclose(fine, slow))
```

Output:

```text
0.6 c/px samples: [ 1.    -0.809  0.309  0.309 -0.809  1.    -0.809  0.309]
0.4 c/px samples: [ 1.    -0.809  0.309  0.309 -0.809  1.    -0.809  0.309]
identical: True
```

- cos(2π·0.6·n) = cos(2π·n − 2π·0.4·n) = cos(2π·0.4·n) for whole numbers n. The difference is a whole number of cycles between samples, which the samples cannot see.

### 2. From scratch: where aliases land

```python
import numpy as np

fs = 1.0                                           # 1 sample per pixel
print("pattern (c/px)   seen as (c/px)")
for f in [0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.2]:
    alias = abs(f - round(f / fs) * fs)            # fold back into 0 .. fs/2
    print(f"{f:9.1f}   {alias:12.1f}" + ("   <- aliased" if f > fs / 2 else ""))
```

Output:

```text
pattern (c/px)   seen as (c/px)
      0.1            0.1
      0.3            0.3
      0.5            0.5
      0.6            0.4   <- aliased
      0.8            0.2   <- aliased
      0.9            0.1   <- aliased
      1.0            0.0   <- aliased
      1.2            0.2   <- aliased
```

- Frequencies "fold" around multiples of the sampling rate. Everything ends up between 0 and 0.5.

### 3. OpenCV: shrinking stripes with and without anti-aliasing

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

x = np.arange(600)
stripes = (127.5 + 127.5 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * x / 3.0)).astype(np.float32)[None, :]  # period 3 px
img = np.repeat(stripes, 8, axis=0)

def main_period(row):
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(row - row.mean()))
    k = int(np.argmax(spec))
    return len(row) / k if k else float("inf"), spec[k] / len(row)

for name, small in [("every 2nd pixel (nearest)", cv2.resize(img, (300, 8), interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)),
                    ("area average", cv2.resize(img, (300, 8), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)),
                    ("blur, then every 2nd", cv2.resize(cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (0, 0), 1.2), (300, 8), interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST))]:
    period, amp = main_period(small[4])
    print(f"{name:26s}: strongest pattern period {period:4.1f} px, amplitude {amp:5.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
every 2nd pixel (nearest) : strongest pattern period  3.0 px, amplitude  63.8
area average              : strongest pattern period  3.0 px, amplitude  31.9
blur, then every 2nd      : strongest pattern period  3.0 px, amplitude   2.7
```

- After shrinking 2×, the true stripes would have a period of 1.5 new pixels: impossible (below 2). What appears instead is a **false** period of 3 new pixels.
- Point sampling keeps the false pattern at full strength; area averaging halves it; blurring first (σ = 1.2) almost removes it.
- `np.fft.rfft` finds the strongest repeating component (Chapter 20).

## OpenCV API notes

**Shrinking**: `cv2.resize(..., interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)`; for repeated halving, `cv2.pyrDown(img)` (Gaussian blur + drop every other row and column).

**Blurring first**: `cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (0, 0), sigma)`: for a reduction by a factor k, a sigma around 0.5·k is a common starting point.

**Anti-aliased drawing**: pass `lineType=cv2.LINE_AA` to `cv2.line`, `cv2.circle`, `cv2.putText` and others.

**`cv2.warpAffine` / `cv2.remap`** (Chapter 21) also sample; when they shrink, they alias unless you blur first.

## Where it fails

- **Blurring removes real detail too.** If the fine detail matters, sample finer instead.
- **Bayer sensors** (2.7) sample each colour more coarsely than the pixel grid, so colour aliasing appears earlier than gray aliasing.
- **Non-periodic detail** (a single thin crack) does not create neat aliases but can be lost or misplaced in the same way.
- **The simple folding formula** is for pure point sampling of a single frequency; real images contain many frequencies and pixels average over their area.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Shrinking with `INTER_NEAREST`** (or slicing `img[::4, ::4]`) and analysing textures in the result.
- **Trusting a periodic pattern in an image** without checking that it is below the Nyquist limit.
- **Judging motion from video** of fast rotating parts without considering the frame rate.
- **Assuming "more sharpening" helps**: sharpening amplifies aliasing artefacts.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The sampling theorem

A signal containing no frequencies above B can be reconstructed exactly from samples taken at a rate above 2B (Nyquist; Shannon). Below that rate, spectral copies created by sampling overlap and the information is lost irreversibly [1][2].

### Sampling in the frequency domain

Sampling multiplies the signal by an impulse train; in frequency, the spectrum is repeated every fs. Frequencies above fs/2 from one copy fall into the band of the next: that is the folding formula of section 3 [2].

### Anti-aliasing filters

An ideal anti-aliasing filter would pass everything below fs/2 and nothing above, which needs an infinitely wide kernel. Practical filters (Gaussian, Lanczos, area) trade sharpness against residual aliasing [3].

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A thread has a pitch of 1.5 mm. (a) What is the largest mm per pixel that can represent it? (b) At 1.0 mm/px, what pitch would appear?

<Solution>

(a) At least 2 pixels per pitch: **0.75 mm/px** (and in practice less). (b) f = 1.0 / 1.5 ≈ 0.667 c/px → alias |0.667 − 1| = 0.333 c/px → period 3 px = **3 mm**: the thread appears with twice its real pitch.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Draw a 400 × 400 image of thin diagonal lines with `cv2.line` every 3 pixels. Shrink it to 100 × 100 with `INTER_NEAREST` and with `INTER_AREA`, and print the standard deviation of each result. Which still has strong (false) structure?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = np.zeros((400, 400), np.uint8)
for k in range(-400, 400, 3):
    cv2.line(img, (k, 0), (k + 400, 400), 255, 1)
for name, interp in [("nearest", cv2.INTER_NEAREST), ("area", cv2.INTER_AREA)]:
    small = cv2.resize(img, (100, 100), interpolation=interp)
    print(f"{name:7s}: std {small.std():5.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
nearest: std 120.2
area   : std   7.5
```

The nearest version keeps full-contrast structure, but it is a false pattern; area averaging turns lines that are too fine into almost uniform gray, which is the honest result at that resolution.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

A conveyor roller with 8 bolts around it is filmed at 30 fps. On video the bolts look stationary. What is the slowest possible true rotation speed (other than zero)?

<Solution>

The pattern repeats every 1/8 turn. Looking still means a whole number of 1/8 turns per frame: the slowest is 1/8 turn per frame = 30/8 = **3.75 revolutions per second** (225 rpm).

</Solution>
