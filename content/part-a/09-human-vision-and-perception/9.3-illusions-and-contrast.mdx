# 9.3 Illusions and contrast

<Definition term="Simultaneous contrast">the effect that the perceived brightness (or colour) of an area depends on its surroundings: the same grey looks lighter on a dark background and darker on a light one.</Definition>

9.2 showed that we perceive ratios. This chapter shows something stronger: we perceive **relative to the neighbourhood**. That makes the eye excellent at seeing edges and changes, and unreliable at judging absolute values. For inspection, that is a warning: never trust your eye for "how grey is this?", trust the pixel values.

## 1. An analogy

A quiet room makes a whisper loud; a factory hall makes a shout quiet. The sound did not change, the background did. The visual system works like an automatic volume control applied separately in every small region of the image.

## 2. Same grey, different surround

<ContrastLab caption="Part 1: two squares with the same code on different surrounds; connect them to check. Part 2: a staircase of flat steps and the overshoots your visual system adds at every edge." />

Things to try in part 1: move both surrounds to the same value: the squares look equal. Click **Connect the squares**: a bridge of the same grey links them, and the illusion weakens because the eye can follow one continuous region.

## 3. Lateral inhibition: why it happens

Neighbouring cells in the retina inhibit each other: a cell's output is roughly its own input **minus** a fraction of the average of its surroundings. Such a **centre-surround** receptive field responds strongly to edges and weakly to uniform areas [1], [2].

<Flow

A simple model of the perceived brightness profile is

<Equation terms={[{ value: "perceived" }, { op: "=", value: "I + a · (I − Gσ * I)", result: true }]} />

where Gσ \* I is the image blurred with a Gaussian (the surround) and `a` is the inhibition strength. In a flat area I = Gσ \* I and nothing changes; at an edge the bright side is pushed up and the dark side down. These are the **Mach bands** in part 2 of the lab.

The same formula is **unsharp masking**, the most common image-sharpening method (Part C): image processing borrowed the trick from the eye.

## 4. A gallery of effects, and what they mean for inspection

| Effect | What you see | Inspection lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simultaneous contrast | the same grey looks lighter on dark | do not compare grey values between regions by eye; read pixel values |
| Mach bands | light/dark bands at edges that are not in the image | do not report a "halo defect" at an edge without checking the profile |
| Brightness constancy | a white paper in shadow still looks white | the eye compensates lighting; the camera does not, so uneven light shows up in images but not to the operator |
| Size and context illusions | equal lines look different with different arrow heads or surroundings | measure sizes with tools, not by eye |
| Change blindness | a slow or interrupted change goes unnoticed | do not rely on operators to notice slow drift; trend the measurements |

## 5. Turning illusions into tools

- **Show numbers, not just pictures**: HMI overlays with measured values and tolerance bands.
- **Show differences**: for "is this the same as the golden part?", display |test − reference| (`cv2.absdiff`), not two images side by side.
- **Use a consistent surround**: show image tiles on a neutral mid-grey background, the same for every part.
- **Use false colour carefully** (9.5): colour maps can make subtle differences visible, but they bring their own illusions.

## 6. Industrial example

A paint shop compares the grey level of a body panel with a reference tile held next to it, by eye, under the line lights. Panels with a dark trim around the area look lighter than they are, and some are wrongly accepted. A colour camera with a fixed reference patch in the field of view replaces the visual check: both regions are measured with `cv2.mean` in the same image under the same light, and the HMI shows the difference as a number with green/red tolerance limits. Operators still look at the panel for flaws, which the eye's edge sensitivity is good at, but no longer judge the shade.

## Code

Three examples: Mach bands from the lateral-inhibition model in NumPy, simultaneous contrast measured and modelled with OpenCV, and unsharp masking.

### 1. From scratch: Mach bands

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

steps = np.repeat([60.0, 100.0, 140.0, 180.0], 20)               # a 4-step grey staircase, 20 px per step
x = np.arange(-15, 16)
g = np.exp(-x**2 / (2 * 4.0**2)); g /= g.sum()                    # surround: Gaussian, sigma 4 px
surround = np.convolve(np.pad(steps, 15, mode="edge"), g, mode="valid")
perceived = steps + 0.6 * (steps - surround)                      # centre minus part of the surround (lateral inhibition)
print("physical  around edge 1:", steps[16:24].astype(int).tolist())
print("perceived around edge 1:", np.round(perceived[16:24]).astype(int).tolist())
print("inside step 2 (flat):   ", np.round(perceived[28:32]).astype(int).tolist())
```

Output:

```text
physical  around edge 1: [60, 60, 60, 60, 100, 100, 100, 100]
perceived around edge 1: [55, 54, 52, 49, 111, 108, 106, 105]
inside step 2 (flat):    [100, 100, 100, 100]
```

- At the edge the model pushes the dark side down (60 → 49) and the bright side up (100 → 111); in the middle of a step it changes nothing. That is exactly the pattern of Mach bands.
- `np.pad(..., mode="edge")` repeats the end values so the borders do not create false edges.

### 2. OpenCV: equal greys, unequal responses

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = np.zeros((120, 240), np.uint8)
img[:, :120], img[:, 120:] = 40, 210                              # dark and bright surround
img[40:80, 40:80] = 128                                           # two identical grey centre squares
img[40:80, 160:200] = 128
print("mean of left centre:", cv2.mean(img[40:80, 40:80])[0], "| right centre:", cv2.mean(img[40:80, 160:200])[0])

f = img.astype(np.float32)
dog = cv2.GaussianBlur(f, (0, 0), 3) - cv2.GaussianBlur(f, (0, 0), 30)   # centre-surround response
print(f"centre-surround response: left square {dog[60, 60]:+.1f} (looks lighter) | right square {dog[60, 180]:+.1f} (looks darker)")
```

Output:

```text
mean of left centre: 128.0 | right centre: 128.0
centre-surround response: left square +62.4 (looks lighter) | right square -58.2 (looks darker)
```

- The measurement says "equal"; the centre-surround model (a **difference of Gaussians**, DoG) says the left square stands out positively and the right one negatively, which matches what we see.
- DoG filters are used in machine vision too: blob detection and SIFT (Part E) look for strong DoG responses.

### 3. OpenCV: unsharp masking is the same formula

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

stair = np.repeat(np.array([[60, 100, 140, 180]], np.uint8), 20, axis=1).repeat(10, axis=0)   # 10 x 80 staircase image
blur = cv2.GaussianBlur(stair, (0, 0), 4)
sharp = cv2.addWeighted(stair, 1.6, blur, -0.6, 0)             # unsharp mask: I + 0.6 (I - blur)
print("row 5 around edge 1, original:", stair[5, 16:24].tolist())
print("row 5 around edge 1, sharpened:", sharp[5, 16:24].tolist())
```

Output:

```text
row 5 around edge 1, original: [60, 60, 60, 60, 100, 100, 100, 100]
row 5 around edge 1, sharpened: [55, 53, 52, 49, 111, 108, 107, 105]
```

- Almost the same numbers as the perception model in example 1 (OpenCV uses a slightly different kernel size and border handling). `addWeighted` computes 1.6·I − 0.6·blur with rounding and saturation.
- Sharpening makes images look crisper to people because it adds the overshoot the eye adds anyway. For measurement it adds false edges: sharpen for display, not before measuring.

## OpenCV API notes

**Blur and difference of Gaussians**: `cv2.GaussianBlur(src, (0, 0), sigma)`; DoG = blur(σ₁) − blur(σ₂) in float32.

**Unsharp masking**: `cv2.addWeighted(src, 1 + a, cv2.GaussianBlur(src, (0, 0), s), -a, 0)`; for 8-bit images the result is saturated to 0–255.

**Measuring instead of looking**: `cv2.mean(img[roi])` or `cv2.mean(img, mask)`, `cv2.absdiff(test, ref)` for difference images.

## Where it fails

- **The model is a cartoon**: real perception involves many scales, orientation, attention and learned knowledge (shadows, transparency); simple centre-surround models do not explain every illusion.
- **Individual variation**: illusion strength differs between people and viewing conditions.
- **Display pipelines**: monitors and video streams often add their own sharpening; check the display chain before blaming the eye.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Comparing shades by eye** between areas with different surroundings.
- **Reporting halos** at edges without a profile measurement.
- **Sharpening before measuring** (edge positions and widths change).
- **Showing a reference and a test image side by side** instead of their difference.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Receptive fields

Kuffler (1953) recorded retinal ganglion cells in the cat and found circular receptive fields with an excitatory centre and an inhibitory surround (or the reverse). The difference-of-Gaussians model of such fields was proposed by Rodieck (1965); Marr and Hildreth (1980) used the closely related Laplacian of Gaussian as the basis of edge detection [3].

### Brightness vs lightness

Illusions such as checkerboards in shadow show that the visual system estimates the **lightness** (reflectance) of surfaces, discounting the illumination, rather than reporting **brightness** (luminance). This is useful in daily life and is the reason operators do not notice uneven lighting that is obvious to an algorithm [1].

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

With the model perceived = I + 0.5·(I − surround), a flat area has I = 120 and surround 120; a pixel next to an edge has I = 120 and surround 100. What are the perceived values?

<Solution>

Flat: 120 + 0.5·0 = **120**. Next to the edge: 120 + 0.5·20 = **130**: brighter than the flat part of the same grey.

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

Why does an operator not notice that one side of a conveyor is 30 % darker in the camera image when the lamp ages, while the defect-detection algorithm starts to fail?

<Solution>

The visual system discounts slow, smooth changes in illumination (lightness constancy, adaptation, change blindness), so the scene still "looks normal". The algorithm uses absolute grey values and fixed thresholds, so the darker side crosses them. Remedies: monitor the mean grey of a reference area over time, alarm on drift, use flat-field correction or adaptive thresholds.

</Solution>
