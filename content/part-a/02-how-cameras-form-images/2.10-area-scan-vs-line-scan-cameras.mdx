# 2.10 Area-scan vs line-scan cameras

<Definition term="Line-scan camera">a camera with one row of pixels that builds an image line by line while the object moves past.</Definition>

Every camera so far had a **rectangle** of pixels and took **frames**: an **area-scan** camera. There is a second family, built for objects that move continuously: the **line-scan** camera. It works like a **document scanner** or **photocopier**, except the paper moves instead of the scanner.

## 1. Area-scan: what we know so far

An **area-scan** camera exposes a 2D grid of pixels and delivers **frames**. Each frame is a snapshot of a rectangular field of view (2.4). It is the natural choice for **discrete parts**: a billet end, a label, a person, a bundle of rods.

## 2. Line-scan: one line at a time

A **line-scan** camera has a sensor with **one row** of pixels (sometimes a few rows). It sees a **thin line** across the object. As the object moves under it, the camera takes line after line, and the computer stacks them into an image.

<LineScanSketch />

The motion of the object provides the **second dimension** of the image.

## 3. Why use a line-scan camera?

<Chips items={["Continuous objects: strip, sheet, paper, film, textiles", "Very long objects: rails, slabs, pipes", "Very high resolution across: 4k, 8k, 16k pixels", "No stitching of overlapping frames", "Even lighting along one line"]} />

Area-scan cameras can do these jobs too, but they must take many overlapping frames and **stitch** them, and lighting a whole rectangle evenly is harder than lighting a line.

## 4. Resolution across the motion

Across the line, it works like 2.4:

```text
across (mm/px) = field-of-view width ÷ number of pixels
```

A **1500 mm** wide strip seen by a **4096-pixel** line: 1500 / 4096 = **0.366 mm/px**.

## 5. Resolution along the motion

Along the motion, the pixel size is **how far the object moves between two lines**:

```text
along (mm/line) = speed ÷ line rate
```

At **5 m/s** and **10 000 lines per second** (10 kHz), each line is 5000 / 10 000 = **0.5 mm** apart.

## 6. Square pixels

For an undistorted image, both should match: **along = across**. Solve for the line rate:

<Equation
  terms={[
    { value: "5000 mm/s", label: "speed" },
    { op: "÷", value: "0.366 mm", label: "across" },
    { op: "=", value: "13.65 kHz", label: "line rate", result: true },
  ]}
/>

That is a new line every **73 µs**.

## 7. Wrong line rate: stretched or squashed

If the line rate is too **low** for the speed, the object moves more than a pixel between lines: the image is **squashed** along the motion and detail is lost. If it is too **high**, the image is **stretched**.

**Try it.** The camera sees 320 mm across with 320 pixels (1 mm/px).

<LineScanLab caption="The scanned image of the test chart. Grey area at the bottom: the object has already passed. Turn on encoder triggering to keep the pixels square at any speed." />

## 8. Encoders: follow the real speed

Conveyors speed up and slow down. A fixed line rate then gives a stretched image when slow and a squashed one when fast. The fix: an **encoder** (a rotary sensor on a roller) sends a pulse every fixed distance, and the camera takes **one line per pulse**. The along-track resolution is then fixed in millimetres, whatever the speed.

<Flow direction="right" steps={["Roller turns", "Encoder pulse every 0.366 mm", "Camera takes one line", { label: "Square pixels at any speed", tone: "result" }]} />

## 9. Exposure in line-scan cameras

Each line must be exposed **within one line period**: at 13.65 kHz, at most **73 µs**. That is very little time to collect light (2.5, 2.6), so line-scan systems need **very bright, focused line lights**, often LED bars concentrating light onto the thin line the camera sees.

## 10. TDI: more light from more rows

A **TDI** (time delay integration) sensor has many rows, say 64 or 128. As the object moves, the charge is shifted from row to row **in step with the motion**, so each point of the object is exposed many times and the signals add up. More signal, same speed. It needs an accurate encoder: if the shifting and the motion are out of step, the image blurs.

## 11. Data rates

Line-scan cameras produce a lot of data:

<Equation terms={[{ value: "4096 px", label: "per line" }, { op: "×", value: "13 650", label: "lines/s" }, { op: "=", value: "56 MB/s", label: "8-bit", result: true }, { op: "≈", value: "201 GB", label: "per hour", result: true }]} />

High-end line-scan cameras with 16k pixels and line rates in the hundreds of kHz need fast interfaces (for example CoaXPress or Camera Link) and processing that keeps up in real time.

## 12. Area-scan for long objects

You can inspect a long slab with an area-scan camera by taking **overlapping frames**. With a 500 mm view along the motion and 20 % overlap, each frame advances 400 mm: a 12 m slab needs **30 frames**, and at 1.5 m/s the camera must run at **3.75 fps** (Code example 3). Then software stitches or analyses frame by frame.

## 14. Setup and alignment

A line-scan camera's line must be **exactly** across the motion and **in focus along its whole length**. Small tilts make diagonal stripes; vibration of the conveyor makes wavy images. Area-scan cameras are more forgiving to set up.

## 15. Summary table

| | Area-scan | Line-scan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sensor | 2D, e.g. 2448 × 2048 | 1D (or a few rows), e.g. 4096 × 1 |
| Needs motion | No | Yes, steady and known (encoder) |
| Best for | Discrete parts, scenes, people | Continuous or very long objects, webs |
| Resolution across | Up to ~5–8k px | Up to 16k px and more |
| Lighting | Area lighting | Intense line lighting |
| Setup | Simpler | Alignment and synchronization critical |
| Output | Frames | Endless stream of lines, cut into images |

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A 2048-pixel line-scan camera sees 800 mm across. What is the resolution across?

<Solution>

800 / 2048 ≈ **0.39 mm/px**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** The conveyor moves at 2 m/s. Which line rate gives square pixels?

<Solution>

2000 / 0.39 ≈ **5.1 kHz** (5120 lines/s with the exact 0.390625 mm).

</Solution>

**Q3.** The line rate is set to 2.5 kHz by mistake. What does the image look like?

<Solution>

Each line is 2000 / 2500 = 0.8 mm apart, about 2× the across resolution: the image is **squashed** to half its length along the motion, and detail along the motion is lost.

</Solution>

**Q4.** Why use an encoder instead of a fixed line rate?

<Solution>

Conveyor speed varies. With encoder triggering, the camera takes one line per fixed distance travelled, so the along-track resolution stays constant.

</Solution>

**Q5.** Area-scan or line-scan for reading a painted ID on the end of each billet? For finding scratches on a 1.8 m wide moving strip?

<Solution>

Billet end ID: **area-scan** (a discrete part). Strip scratches: **line-scan** (continuous, wide, high resolution across).

</Solution>

## Code

Three calculations: line rate and data rate for square pixels, a simulated line-scan capture with the wrong line rate (and its correction), and covering a long slab with area-scan frames.

### 1. From scratch: line rate and data rate

```python
import numpy as np

strip_width = 1500.0      # mm across the line
pixels = 4096             # line-scan sensor length
speed = 5.0               # m/s along the line

cross = strip_width / pixels                    # mm per pixel across
line_rate = speed * 1000 / cross                # lines per second for square pixels
data = pixels * line_rate                       # bytes per second at 8 bits per pixel
print(f"across: {cross:.3f} mm/px")
print(f"line rate for square pixels: {line_rate / 1000:.2f} kHz (line period {1e6 / line_rate:.1f} us)")
print(f"data rate: {data / 1e6:.0f} MB/s, {data * 3600 / 1e9:.0f} GB per hour")
```

Output:

```text
across: 0.366 mm/px
line rate for square pixels: 13.65 kHz (line period 73.2 us)
data rate: 56 MB/s, 201 GB per hour
```

- The line period is also the **longest possible exposure**: 73 µs here.
- At 201 GB per hour, systems usually analyse the stream on the fly and store only defects.

### 2. Simulated line-scan capture, wrong line rate, and the fix

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

# a long object passing under a line-scan camera: the "true" object, 1 px = 1 mm both ways
obj = cv2.imread("sample-pinhole-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)   # rows = along the motion
true_rows = obj.shape[0]

def scan(speed_mm_s, line_rate_hz):
    # the camera grabs one row every 1/line_rate s; the object moves speed/line_rate mm per line
    step = speed_mm_s / line_rate_hz                     # mm (= true rows) travelled per line
    positions = np.arange(0, true_rows, step)
    return obj[np.minimum(positions.astype(int), true_rows - 1)]

for speed in [1000, 2000, 500]:                          # mm/s; line rate fixed at 1 kHz
    img = scan(speed, 1000)
    print(f"speed {speed:4d} mm/s: image {img.shape[1]} x {img.shape[0]} px (true 320 x {true_rows})")

stretched = scan(500, 1000)
fixed = cv2.resize(stretched, (obj.shape[1], true_rows), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)
print("after resize:", fixed.shape, " mean abs difference:", round(float(np.abs(fixed.astype(int) - obj).mean()), 2))
```

Output:

```text
speed 1000 mm/s: image 320 x 200 px (true 320 x 200)
speed 2000 mm/s: image 320 x 100 px (true 320 x 200)
speed  500 mm/s: image 320 x 400 px (true 320 x 200)
after resize: (200, 320)  mean abs difference: 0.0
```

- At 1 kHz, only 1 m/s gives square pixels. Twice the speed halves the image length (squashed); half the speed doubles it (stretched).
- A **stretched** image can be resized back without loss (here exactly, because each line was repeated twice). A **squashed** one has lost rows: resizing cannot bring the detail back.
- `cv2.resize(img, (width, height))` takes the size as **(width, height)**, not (rows, columns). `INTER_AREA` is the usual choice for shrinking.

### 3. Covering a long slab with area-scan frames

```python
import numpy as np

slab_len = 12_000      # mm
fov_along = 500        # mm seen along the motion per area-scan frame
overlap = 0.2          # 20 % overlap between frames for stitching
speed = 1.5            # m/s

advance = fov_along * (1 - overlap)
frames = int(np.ceil((slab_len - fov_along) / advance)) + 1
fps = speed * 1000 / advance
print(f"each new frame advances {advance:.0f} mm")
print(f"frames per slab: {frames}, frame rate needed: {fps:.2f} fps")
```

Output:

```text
each new frame advances 400 mm
frames per slab: 30, frame rate needed: 3.75 fps
```

- The first frame covers 500 mm; each further frame adds 400 mm. (12 000 − 500) / 400 rounded up, plus 1, is 30.
- A low frame rate is easy for an area-scan camera; the harder parts are the even lighting over 500 mm and the stitching.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.resize(src, dsize[, fx, fy, interpolation])`**: `dsize = (width, height)`. Interpolation: `INTER_AREA` for shrinking, `INTER_LINEAR` or `INTER_CUBIC` for enlarging, `INTER_NEAREST` to keep exact values.

**Building line-scan images**: line-scan SDKs deliver blocks of lines (for example 1024 lines per buffer). Stack them with `np.vstack` or write them into a preallocated array; process overlapping blocks so defects on block boundaries are not missed.

**Stitching area-scan frames**: for calibrated, known motion, place frames by their encoder position. OpenCV's `cv2.Stitcher` is designed for panoramas from hand-held cameras and is usually not the right tool for conveyor images.

## Where it fails

- **Unsteady motion** (slip, vibration, speed changes without an encoder) distorts line-scan images; sideways wander shows as wavy edges.
- **Stopping objects**: a line-scan camera sees nothing new while the object stands still.
- **Very bright line lighting** heats nearby parts and needs good cooling; in hot plants the camera also needs protection.
- **Discrete parts with gaps** waste line-scan data and need extra logic to find each part in the stream.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Running a line-scan camera at a fixed line rate on a variable-speed line.**
- **Forgetting that exposure ≤ line period.** High line rates need much more light.
- **Ignoring data rates and storage** when choosing pixels and line rate.
- **Using area-scan with too little overlap**, so defects on frame borders are missed or cut in half.
- **Mixing up (width, height) in `cv2.resize`.**

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### TDI sensors

Time delay integration shifts accumulated charge along N rows synchronously with the image motion, giving about N times the signal and √N better shot-noise SNR than a single row at the same line rate. The shift must match the motion; a mismatch smears the image [1].

### Contact image sensors

Contact image sensors (CIS) combine a full-width line of sensors and a lens array with no conventional lens, placed millimetres from the object; they are used for flat, close objects such as banknotes and print inspection [2].

### Line-scan geometry

Line-scan cameras have a perspective geometry across the line and an orthographic, motion-defined geometry along it. Calibrating them needs a model of the motion as well as the lens [1][3].

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A 8192-pixel line-scan camera inspects a 2000 mm wide plate moving at 1.2 m/s. (a) Across resolution? (b) Line rate for square pixels? (c) Longest exposure? (d) Data rate at 8 bits?

<Solution>

(a) 2000 / 8192 ≈ **0.244 mm/px**. (b) 1200 / 0.244 ≈ **4.9 kHz** (4915 lines/s). (c) 1 / 4915 s ≈ **203 µs**. (d) 8192 × 4915 ≈ **40 MB/s**.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

An encoder gives 2000 pulses per revolution on a roller of 150 mm diameter. Compute the distance per pulse, and how many pulses to skip between lines (divider) to get as close as possible to 0.366 mm per line.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

per_pulse = np.pi * 150.0 / 2000          # roller circumference / pulses
target = 0.366
divider = max(1, round(target / per_pulse))
print(f"{per_pulse:.4f} mm per pulse; divider {divider} -> {divider * per_pulse:.4f} mm per line")
```

Output:

```text
0.2356 mm per pulse; divider 2 -> 0.4712 mm per line
```

A divider of 2 gives 0.471 mm per line: pixels 1.29× longer along the motion than across. For a closer match use an encoder with a different resolution, a camera with a fractional encoder divider/multiplier, or resize the image afterwards.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

A 20 m rail moves at 0.8 m/s past an area-scan camera that sees 600 mm along the rail. With 15 % overlap, how many frames per rail and what frame rate?

<Solution>

Advance per frame 600 × 0.85 = 510 mm. Frames: (20 000 − 600) / 510 = 38.04 → 39, plus 1 = **40 frames**. Frame rate 800 / 510 ≈ **1.57 fps**.

</Solution>
