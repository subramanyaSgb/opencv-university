# 2.6 Exposure and motion blur

<Definition term="Exposure time">how long the sensor collects light for one image.</Definition>

In 2.5 the pixels were **buckets in the rain**. Exposure time is **how long the buckets stay open**. Longer means more light and less noise. But if the object moves while the buckets are open, its image **smears**: that is **motion blur**.

## 1. What exposure does

When the camera takes a picture, every pixel starts empty, collects light for the **exposure time**, and is then read out. Nothing that happens **outside** that window is recorded; everything **inside** it is added together.

## 2. Units

Exposure times are short. You will see:

<Chips items={["10 µs (microseconds)", "100 µs", "1 ms = 1000 µs", "10 ms", "33 ms ≈ one frame at 30 fps"]} />

Industrial cameras typically allow exposures from a few microseconds up to seconds.

## 3. Exposure and brightness

Collected light is **proportional** to exposure time. Double the time → double the electrons → double the pixel value, until the pixel is full (2.5).

| Exposure | Electrons (mid-gray) | DN (8-bit) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 200 µs | 800 | 20 |
| 1000 µs | 4000 | 102 |
| 2500 µs | 10 000 (full) | 255, saturated |

*(Numbers from Code example 3.)*

## 4. Four ways to get more light

Exposure time is one of four controls. Each has a price:

<VsTable
  left="Control"
  right="Price"
  rows={[
    ["Longer exposure", "More motion blur"],
    ["Larger aperture (smaller f-number)", "Less depth of field (2.3)"],
    ["More gain", "No SNR gain, noise amplified (2.5)"],
    ["More / better lighting", "Cost, heat, power; usually the best fix"],
  ]}
/>

Photographers call the first three the **exposure triangle**. In machine vision the fourth, **lighting**, is usually the strongest lever.

## 5. Stops: a common unit

A **stop** is a factor of 2 in light. One stop more = double the exposure time, or one f-stop wider (f/5.6 → f/4), or double the light. These are interchangeable: halving the exposure and opening one stop gives the **same brightness**.

## 6. Too dark, too bright

- **Under-exposed**: few photons → noisy, dark, low contrast; details in shadows vanish.
- **Over-exposed**: bright parts **saturate** at 255 → detail lost, edges move.

A quick check is the image **histogram** (Chapter 12): the important part of the scene should sit well inside 0–255, not piled up at either end.

## 7. Motion blur

If the object moves while the shutter is open, each point of it is recorded at **many positions**, and the image is **smeared** along the direction of motion.

## 8. How much blur?

Blur in pixels is simply **how far the object moves** during the exposure, divided by **mm per pixel** (2.4):

```text
blur (px) = speed × exposure time ÷ mm per pixel
```

**Example.** A billet moves at **2 m/s** (2000 mm/s). Exposure **1 ms**. Scale **0.5 mm/px**.

## 9. How much blur is acceptable?

It depends on the task:

<Columns>
<Panel title="Measurement" tone="a">Keep blur **below about 1 pixel** (often 0.5) along the measured direction, or edges shift and widen.</Panel>
<Panel title="Detection / counting" tone="c">A few pixels may be fine, as long as objects stay separated and features stay visible.</Panel>
</Columns>

Turn the formula around to get the **longest allowed exposure**:

```text
max exposure = allowed blur (px) × mm per pixel ÷ speed
```

At 0.5 mm/px and 0.5 px allowed blur: **500 µs** at 0.5 m/s, **125 µs** at 2 m/s, **25 µs** at 10 m/s.

## 10. The conflict

Fast objects need **short exposures**. Short exposures collect **little light**, so the image is **dark and noisy** (2.5). This is the central trade-off of moving inspection.

**Try it.** Change the speed, the scale and the exposure. Watch the blur and the noise.

## 11. Strobe lighting

The cleanest way out is **strobe** (flash) lighting: a very **bright** LED pulse lasting only a few microseconds, synchronized with the camera. The exposure can stay longer; the **flash duration** sets the motion blur, because almost all the light arrives during the flash.

<StrobeTimeline />

LED strobes can be driven far above their continuous power for short pulses ("overdrive"), so they give a lot of light in a short time. Ambient light still enters during the whole exposure, so keep the exposure short or the ambient light low.

## 12. Global shutter

All of this assumes every pixel starts and stops at the **same moment**: a **global shutter**. Many cheaper cameras use a **rolling shutter**, which exposes rows one after another; moving objects then **skew** or **wobble**. Chapter 2.9 explains it. For moving parts, choose **global shutter** cameras.

## 13. Triggering

To image a moving part **at the right place**, the camera is **triggered**: a photoelectric sensor, a PLC output or an encoder on the conveyor sends a pulse, and the camera exposes exactly then.

<Flow direction="right" steps={["Part reaches the sensor", "Trigger pulse", "Strobe + exposure", "Image of the part in the same place every time"]} />

With an **encoder**, the camera can also fire every N millimetres of travel, independent of speed.

## 14. Auto exposure

Auto exposure adjusts the exposure to keep the image brightness constant. It is handy when the light changes, but it makes images **inconsistent**: the same part can look different, and thresholds tuned on one image fail on another.

<Callout kind="key">

In most inspection systems: **fixed exposure, fixed gain, controlled lighting**. Use auto exposure only when the scene brightness truly changes, and then limit its range and region.

</Callout>

## 15. Hot steel lights itself

A billet at 1000 °C **glows**: it is its own light source, and very bright. Cameras looking at hot steel often need **very short exposures** (or a small aperture or a filter) just to avoid saturation, and the brightness changes as the steel cools. That is one case where a controlled auto-exposure on the object region makes sense.

## 16. High dynamic range scenes

A glowing slab next to a dark floor can exceed the sensor's dynamic range (2.5). Options: expose for the **important part** and accept clipping elsewhere, use a **HDR** mode that combines short and long exposures, or use a **filter** to dim the brightest light.

## 17. Exposure and frame rate

A camera cannot expose longer than one frame period: at **100 fps** each frame lasts 10 ms, so the exposure is at most about 10 ms (minus readout overheads on some sensors). If you need both high frame rate and plenty of light, again: **more light**.

## 18. Blur has a direction

Motion blur smears only **along the motion**. Edges perpendicular to the motion blur; edges parallel to it stay sharp (Code example 2 measures this). So:

- Measuring a billet's **width** (across the motion) can tolerate more exposure than measuring its **length** (along the motion).
- Orient the camera so the **critical measurement** is across the motion when possible.

## 19. Industrial example: bars on a fast line

Bars leave a rolling mill at around **10 m/s**. At 0.5 mm/px and ≤ 1 px of blur, the exposure must be at most **50 µs**. At that exposure a normal continuous light gives a dark, noisy image; the usual answer is **high-power strobe** lighting or, for glowing hot bars, using the bars' **own glow** with a very short exposure.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A part moves at 1 m/s, scale 0.2 mm/px, exposure 500 µs. How many pixels of blur?

<Solution>

1000 mm/s × 0.0005 s = 0.5 mm; 0.5 / 0.2 = **2.5 px**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** For the same part, what is the longest exposure for at most 1 px of blur?

<Solution>

1 × 0.2 mm ÷ 1000 mm/s = 0.0002 s = **200 µs**.

</Solution>

**Q3.** You halve the exposure to reduce blur. Name two ways to keep the image brightness without adding much noise.

<Solution>

Double the **light** (stronger lighting or strobe), or open the **aperture** one stop (if depth of field allows).

</Solution>

**Q4.** Why does motion blur hurt length measurement more than width measurement for a billet moving lengthwise?

<Solution>

Blur smears only along the motion, so the **front and back edges** (length) are smeared; the side edges (width) stay sharp.

</Solution>

**Q5.** Why is auto exposure usually switched off in inspection systems?

<Solution>

It changes brightness from image to image, so thresholds and measurements become inconsistent. Fixed settings with controlled light are repeatable.

</Solution>

### 1. From scratch: blur and the longest allowed exposure

```python
import numpy as np

mm_per_px = 0.5
print("speed      exposure   travel    blur")
for speed in [0.5, 2.0, 10.0]:                     # m/s
    for t_ms in [10.0, 1.0, 0.1]:                  # exposure time, ms
        travel = speed * 1000 * t_ms / 1000        # mm moved while the shutter is open
        print(f"{speed:4.1f} m/s  {t_ms:6.1f} ms  {travel:6.2f} mm  {travel / mm_per_px:6.1f} px")
max_blur_px = 0.5
for speed in [0.5, 2.0, 10.0]:
    t_max = max_blur_px * mm_per_px / (speed * 1000)   # seconds
    print(f"{speed:4.1f} m/s: exposure <= {t_max * 1e6:6.1f} us for {max_blur_px} px blur")
```

Output:

```text
speed      exposure   travel    blur
 0.5 m/s    10.0 ms    5.00 mm    10.0 px
 0.5 m/s     1.0 ms    0.50 mm     1.0 px
 0.5 m/s     0.1 ms    0.05 mm     0.1 px
 2.0 m/s    10.0 ms   20.00 mm    40.0 px
 2.0 m/s     1.0 ms    2.00 mm     4.0 px
 2.0 m/s     0.1 ms    0.20 mm     0.4 px
10.0 m/s    10.0 ms  100.00 mm   200.0 px
10.0 m/s     1.0 ms   10.00 mm    20.0 px
10.0 m/s     0.1 ms    1.00 mm     2.0 px
 0.5 m/s: exposure <=  500.0 us for 0.5 px blur
 2.0 m/s: exposure <=  125.0 us for 0.5 px blur
10.0 m/s: exposure <=   25.0 us for 0.5 px blur
```

- Convert units carefully: m/s × 1000 = mm/s; ms ÷ 1000 = s.
- A typical "10 ms" webcam exposure is useless for a 10 m/s bar: 200 pixels of smear.

### 2. OpenCV: simulate motion blur and see its direction

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

chart = cv2.imread("sample-pinhole-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)

def motion_blur(img, length):
    # the object moves `length` pixels to the right during the exposure
    k = np.zeros((1, length), np.float32)
    k[0, :] = 1.0 / length
    return cv2.filter2D(img, -1, k)

for length in [1, 5, 15]:
    img = motion_blur(chart, length) if length > 1 else chart
    gx = cv2.Sobel(img, cv2.CV_64F, 1, 0)       # changes along x (direction of motion)
    gy = cv2.Sobel(img, cv2.CV_64F, 0, 1)       # changes along y
    print(f"blur {length:2d} px: edge energy x {np.mean(np.abs(gx)):6.1f}, y {np.mean(np.abs(gy)):6.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
blur  1 px: edge energy x   75.7, y   21.3
blur  5 px: edge energy x   64.9, y   20.9
blur 15 px: edge energy x   30.7, y   18.1
```

- Horizontal motion blur is an average along a **row**: a 1 × L kernel of 1/L values.
- `cv2.Sobel(img, cv2.CV_64F, 1, 0)` measures change along x (vertical edges); `(0, 1)` along y.
- The x edge energy falls by more than half at 15 px; the y edge energy barely changes. (It drops a little because the letters' slanted edges also have an x component.)

### 3. From scratch: exposure, brightness, saturation and SNR

```python
import numpy as np

rate = 4_000_000          # electrons per second reaching one pixel (mid-gray part of the scene)
full_well, read = 10_000, 6.0
for t_us in [50, 200, 1000, 2500, 5000]:
    e_raw = rate * t_us * 1e-6
    e = min(e_raw, full_well)
    dn = round(e / full_well * 255)
    if e_raw >= full_well:
        print(f"{t_us:5d} us: {e:7.0f} e-, {dn:3d} DN, saturated: information lost")
    else:
        print(f"{t_us:5d} us: {e:7.0f} e-, {dn:3d} DN, SNR {e / np.sqrt(e + read ** 2):5.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
   50 us:     200 e-,   5 DN, SNR  13.0
  200 us:     800 e-,  20 DN, SNR  27.7
 1000 us:    4000 e-, 102 DN, SNR  63.0
 2500 us:   10000 e-, 255 DN, saturated: information lost
 5000 us:   10000 e-, 255 DN, saturated: information lost
```

- Brightness and electrons grow linearly with exposure until the full well.
- SNR grows only with √exposure: 5× the exposure (200 → 1000 µs) gives about 2.3× the SNR.
- Past saturation, more exposure gives nothing but lost detail.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.filter2D(src, ddepth, kernel)`** with a 1 × L kernel simulates horizontal motion blur; an L × 1 kernel simulates vertical motion. For an angled motion, draw a line into a square kernel with `cv2.line` and normalize it.

**`cv2.Sobel(src, ddepth, dx, dy[, ksize=3])`** returns derivative images; use `cv2.CV_64F` (or `CV_16S`) to keep negative values. Chapter 17 covers it in depth.

**Exposure is a camera setting.** Exposure time, gain, trigger mode and strobe output are set through the camera's SDK (GenICam names such as `ExposureTime`, `Gain`, `TriggerMode`, `LineSource`). `cv2.VideoCapture` can set `cv2.CAP_PROP_EXPOSURE` on some webcams, but its units and behaviour depend on the backend and driver.

## Where it fails

- **Simulated blur** assumes constant speed and a uniform shutter. Real objects accelerate, vibrate and rotate.
- **Strobes** fight ambient light: if the plant lighting is bright, the long exposure still collects it and blurs.
- **Glowing hot objects** change brightness as they cool and with emissivity; one fixed exposure may not fit all products.
- **Rolling-shutter cameras** add skew that is not simple blur (2.9).
- **Deblurring** by software (deconvolution) exists but is fragile with noise; prevention is far more reliable.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Leaving the webcam default exposure (often 10–30 ms) for moving parts.**
- **Fixing a dark short-exposure image with gain.** Bright, noisy and unstable; add light.
- **Unit slips:** m/s vs mm/s, ms vs µs. Always convert to mm and seconds before dividing.
- **Auto exposure in an inspection system.** Changes brightness between parts.
- **Free-running camera on a conveyor.** Parts appear at random positions; trigger it.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Exposure value

Photographers combine aperture and time into one number, EV = log₂(N² / t): one EV is one stop. Equal EV means equal light on the sensor for the same scene, for example f/4 at 1/250 s and f/5.6 at 1/125 s [1].

### Motion blur as convolution

For constant-speed motion, the blurred image is the sharp image convolved with a line kernel whose length is the travel in pixels (Code example 2). Knowing the kernel, deconvolution (Wiener filtering) can partly reverse it; noise and the zeros of the line kernel's spectrum limit what can be recovered [2].

### LED strobing and overdrive

LEDs can be pulsed at currents well above their continuous rating if the duty cycle is low, giving several times more light during the pulse. Strobe controllers specify maximum pulse width and duty cycle; exceeding them shortens LED life [3].

### Blur and edge accuracy

Symmetric blur widens an edge but does not move its 50 % point; asymmetric effects (non-constant speed, rolling shutter, saturation) do. That is why measurement systems limit blur even when sub-pixel edge methods are used [3].

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Slabs move at 0.3 m/s under a camera with 0.8 mm/px. (a) Longest exposure for ≤ 0.5 px blur? (b) The current exposure is 5 ms. How much blur? (c) What would you change?

<Solution>

(a) 0.5 × 0.8 mm ÷ 300 mm/s ≈ **1.33 ms**.

(b) 300 mm/s × 0.005 s = 1.5 mm → **1.9 px**.

(c) Cut the exposure to about 1.3 ms and make up the 4× light loss with lighting (or open the aperture two stops if DOF allows).

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Write a function `max_exposure_us(speed_m_s, mm_per_px, max_blur_px)` and print a table for speeds 0.2, 1, 5 and 15 m/s at 0.25 mm/px and 1 px of blur.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

def max_exposure_us(speed_m_s, mm_per_px, max_blur_px):
    return max_blur_px * mm_per_px / (speed_m_s * 1000) * 1e6

for v in [0.2, 1, 5, 15]:
    print(f"{v:4} m/s -> {max_exposure_us(v, 0.25, 1):7.1f} us")
```

Output:

```text
 0.2 m/s ->  1250.0 us
   1 m/s ->   250.0 us
   5 m/s ->    50.0 us
  15 m/s ->    16.7 us
```

At 15 m/s you need a 17 µs exposure: strobe territory.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

A camera runs at 50 fps with 8 ms exposure on a slow conveyor. The customer wants 200 fps for a faster line. What is the new maximum exposure, and what happens to the image if you change nothing else?

<Solution>

At 200 fps a frame lasts **5 ms**, so the exposure must be shorter than that (in practice a little less). Going from 8 ms to under 5 ms loses more than a third of the light: the image is darker and noisier unless the lighting is increased.

</Solution>
