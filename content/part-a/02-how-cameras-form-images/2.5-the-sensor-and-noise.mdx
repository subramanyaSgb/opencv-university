# 2.5 The sensor and noise

<Definition term="Image sensor">a grid of tiny light detectors that count light during the exposure and turn the count into numbers.</Definition>

In 2.1 we said a camera **measures light**. Now we open the sensor and see **how** it measures, and why every measurement comes with **noise**: small random errors that change from frame to frame.

## 1. Light comes in packets

Light arrives as tiny packets of energy called **photons**. A bright scene sends many photons to a pixel during the exposure; a dark scene sends few. The sensor's job is to **count them**.

## 2. Pixels are buckets in the rain

Picture a field of **buckets in the rain**. Each bucket collects the drops that fall on it. Afterwards, you measure how much water is in each bucket.

<BucketSketch caption="Each pixel (photosite) collects light like a bucket collects rain. A full bucket overflows: that pixel is saturated." />

Each pixel of the sensor is a bucket. The "water" is **electric charge**: electrons freed by the photons.

## 3. Quantum efficiency

Not every photon is caught. **Quantum efficiency (QE)** is the fraction of photons that become electrons.

<Equation terms={[{ value: "1000 photons", label: "arrive" }, { op: "×", value: "0.6", label: "QE (60 %)" }, { op: "=", value: "600 electrons", label: "collected", result: true }]} />

QE depends on the **wavelength** (colour) of the light. Datasheets show it as a curve; it usually falls off in deep blue and in the near infrared.

## 4. Full well: the bucket's size

A pixel can hold only so many electrons: its **full well capacity**, for example 10 000 electrons. More light than that and the bucket overflows: the pixel **saturates** and reads the maximum value (255 in an 8-bit image), no matter how much brighter the scene really is.

<Callout kind="mistake">

Saturated pixels have **lost information**. A white-hot billet edge, a reflection or a lamp that reads 255 cannot be measured. Reduce exposure, aperture or gain until the important areas stay below the maximum.

</Callout>

## 5. From electrons to numbers

After the exposure, each pixel's charge is turned into a **voltage**, amplified by the **gain**, and converted by the **ADC** (analog-to-digital converter) into a **digital number (DN)**: 0–255 for 8 bits, 0–4095 for 12 bits (Chapter 3.2).

<Flow direction="right" steps={["electrons", "× conversion (µV per e⁻)", "× gain", "ADC", { label: "DN", tone: "result" }]} />

Two families of sensors do this:

For this course the important point is the same for both: **light → electrons → numbers**, with noise added along the way.

## 7. What is noise?

Point a camera at a **perfectly uniform gray card** and look at the numbers. They are not all equal, and they change every frame:

<PixelMatrix values={[[127, 131, 124, 129], [133, 126, 128, 122], [125, 130, 134, 127]]} caption="A 'flat' gray patch from a real-looking sensor: same light everywhere, yet the values scatter around 128." />

That scatter is **noise**. It is not a fault; it is part of every measurement. The question is **how big** it is compared with the signal.

## 8. Shot noise: light itself is random

Even perfect light does not arrive at an exact rate. Photons arrive **randomly**, like raindrops. If a pixel collects on average N electrons, the actual number varies by about **√N** (a Poisson process).

| Average electrons | Shot noise √N | Relative noise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | ±10 | 10 % |
| 10 000 | ±100 | 1 % |

More light means more noise in electrons, but **less noise relative to the signal**. Shot noise is the reason **bright images look clean and dark images look grainy**.

## 9. Read noise: the electronics

Reading out a pixel adds a little random error from the amplifier and ADC: **read noise**, a few electrons on modern sensors. It is the same whether the pixel is bright or dark, so it matters most **in the dark**.

## 10. Dark current: heat makes electrons too

Even with no light, heat slowly frees electrons in the silicon: **dark current**. It grows with **exposure time** and rises steeply with **temperature**. Its randomness is **dark noise**.

<Callout kind="key">

In steel plants the camera may sit near very hot material. A hot sensor is a **noisy sensor**. Cooling housings, air purging and short exposures are part of the vision design, not just protection.

</Callout>

## 11. Fixed-pattern noise

Some differences do **not** change from frame to frame:

- **DSNU** (dark signal non-uniformity): some pixels are a little brighter in the dark; a few **hot pixels** are much brighter.
- **PRNU** (photo-response non-uniformity): pixels differ slightly in sensitivity, so a flat bright scene shows a faint pattern.

Because they are fixed, they can be **calibrated out**: subtract a dark frame and divide by a flat-field frame (Code example 4 shows the idea).

## 12. Quantization noise

The ADC rounds to whole numbers. That rounding adds a small error of about **1/√12 ≈ 0.29 DN**. It only matters when the real noise is smaller than one step, for example a very dark image at low gain.

## 13. Signal-to-noise ratio

The key quality number is the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)**:

```text
SNR = signal / noise = e / √( e + read² + dark )
```

| Signal | Noise | SNR | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7200 e⁻ | ≈ 85 e⁻ | ≈ 85 | clean |
| 450 e⁻ | ≈ 22 e⁻ | ≈ 20 | grainy |
| 20 e⁻ | ≈ 7 e⁻ | ≈ 3 | barely visible |

## 14. Dynamic range

**Dynamic range** = the brightest level a pixel can hold ÷ the darkest level it can tell from noise ≈ full well ÷ read noise. With 10 000 e⁻ and 6 e⁻ read noise:

<Equation terms={[{ value: "10 000", label: "full well (e⁻)" }, { op: "÷", value: "6", label: "read noise (e⁻)" }, { op: "≈", value: "1667", label: "ratio" }, { op: "=", value: "64.4 dB", label: "20·log₁₀", result: true }]} />

A scene with very bright and very dark parts (glowing steel next to a dark floor) can exceed the sensor's dynamic range: then something is either saturated or lost in noise.

## 15. Gain: brighter numbers, not better data

**Gain** multiplies the signal **and the noise** by the same factor. A dark image with high gain looks bright, but the SNR stays the same as the dark image (or a little worse).

<Callout kind="key">

**More light improves SNR. Gain does not.** Gain only helps when the signal is so small that ADC rounding would hide it.

</Callout>

## 17. Averaging frames

Noise is random, so it **averages out**. Averaging N frames of a still scene reduces random noise by **√N**: 4 frames → half the noise, 16 frames → a quarter.

<Equation terms={[{ value: "11.5", label: "1 frame (std)" }, { op: "→", value: "5.8", label: "4 frames" }, { op: "→", value: "3.0", label: "16 frames", result: true }]} />

This only works if **nothing moves**. For moving steel on a roller table, use more light instead.

## 18. Bigger pixels collect more light

With the same lens and light, a **larger pixel** covers more area and collects more photons, so it has a better SNR. A 5.86 µm pixel has about **2.9×** the area of a 3.45 µm pixel. More megapixels on the same sensor size means **smaller pixels** and, in low light, **more noise per pixel**.

## 19. Why noise matters to algorithms

Noise is not just ugly. It breaks algorithms:

<Chips items={["Thresholds flicker at the boundary", "False edges and false defects", "Edge positions jitter → measurement scatter", "OCR misreads", "Unstable counts"]} />

Smoothing (Part C) can reduce noise, but it also blurs detail. The best fix is upstream: **light, exposure, cooling, pixel size**.

## 20. Measuring a camera's noise

You can measure noise yourself: point the camera at a flat, evenly lit target, grab frames, and compute the **standard deviation** of a patch (spatial) or of one pixel over many frames (temporal). The **EMVA 1288** standard defines exactly how to measure and report QE, read noise, dark current, full well, SNR and dynamic range, so cameras can be compared fairly.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A pixel collects 400 electrons on average. What is its shot noise and SNR (ignore other noise)?

<Solution>

√400 = **20 e⁻**; SNR = 400 / 20 = **20**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** You need twice the SNR in a shot-noise-limited image. How much more light?

<Solution>

SNR = √N, so twice the SNR needs **4×** the light.

</Solution>

**Q3.** A colleague says: *"The image is too dark, just turn the gain up to ×16."* What happens?

<Solution>

The image gets 16× brighter, but the noise is amplified by the same factor: the SNR does not improve and the image looks grainy. Better: more light, a larger aperture (if DOF allows) or a longer exposure (if motion allows).

</Solution>

**Q4.** Why do cameras near hot steel need cooling for good images?

<Solution>

Dark current, and so dark noise and hot pixels, rise steeply with sensor temperature. A cooler sensor gives cleaner images.

</Solution>

**Q5.** Which noise can be removed by calibration: shot noise or fixed-pattern noise?

<Solution>

**Fixed-pattern noise**, because it is the same every frame (dark-frame subtraction, flat-field correction). Shot noise is random and can only be reduced by collecting more light or averaging.

</Solution>

## Code

Four experiments: shot noise as a Poisson process, a complete small sensor model, averaging frames with OpenCV, and fixed-pattern correction.

### 1. From scratch: shot noise is √N

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=1)
for mean_e in [100, 10_000]:
    samples = rng.poisson(mean_e, size=100_000)   # electrons collected by one pixel, many exposures
    print(f"mean {samples.mean():8.1f} e-, std {samples.std():6.1f} e- (sqrt = {np.sqrt(mean_e):5.1f}), SNR {samples.mean() / samples.std():5.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
mean    100.0 e-, std   10.0 e- (sqrt =  10.0), SNR  10.0
mean  10000.0 e-, std  100.1 e- (sqrt = 100.0), SNR  99.9
```

- `rng.poisson(mean, size)` draws photon-like counts. The spread is √mean, exactly as section 8 says.
- 100× more light → 10× better SNR.

### 2. From scratch: a small sensor model, and why gain does not help

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=2)
qe, read_noise, full_well = 0.6, 6.0, 10_000      # electrons; typical-looking numbers for illustration
e_per_dn = full_well / 255                          # gain 1: full well -> 255

def capture(photons, gain=1.0, n=50_000):
    e = rng.poisson(photons * qe, size=n).astype(np.float64)      # shot noise
    e += rng.normal(0, read_noise, size=n)                         # read noise
    dn = np.clip(np.rint(e * gain / e_per_dn), 0, 255)            # amplify, digitize, clip
    return dn

for label, photons, gain in [("bright, gain 1", 12_000, 1), ("dark,   gain 1", 750, 1), ("dark,   gain 16", 750, 16)]:
    dn = capture(photons, gain)
    print(f"{label}: mean {dn.mean():6.1f} DN, std {dn.std():5.2f} DN, SNR {dn.mean() / dn.std():5.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
bright, gain 1: mean  183.6 DN, std  2.20 DN, SNR  83.6
dark,   gain 1: mean   11.5 DN, std  0.63 DN, SNR  18.1
dark,   gain 16: mean  183.6 DN, std  8.96 DN, SNR  20.5
```

- The dark capture with gain 16 is **as bright** as the bright capture (183.6 DN) but its SNR is **4× worse**: 16× fewer photons → √16 = 4.
- Gain 16 gives a slightly better SNR (20.5) than gain 1 (18.1) in the dark: at gain 1 the noise is under one DN step and the ADC rounding adds error. That is the one thing gain helps with.
- The physics limit for 450 e⁻ with 6 e⁻ read noise is 450 / √(450 + 36) ≈ 20.4. Gain cannot beat it.

### 3. OpenCV: average frames, measure the noise

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=3)
clean = cv2.imread("sample-pinhole-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE).astype(np.float32)

def noisy_frame():
    return np.clip(clean + rng.normal(0, 12, clean.shape), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)

acc = np.zeros(clean.shape, np.float32)
flat = (slice(150, 190), slice(250, 280))          # a flat background patch (value 25)
for n in range(1, 17):
    cv2.accumulate(noisy_frame(), acc)
    if n in (1, 4, 16):
        avg = acc / n
        _, std = cv2.meanStdDev(avg[flat])
        print(f"{n:2d} frames: noise std {std[0, 0]:5.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
 1 frames: noise std 11.54
 4 frames: noise std  5.75
16 frames: noise std  2.95
```

- `cv2.accumulate(src, dst)` adds each frame into a **float32** (or float64) accumulator; 8-bit sums would overflow.
- `cv2.meanStdDev(img)` returns the mean and standard deviation as 1 × 1 arrays (one per channel). The std of a **flat** patch is the noise.
- Noise falls by √N: 11.5 → 5.8 → 3.0. (The first value is a little under 12 because noise below 0 is clipped.)

### 4. Fixed-pattern noise: dark-frame and flat-field correction

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=4)
h, w = 50, 50
offset = rng.normal(5, 2, (h, w))          # DSNU: each pixel's dark level (fixed)
sensitivity = rng.normal(1, 0.03, (h, w))  # PRNU: each pixel's gain (fixed)

def grab(light):                           # one frame, plus a little random noise
    return light * sensitivity + offset + rng.normal(0, 1, (h, w))

dark = np.mean([grab(0) for _ in range(32)], axis=0)        # average dark frames
flat = np.mean([grab(150) for _ in range(32)], axis=0)      # average flat-field frames
raw = grab(100)
corrected = (raw - dark) / (flat - dark) * 150      # back to the flat level's scale
print(f"raw:       mean {raw.mean():6.2f}, spatial std {raw.std():.2f}")
print(f"corrected: mean {corrected.mean():6.2f}, spatial std {corrected.std():.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
raw:       mean 105.09, spatial std 3.71
corrected: mean 100.01, spatial std 1.01
```

- Averaging 32 dark frames gives each pixel's **offset**; averaging 32 flat frames gives its **sensitivity**.
- After correction only the random noise (std ≈ 1) is left; the fixed pattern is gone.
- Cameras often do this internally ("defect pixel correction", "flat-field correction"); check the camera settings before doing it twice.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.accumulate(src, dst[, mask])`** adds `src` to the float accumulator `dst` in place. Related: `cv2.accumulateWeighted(src, dst, alpha)` for a running average of a live stream (useful for background models, Chapter 14.5).

**`cv2.meanStdDev(src[, mask]) → mean, stddev`** returns per-channel mean and standard deviation as arrays of shape (channels, 1). Use a `mask` to measure only a region.

**Noise and dtypes.** Simulate or average in float32/float64, then clip and convert back with `np.clip(...).astype(np.uint8)`. Adding noise directly to `uint8` wraps around (Chapter 1.5).

**Camera settings are not OpenCV settings.** Gain, exposure, black level and on-camera corrections are set through the camera's SDK (GenICam / vendor API). `cv2.VideoCapture` properties exist for some webcams, but industrial cameras normally use their own SDK.

## Where it fails

- **The simple model** (shot + read noise, Poisson + Gaussian) ignores non-linearity, dark current, fixed pattern and on-camera processing. Real cameras are characterized with EMVA 1288.
- **Averaging** needs a still scene and a stable light; flickering lamps (Chapter 2.9) or vibration ruin it.
- **Flat-field correction** is only as good as the flat target and lighting used to make it; redo it when the lens, aperture or lighting changes.
- **"8-bit" images from cameras** may already be gamma-corrected or denoised by the camera (Chapter 2.8), so the √N relationship no longer holds exactly in DN.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Using gain instead of light.** Bright but noisy; algorithms suffer.
- **Letting important areas saturate.** 255 means "unknown", not "very bright".
- **Ignoring temperature.** The same camera is noisier on a hot afternoon or near the furnace.
- **Accumulating in uint8.** Sums overflow; use a float accumulator.
- **Judging noise by eye on a small preview.** Measure the standard deviation of a flat patch.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The photon transfer curve

Plot the noise variance (in DN²) against the mean signal (in DN) for flat images at many light levels. In the shot-noise region the points lie on a straight line whose slope is the camera's **gain in DN per electron**; the intercept gives the read noise. This "photon transfer" method is the core of EMVA 1288 [1][2].

### Why √N

Photon arrivals in a fixed time follow a Poisson distribution, whose variance equals its mean. So the standard deviation is √mean. Read noise is independent, so variances add: σ² = e + σ_read² (+ dark) [2][3].

### Quantization noise

Rounding a continuous value to the nearest integer adds an error uniformly spread over ±0.5, whose standard deviation is 1/√12 ≈ 0.289 of one step. It is negligible once the other noise exceeds about one step.

### Pixel binning

Some cameras can add neighbouring pixels (for example 2 × 2) before or after readout. Charge-domain binning (CCD) adds the signal before read noise; digital binning on CMOS adds read noise for each pixel. Either way you trade resolution for SNR.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A camera has QE 0.5, read noise 5 e⁻ and full well 12 000 e⁻. A pixel receives 8000 photons. (a) Electrons? (b) Total noise? (c) SNR? (d) Dynamic range in dB?

<Solution>

(a) 8000 × 0.5 = **4000 e⁻**. (b) √(4000 + 25) ≈ **63.4 e⁻**. (c) 4000 / 63.4 ≈ **63**. (d) 12 000 / 5 = 2400 → 20·log₁₀(2400) ≈ **67.6 dB**.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Simulate a pixel that receives on average 200 electrons, with read noise 8 e⁻, for 100 000 exposures. Print the measured SNR and compare it with the formula e / √(e + read²).

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=7)
e_mean, read = 200, 8.0
e = rng.poisson(e_mean, 100_000) + rng.normal(0, read, 100_000)
print(f"measured SNR {e.mean() / e.std():.2f}, formula {e_mean / np.sqrt(e_mean + read ** 2):.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
measured SNR 12.35, formula 12.31
```

At 200 e⁻ the read noise (8 e⁻, variance 64) is a large part of the total (variance 264). In darker scenes read noise dominates.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

An inspection image is grainy. The current settings are f/8, exposure 200 µs, gain ×8. The part moves slowly and the depth of field needed is small. Suggest two changes, in order of preference, and say what each does to the SNR.

<Solution>

1. **Open the aperture** to f/4 (DOF allows it): 4× more light → SNR about 2× better; then reduce gain to ×2.
2. **Longer exposure**, for example 400 µs (motion is slow): 2× more light → SNR about 1.4× better; reduce gain further.

(Or add lighting: same effect as more exposure without motion blur.) Gain itself should go **down**, not up, once the light increases.

</Solution>
