# 2.2 Lenses and focus

<Definition term="Lens">an optical element that collects light and redirects it, so that light from each point of the object comes together at one point on the sensor.</Definition>

Now we move from the pinhole camera to the real camera. The big question is: **if a pinhole can form an image, why do real cameras use a lens?** The simple answer:

<Callout kind="key">

A lens lets us **collect much more light** while **still forming a sharp image**.

</Callout>

## 1. Why do we need a lens?

Remember our pinhole camera:

The tiny hole allows only a small amount of light to enter. That creates two problems.

**Problem 1: very little light.** Imagine trying to drink water through a tiny needle hole: you get very little water. Same thing with light:

## 2. What does a lens do?

A lens **controls the direction** of incoming light rays. Instead of simply blocking most of the light like a pinhole, the lens **redirects** the light so that rays from the same object point **come together** at the correct location on the sensor:

<LensRays caption="Very simplified: the lens takes many incoming rays from one point and guides them toward a common point." />

That gives us:

## 3. Think of the lens like a traffic controller

This is a useful mental model. Imagine thousands of cars coming toward an intersection. **Without traffic control**, everything gets mixed. A lens acts more like a **traffic controller**: it directs the light to the correct locations.

## 4. The most important concept: focus

Now we reach one of the most important concepts in cameras:

<Callout kind="key">

**Focus** means making light from an object at a particular distance form a **sharp image on the sensor**.

</Callout>

Suppose we have a camera looking at a bottle. If the lens is correctly positioned, the light converges exactly on the sensor: **sharp image**.

<FocusDiagram objectM={2} focusM={2} caption="Correct lens–sensor relationship: the rays meet on the sensor." />

But if the sensor / lens relationship is wrong, the light meets before (or behind) the sensor and arrives spread out: **blurry image**.

<FocusDiagram objectM={2} focusM={0.8} caption="Wrong lens–sensor relationship: the rays have not met at the sensor, so the point arrives as a patch (red)." />

## 5. What does "sharp" actually mean?

Suppose we have a **tiny point** on an object, for example the tip of a screw. Ideally, the camera should turn that point into **another tiny point** on the sensor. That is sharp.

But if the camera isn't focused correctly, the light **spreads over an area** instead of a tiny point. That creates **blur**:

## 6. Focus distance

Here's something extremely important: a lens **cannot generally make every possible distance perfectly sharp at the same time**.

Suppose your camera is focused at **5 metres**. Objects around that distance will be very sharp. Now put another object at **1 metre**: it may be blurry, depending on the lens, aperture and depth of field.

<DistanceFocus caption="Focus set for 5 m: the person at 5 m is sharp, the person at 1 m is not." />

This is why focus matters in industrial vision.

## 7. What is focal length?

You will hear this term constantly in computer vision, for example a **12 mm, 25 mm, 50 mm or 100 mm lens**. These numbers refer to the lens's **focal length**: a fundamental property describing the lens's optical geometry.

For a simple **thin-lens** model, light arriving from very far away (parallel rays) is brought to a point called the **focal point**, F. The **focal length** is the distance from the lens's optical centre to the focal point. It is usually written **f**.

<FocalSketch />

## 13. Why does the image become blurry?

Let's understand this physically. A point on the object sends light toward the lens. **For a particular object distance, the lens forms the sharp image at a particular image distance.** The rays meet at that image plane.

Suppose your camera is focused on an object at **2 m**. Now the object moves to **5 m**. The light geometry changes, so **the place where the lens forms the sharp image also changes**. That's why focusing is related to object distance.

## 24. Common beginner mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

**Mistake 1: "Focal length is the distance to the object."** No. Focal length ≠ working distance. For example, lens focal length = 25 mm; working distance = 2000 mm.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">

**Mistake 2: "Longer focal length means physically moving the camera farther away."** Not necessarily. Focal length is a property of the lens. Working distance is the camera-to-object setup distance.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">

**Mistake 3: "Focus means zoom."** No. **Zoom** changes the focal length (on a zoom lens). **Focus** makes the image sharp. They are different things.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">

**Mistake 4: "A sharp-looking image is always good enough."** Not for industrial vision. An image can look acceptable to a human but still have insufficient sharpness for edge measurement, OCR, defect detection, dimensional measurement or small-object detection.

</Callout>

This is where we'll answer a very practical question: *why can one camera keep many objects sharp, while another camera has only a small region that is sharp?* We'll connect **aperture → light → depth of field → blur → diffraction**, and then see why these matter enormously in industrial vision.

## Code

Three experiments: the thin-lens numbers behind the blur circle, a simulated autofocus sweep with the variance-of-Laplacian score, and how blur turns into a measurement error.

### 1. From scratch: where the sharp image forms, and how big the blur is

```python
import numpy as np

f = 25.0                     # focal length, mm
N = 2.8                      # f-number (aperture, Chapter 2.3)
D = f / N                    # aperture diameter, mm
pixel = 0.00345              # pixel pitch, mm (3.45 µm)

def image_distance(f, s):
    # thin lens: 1/f = 1/s + 1/s'  ->  s' = 1 / (1/f - 1/s)
    return 1.0 / (1.0 / f - 1.0 / s)

sensor = image_distance(f, 2000.0)          # focus set for an object at 2 m
print(f"sensor sits {sensor:.3f} mm behind the lens")
for s in [1000.0, 2000.0, 5000.0]:
    sp = image_distance(f, s)
    c = D * abs(sensor - sp) / sp           # blur circle diameter on the sensor, mm
    print(f"object at {s / 1000:.0f} m: sharp image at {sp:.3f} mm, blur circle {c * 1000:5.1f} µm = {c / pixel:4.1f} px")
```

Output:

```text
sensor sits 25.316 mm behind the lens
object at 1 m: sharp image at 25.641 mm, blur circle 113.0 µm = 32.8 px
object at 2 m: sharp image at 25.316 mm, blur circle   0.0 µm =  0.0 px
object at 5 m: sharp image at 25.126 mm, blur circle  67.8 µm = 19.7 px
```

- The **thin-lens equation** 1/f = 1/s + 1/s′ links object distance s and image distance s′. A distant object focuses almost exactly at f; a near one focuses farther back.
- The light through an aperture of diameter D forms a cone that meets at s′. A sensor at the wrong place cuts that cone: by similar triangles the blur circle is c = D·|sensor − s′| / s′.
- Differences of a fraction of a millimetre behind the lens become **tens of pixels** of blur. That is why industrial focus is set carefully and locked.

### 2. A simulated autofocus sweep with the variance of the Laplacian

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

chart = cv2.imread("sample-pinhole-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)

def defocus(img, c):
    # blur circle of diameter c pixels: average over a disk
    if c <= 1:
        return img.copy()
    k = np.zeros((c, c), np.float32)
    cv2.circle(k, (c // 2, c // 2), c // 2, 1.0, -1)
    return cv2.filter2D(img, -1, k / k.sum())

def focus_score(gray):
    return cv2.Laplacian(gray, cv2.CV_64F).var()

# autofocus sweep: lens positions give these blur circles (px); the sharpest is at position 3
blur_at_position = [15, 9, 5, 1, 5, 9, 15]
scores = []
for pos, c in enumerate(blur_at_position):
    s = focus_score(defocus(chart, c))
    scores.append(s)
    print(f"position {pos}: blur {c:2d} px, focus score {s:8.1f}")
print("best position:", int(np.argmax(scores)))
```

Output:

```text
position 0: blur 15 px, focus score     10.3
position 1: blur  9 px, focus score     48.9
position 2: blur  5 px, focus score    292.8
position 3: blur  1 px, focus score   4479.8
position 4: blur  5 px, focus score    292.8
position 5: blur  9 px, focus score     48.9
position 6: blur 15 px, focus score     10.3
best position: 3
```

- `cv2.Laplacian(gray, cv2.CV_64F)` responds strongly at edges and fine detail. Blur weakens edges, so the **variance** of the response falls quickly.
- `cv2.CV_64F` keeps negative values. With an 8-bit output, negative responses would be clipped to 0 and the score would be wrong.
- Taking the **position with the highest score** is the basic idea of contrast autofocus (section 17). In a fixed industrial setup you use the same score once, while turning the focus ring, and then lock it.

### 3. Blur becomes measurement error

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

# a bright billet 150 px wide on a dark background (1 px = 1 mm here)
img = np.full((60, 300), 40, np.uint8)
img[:, 75:225] = 220

for c in [1, 9, 21, 41]:
    if c == 1:
        blurred = img
    else:
        k = np.zeros((c, c), np.float32)
        cv2.circle(k, (c // 2, c // 2), c // 2, 1.0, -1)
        blurred = cv2.filter2D(img, -1, k / k.sum())
    row = blurred[30]
    width_mid = np.count_nonzero(row > 130)   # threshold halfway between 40 and 220
    width_hi = np.count_nonzero(row > 200)    # threshold set too high
    print(f"blur {c:2d} px: width {width_mid} mm (mid threshold), {width_hi} mm (high threshold)")
```

Output:

```text
blur  1 px: width 150 mm (mid threshold), 150 mm (high threshold)
blur  9 px: width 150 mm (mid threshold), 144 mm (high threshold)
blur 21 px: width 150 mm (mid threshold), 136 mm (high threshold)
blur 41 px: width 150 mm (mid threshold), 124 mm (high threshold)
```

- When the image is sharp, **any** threshold gives 150 mm: the edge is a clean step.
- When it is blurred, the edge becomes a ramp, and the measured width **depends on where you cut the ramp**. A threshold that is not exactly mid-level (or uneven lighting, which shifts the levels) gives a wrong width.
- In this ideal, noise-free case the mid-level threshold still gives 150 mm. Real images add noise and uneven light, so blur makes the result less certain either way.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.Laplacian(src, ddepth, ksize=1, scale=1, delta=0, borderType=cv2.BORDER_DEFAULT) → dst`**

- `ddepth`: output depth. Use `cv2.CV_64F` (or `CV_32F`) so negative responses survive; `-1` keeps 8-bit and clips them.
- `ksize = 1` (default) uses the 3 × 3 kernel `[[0, 1, 0], [1, -4, 1], [0, 1, 0]]`. Larger odd sizes use Sobel-based second derivatives and respond to coarser detail.
- Works per channel; convert to grayscale first for a single focus score.
- `.var()` on the result gives the variance-of-Laplacian focus measure [5].

**Focus score in practice.** Compute it on a fixed **region of interest** that always contains texture or edges (for example the part being inspected), not on the whole frame. Compare scores only across images of the same scene and lighting.

**OpenCV and the lens.** OpenCV does not control a lens. Camera SDKs or motorized/liquid lenses do that; OpenCV measures the result.

## Where it fails

- **Thin-lens numbers are ideal.** Real lenses are thick, multi-element systems with aberrations. Use the manufacturer's data and test at the real working distance.
- **Focus scores need texture.** A plain, smooth billet face gives a low score even when sharp. Noise raises the score even when blurry. Use a region with edges, and the same lighting.
- **Motion blur is not defocus.** A moving object blurs along its direction of travel. The focus score drops, but refocusing does not help (Chapter 2.6).
- **Depth.** One focus setting cannot make objects at very different distances all sharp. How much depth stays acceptably sharp is depth of field (Chapter 2.3).
- **Field-of-view estimate.** `w·(WD − f)/f` assumes a thin lens and no distortion. Wide-angle lenses distort, and exact values come from calibration (Module 41).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Setting focus on a sample at the wrong height.** Focus on a part at the real working distance (conveyor height, billet top), then lock the ring.
- **Reading a single focus score in isolation.** The number has no absolute meaning; compare it with other images of the same scene.
- **Using `ddepth=-1` with `cv2.Laplacian` on 8-bit images.** Negative edge responses are clipped, and the focus score is wrong.
- **Choosing the lens before the field of view.** Work out the FOV, working distance and accuracy first; the focal length follows (section 21).
- **Ignoring sensor format.** The same 25 mm lens gives a different field of view on a 1/2-inch and a 1.1-inch sensor (section 20).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The thin-lens equation

For an ideal thin lens, an object at distance s forms a sharp image at distance s′ behind the lens, with 1/f = 1/s + 1/s′ (Gaussian form, distances measured from the lens) [1]. As s grows very large, 1/s → 0 and s′ → f: that is the definition of the focal length. Magnification is m = s′/s, which for distant objects is about f/s: the same 1/Z behaviour as the pinhole model of 2.1.

### Blur circle from similar triangles

The aperture of diameter D sends a cone of light that converges at s′. A sensor at distance s_sensor cuts the cone in a disk of diameter c = D·|s_sensor − s′| / s′. Because D = f/N (N is the f-number), closing the aperture shrinks the blur: that is the start of depth of field, Chapter 2.3 [1][2].

### Field of view from the sensor and the lens

The sensor width w maps back onto the object through the magnification. With the thin lens, the object-side width is w/m = w·(s − f)/f, close to w·s/f when s ≫ f. Field of view therefore grows with working distance and sensor size and shrinks with focal length. Chapter 2.4 builds mm per pixel from this.

### Focus measures

The variance of the Laplacian was compared with other focus measures for microscope autofocus by Pech-Pacheco et al. [5]. Pertuz et al. compare dozens of focus operators (gradient-, Laplacian-, wavelet- and statistics-based) and how they behave with noise and contrast [6].

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A 16 mm lens is used with a sensor 8.8 mm wide. (a) Using the thin-lens estimate w·(WD − f)/f, what field of view do you get at a working distance of 1500 mm? (b) The conveyor in section 21 needs at least 500 mm. Is it enough? (c) With 2448 pixels across, how many mm does one pixel cover?

<Solution>

(a) 8.8 × (1500 − 16) / 16 = 8.8 × 92.75 = **816.2 mm**.

(b) **Yes**: 816 mm covers the 500 mm part with margin for position variation.

(c) 816.2 / 2448 ≈ **0.33 mm per pixel**.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Using the thin-lens equation, find the **sensor position** for a 25 mm lens focused at 1.5 m, then the blur circle (in pixels, 3.45 µm pixels, f/2.8) for objects at 1.4 m and 1.6 m. Which side of the focus distance blurs more for the same 100 mm shift?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

f, N, pixel = 25.0, 2.8, 0.00345
D = f / N

def image_distance(f, s):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 / f - 1.0 / s)

sensor = image_distance(f, 1500.0)
for s in [1400.0, 1600.0]:
    sp = image_distance(f, s)
    c = D * abs(sensor - sp) / sp
    print(f"object at {s:.0f} mm: blur {c / pixel:.1f} px")
```

Output:

```text
object at 1400 mm: blur 3.1 px
object at 1600 mm: blur 2.7 px
```

The **nearer** side blurs more for the same 100 mm shift: the image distance changes faster for close objects. This is why depth of field extends further behind the focus distance than in front of it (Chapter 2.3).

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

Your colleague says: *"The camera is 2 m from the conveyor and the lens is 2 m, so it's in focus."* What two things are wrong with this sentence, and what would you check on site?

<Solution>

1. **Focal length is not working distance.** A lens is described by its focal length (for example 25 mm), not by the distance to the object.
2. **Distance alone does not make an image sharp.** The focus ring must be set so that the sensor sits at the image distance for 2 m.

On site: put a textured target at the real conveyor height, adjust the focus ring while watching a focus score (variance of the Laplacian on a fixed ROI), stop at the maximum, and lock the ring.

</Solution>
