# 2.4 Field of view and mm per pixel

<Definition term="mm per pixel">how much real-world distance one pixel covers on the object.</Definition>

This chapter connects the camera to **real-world measurement**. In 1.6 we said *"1200 pixels does not mean 1200 mm"*. Now we learn exactly **how to turn pixels into millimetres**, and how to design a camera so that the millimetres are small enough for the job.

## 1. The big question

Your software finds a billet edge at column 212 and the other edge at column 1411. That is **1199 pixels**. The customer asks: *"How long is the billet, in millimetres?"*

To answer, you need one number: **how many millimetres one pixel covers**. That number is **mm per pixel** (mm/px). Everything in this chapter is about finding it, and making it small enough.

## 2. What is the field of view?

Look at a wall through a cardboard tube. You see only a **patch** of the wall. Move closer and the patch gets smaller; move back and it gets bigger.

<Callout kind="key">

**Field of view (FOV)** = the size of the real-world area the camera sees, usually given as **width × height in mm** at the working distance.

</Callout>

<FovPyramid caption="A 2448 × 2048-pixel camera with a 16 mm lens, 1 m above a surface, sees about 519 × 435 mm." />

## 3. Two ways to describe FOV

| Description | Unit | Example | Depends on distance? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Linear FOV | mm | 519 × 435 mm | Yes |
| Angle of view | degrees | 29.6° horizontal | No |

The **angle** belongs to the camera and lens. The **linear FOV** is what that angle covers at your working distance. For measurement we almost always need the linear FOV.

## 4. What sets the field of view?

From 2.2 you already know the three ingredients:

<Equation terms={[{ value: "Sensor size" }, { op: "+", value: "Focal length" }, { op: "+", value: "Working distance" }, { op: "=", value: "FOV", result: true }]} />

- **Bigger sensor** → wider FOV.
- **Shorter focal length** → wider FOV.
- **Longer working distance** → wider FOV.

## 5. Sensor size from pixels

Datasheets give the resolution (for example **2448 × 2048**) and the **pixel size** (for example **3.45 µm**). Multiply:

<Equation
  terms={[
    { value: "2448", label: "pixels" },
    { op: "×", value: "3.45 µm", label: "pixel size" },
    { op: "=", value: "8.45 mm", label: "sensor width", result: true },
  ]}
/>

And 2048 × 3.45 µm = **7.07 mm** high. This is the light-sensitive area the lens must cover.

## 6. The FOV formula

The lens makes two similar triangles: the sensor on one side, the field of view on the other.

<FovTriangles />

```text
FOV / WD ≈ sensor width / f        →        FOV ≈ sensor width × WD / f
```

The exact thin-lens version, which the tools in this course use, is **FOV = sensor width × (WD − f) / f**. When WD is much bigger than f the two give almost the same answer.

**Worked example.** Sensor 8.45 mm wide, 16 mm lens, WD 1000 mm:

<Equation
  terms={[
    { value: "8.45", label: "sensor (mm)" },
    { op: "×", value: "(1000 − 16)", label: "WD − f" },
    { op: "÷", value: "16", label: "f (mm)" },
    { op: "=", value: "519.4 mm", label: "FOV width", result: true },
  ]}
/>

## 7. Angle of view

The angle depends only on the sensor and the lens:

```text
angle = 2 × atan( sensor width / (2 × f) )
```

8.45 mm sensor, 16 mm lens → **29.6°** horizontally. A wider angle means a wider FOV at every distance.

## 8. mm per pixel

Now the key step. Spread the FOV over the pixels:

<Equation
  terms={[
    { value: "519.4 mm", label: "FOV width" },
    { op: "÷", value: "2448", label: "pixels" },
    { op: "=", value: "0.212 mm/px", label: "scale", result: true },
  ]}
/>

So each pixel covers about **0.21 mm** of the object. Our billet edge-to-edge distance of 1199 pixels would be 1199 × 0.212 ≈ **254 mm**: a short billet, or a camera that is very close.

**Try it.** The calculator from Chapter 1.6, now with proper meaning: change the FOV and the image width.

<ScaleCalc />

## 9. Magnification

Another way to say the same thing: **magnification** m = sensor size ÷ FOV. Here 8.45 / 519.4 ≈ **0.016**: the object is imaged at 1/61 of its size. Then

```text
mm per pixel = pixel size ÷ magnification = 0.00345 mm ÷ 0.0163 ≈ 0.212 mm
```

Machine-vision lens catalogues often use magnification, especially for close-up and telecentric lenses.

## 10. Square pixels, two directions

Most industrial sensors have **square pixels**, so mm/px is the same horizontally and vertically: 519.4 / 2448 = 434.5 / 2048 = 0.212.

The FOV is **not** square, though: 2448 × 2048 is wider than tall. Put **long objects along the sensor's long side**.

## 11. How small must a pixel be?

This is the design question. A pixel must be small compared with:

- the **smallest thing you must detect** (a scratch, a crack, a missing rod), and
- the **measurement tolerance** you must guarantee.

Two common rules of thumb (not standards; check them on your own images):

<Columns>
<Panel title="Detection" tone="a">
The smallest feature should cover **at least 2–3 pixels**, more for reliable classification.
</Panel>
<Panel title="Measurement" tone="b">
The scale should be **several times finer** than the tolerance. A frequent target is mm/px about **1/10 of the tolerance** band, before sub-pixel methods.
</Panel>
</Columns>

## 12. Working backwards: from requirement to camera

Real projects start from the requirement, not from the camera. For example: inspect a **500 mm belt**, find **1 mm scratches**, camera **1.5 m** above.

## 13. Choosing the lens

Solve the FOV formula for f: **f = sensor × WD / (FOV + sensor)** = 8.45 × 1500 / 558.45 ≈ **22.7 mm**. Nobody sells a 22.7 mm lens, so check the standard ones:

| Lens | FOV at 1.5 m | mm/px | Scratch covers | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 mm | 1047 mm | 0.428 | 2.3 px | too coarse |
| 16 mm | 783 mm | 0.320 | 3.1 px | ✓ |
| 25 mm | 498 mm | 0.204 | 4.9 px | FOV too small |

The 16 mm lens passes. The 25 mm lens is sharper, but does not cover the belt; raise the camera to about **1.65 m** and it would (549 mm).

**Try it.** Enter your own requirement and see which lens works.

## 14. Calibrating the scale from the image

Formulas get you close. On site, you **measure the scale**: place an object of known size (a ruler, a calibrated block, a target) in the field of view, at the **same height as the real parts**, and count its pixels.

<Equation
  terms={[
    { value: "100 mm", label: "known length" },
    { op: "÷", value: "50 px", label: "measured in image" },
    { op: "=", value: "2.0 mm/px", label: "scale", result: true },
  ]}
/>

Then every measurement is **pixels × scale**. Code example 3 does this with OpenCV.

## 15. The height trap

The scale is only valid **on the plane where you measured it**. An object that is **closer** to the camera looks bigger (perspective, 2.1), so it reads too long.

<HeightErrorSketch />

At 3 m working distance, a 1200 mm billet whose top is **150 mm higher** than the calibration plane reads **1263 mm**: a 63 mm error. Even 50 mm higher gives +20 mm.

<Callout kind="mistake">

Calibrate at the height of the surface you measure, keep the parts at that height, or correct for height. Precision gauging often uses **telecentric lenses**, whose scale hardly changes with distance.

</Callout>

## 16. The edges of the image

Real lenses, especially short focal lengths, have **distortion**: straight lines bend near the edges and mm/px is slightly different there. For accurate measurement you **calibrate the camera** (Module 41) and correct the distortion, or keep the measured features near the centre.

## 17. Sub-pixel: better than one pixel

An edge is not a hard step; it is a gradient over a few pixels. By fitting that gradient, software can find edge positions to **a fraction of a pixel** (often around 1/10 of a pixel in good conditions). That is how a 0.2 mm/px system can measure to better than 0.2 mm. It only works with **sharp focus, good lighting and low noise** (2.2, 2.3, 2.5).

## 18. Accuracy, resolution and repeatability

<Glossary
  items={[
    { term: "Resolution", desc: "The smallest step the system can show: roughly mm per pixel (finer with sub-pixel methods)." },
    { term: "Repeatability", desc: "Measure the same part many times: how much do the readings scatter?" },
    { term: "Accuracy", desc: "How close the average reading is to the true value. Needs calibration against a known reference." },
  ]}
/>

A system can be very repeatable and still inaccurate, for example because of the height trap in section 15.

## 19. Industrial example: slab width

A **2000 mm slab** must be measured to **±2 mm**. With the 1/10 rule we want about **0.2 mm/px**, so 2000 / 0.2 = **10,000 pixels** across. That is more than a normal area-scan camera has.

The usual solution: **don't look at the whole slab**. Use **two cameras**, each with a narrow FOV over **one edge**, at a calibrated distance apart. Each edge is measured finely; the width is the camera spacing plus the two edge offsets.

<TwoCameraSlab />

Another option for very long or wide objects is a **line-scan camera** (Chapter 2.10).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A camera sees a 600 mm wide area with 1920 pixels across. What is the mm/px?

<Solution>

600 / 1920 = **0.3125 mm/px**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** The same camera is moved twice as far away (same lens). What happens to the FOV and to mm/px?

<Solution>

Both roughly **double**: about 1200 mm wide and 0.625 mm/px. The camera sees more, but each pixel covers more.

</Solution>

**Q3.** You must detect 0.5 mm cracks with at least 3 pixels on them, over a 300 mm wide area. How many pixels across do you need?

<Solution>

0.5 / 3 = 0.167 mm/px, and 300 / 0.167 ≈ **1800 pixels**.

</Solution>

**Q4.** Why does a billet lying on a higher roller table read longer than one on the calibration plane?

<Solution>

It is closer to the camera, so it covers more pixels (perspective). With the scale from the lower plane, its length in mm comes out too large.

</Solution>

**Q5.** Which change makes mm/px smaller (finer)? A. Shorter focal length · B. More pixels across the same FOV · C. Larger working distance

<Solution>

**B.** More pixels across the same FOV. (A and C both widen the FOV and make mm/px larger.)

</Solution>

## Code

Four calculations: the FOV and scale of a real camera, choosing a lens from a requirement, calibrating the scale from a reference object with OpenCV, and the height trap.

### 1. From scratch: sensor size, FOV, angle and mm per pixel

```python
import numpy as np

px_w, px_h = 2448, 2048          # sensor resolution, pixels
pitch = 0.00345                  # pixel size, mm (3.45 µm)
f = 16.0                         # focal length, mm
wd = 1000.0                      # working distance, mm (lens to object)

sensor_w, sensor_h = px_w * pitch, px_h * pitch
fov_w = sensor_w * (wd - f) / f  # thin lens
fov_h = sensor_h * (wd - f) / f
angle = 2 * np.degrees(np.arctan(sensor_w / (2 * f)))
print(f"sensor: {sensor_w:.2f} x {sensor_h:.2f} mm")
print(f"FOV at {wd:.0f} mm: {fov_w:.1f} x {fov_h:.1f} mm, horizontal angle {angle:.1f} deg")
print(f"mm per pixel: {fov_w / px_w:.3f} (x), {fov_h / px_h:.3f} (y)")
```

Output:

```text
sensor: 8.45 x 7.07 mm
FOV at 1000 mm: 519.4 x 434.5 mm, horizontal angle 29.6 deg
mm per pixel: 0.212 (x), 0.212 (y)
```

- Square pixels give the same scale in x and y.
- The angle does not depend on `wd`; the FOV in mm does.

### 2. From scratch: from a requirement to a lens

```python
import numpy as np

need_fov = 550.0        # mm: 500 mm belt + 10% margin
feature = 1.0           # mm: smallest scratch to detect
px_per_feature = 3      # pixels across the smallest feature
wd = 1500.0             # mm
px_w, pitch = 2448, 0.00345
sensor_w = px_w * pitch

need_mmpp = feature / px_per_feature
print(f"need <= {need_mmpp:.3f} mm/px -> at least {need_fov / need_mmpp:.0f} px across")
f_exact = sensor_w * wd / (need_fov + sensor_w)          # thin lens solved for f
print(f"exact focal length for {need_fov:.0f} mm FOV: {f_exact:.1f} mm")
for f in [8, 12, 16, 25, 35]:
    fov = sensor_w * (wd - f) / f
    mmpp = fov / px_w
    ok = fov >= need_fov and mmpp <= need_mmpp
    print(f"{f:2d} mm lens: FOV {fov:6.1f} mm, {mmpp:.3f} mm/px, scratch = {feature / mmpp:.1f} px  {'OK' if ok else '--'}")
print(f"25 mm lens at 1650 mm: FOV {sensor_w * (1650 - 25) / 25:.1f} mm")
```

Output:

```text
need <= 0.333 mm/px -> at least 1650 px across
exact focal length for 550 mm FOV: 22.7 mm
 8 mm lens: FOV 1575.1 mm, 0.643 mm/px, scratch = 1.6 px  --
12 mm lens: FOV 1047.3 mm, 0.428 mm/px, scratch = 2.3 px  --
16 mm lens: FOV  783.3 mm, 0.320 mm/px, scratch = 3.1 px  OK
25 mm lens: FOV  498.3 mm, 0.204 mm/px, scratch = 4.9 px  --
35 mm lens: FOV  353.5 mm, 0.144 mm/px, scratch = 6.9 px  --
25 mm lens at 1650 mm: FOV 549.0 mm
```

- Only one standard lens meets both conditions at 1.5 m. Moving the camera is the other knob.
- The 8 mm lens sees a lot but puts only 1.6 pixels on a scratch: too coarse to detect it reliably.

### 3. OpenCV: calibrate the scale from a reference block, then measure

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

# synthetic top view: a 100 mm reference block and a billet, on a dark belt
img = np.full((300, 900), 30, np.uint8)
cv2.rectangle(img, (40, 120), (89, 179), 230, -1)       # reference block, 100 mm long
cv2.rectangle(img, (220, 110), (819, 189), 200, -1)     # billet

_, bw = cv2.threshold(img, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
contours, _ = cv2.findContours(bw, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE)
boxes = sorted(cv2.boundingRect(c) for c in contours)   # (x, y, w, h), left to right
ref, billet = boxes
mm_per_px = 100.0 / ref[2]
print("reference:", ref, "-> mm per pixel:", mm_per_px)
print("billet:", billet, "-> length", billet[2] * mm_per_px, "mm")
```

Output:

```text
reference: (40, 120, 50, 60) -> mm per pixel: 2.0
billet: (220, 110, 600, 80) -> length 1200.0 mm
```

- `cv2.rectangle` with thickness `-1` fills the rectangle; both corner pixels are included, so (40 … 89) is **50** pixels wide.
- `cv2.boundingRect` returns `(x, y, w, h)`: the top-left corner and the size in pixels.
- The scale comes from the image itself: **100 mm ÷ 50 px = 2.0 mm/px**. The reference must lie at the same height as the billet.

### 4. The height trap in numbers

```python
import numpy as np

wd = 3000.0             # camera to the calibration plane, mm
true_len = 1200.0       # billet length, mm
for h in [0.0, 50.0, 150.0]:      # billet top this far above the calibration plane
    measured = true_len * wd / (wd - h)
    print(f"top {h:5.0f} mm higher -> reads {measured:7.1f} mm (error {measured - true_len:+.1f} mm)")
```

Output:

```text
top     0 mm higher -> reads  1200.0 mm (error +0.0 mm)
top    50 mm higher -> reads  1220.3 mm (error +20.3 mm)
top   150 mm higher -> reads  1263.2 mm (error +63.2 mm)
```

- Image size goes with 1 / distance (2.1), so a surface h closer reads longer by the factor WD / (WD − h).
- Doubling the working distance halves the error from the same height difference: one reason measurement cameras are often mounted high.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.boundingRect(points) → (x, y, w, h)`**: the upright rectangle around a contour, in whole pixels. For rotated parts use `cv2.minAreaRect(points) → ((cx, cy), (w, h), angle)`, which gives float sizes.

**`cv2.findContours(binary, mode, method) → contours, hierarchy`** (OpenCV 4.x returns two values). `cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL` keeps outer contours only; `cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE` compresses straight runs.

**Pixel counting conventions.** A filled rectangle from column 40 to 89 is 50 pixels wide (89 − 40 + 1). Edge-to-edge distance between two detected edge positions (for example 212 and 1411) is 1411 − 212 = 1199 pixels. Be consistent, or you build in a one-pixel error.

**OpenCV has no "mm".** All OpenCV results are in pixels. Converting to millimetres is your scale (this chapter) or a full camera calibration (`cv2.calibrateCamera`, Module 41).

## Where it fails

- **Distortion.** One scale for the whole image ignores lens distortion. Calibrate the camera or keep measurements near the centre.
- **Different heights.** Parts above or below the calibration plane read wrong (section 15). Use a fixed height, a height measurement, or a telecentric lens.
- **Tilted camera.** If the camera is not square to the surface, the scale changes across the image (perspective). Correct it with a homography (Module 41) or mount the camera square.
- **Thin-lens FOV.** The formula is an estimate. Real lenses and the lens's actual principal point differ; measure the FOV on site.
- **Rules of thumb.** "3 pixels per feature" and "1/10 of the tolerance" are starting points; contrast, noise and edge quality decide what really works.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Choosing the camera before the requirement.** Start from feature size, tolerance and area.
- **Forgetting margin.** Parts are not perfectly centred; leave 10–20 % extra FOV.
- **Calibrating with a reference lying on the floor while parts ride on rollers.** Calibrate at the part's height.
- **Mixing the off-by-one pixel conventions** (`x2 − x1` vs `x2 − x1 + 1`).
- **Assuming more megapixels fix everything.** Optics, focus and lighting must also support the finer scale (2.2, 2.3).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Where the FOV formula comes from

With the thin lens, magnification m = s′ / s = f / (s − f) for an object at distance s. The object-side size of the sensor is sensor / m = sensor × (s − f) / f. For s ≫ f this is sensor × s / f, the similar-triangles estimate [1][2].

### Sensor formats

Sensor "inch" formats (1/2″, 2/3″, 1.1″ …) are historical names from video camera tubes; they do not equal any physical dimension of the sensor. Always compute the size from pixels × pixel size, or read the active-area size in the datasheet [2].

### Telecentric lenses

An object-side telecentric lens accepts only rays parallel to its axis, so magnification hardly changes with distance inside its working range. The price: the FOV can be no larger than the front lens, so telecentric lenses are large and used for small, precise parts [2].

### Sub-pixel edges

Edge positions can be estimated to fractions of a pixel by fitting the intensity profile across the edge or interpolating the gradient peak. Steger et al. discuss sub-pixel edge extraction and its accuracy limits in detail [2].

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A 1920 × 1200 camera with 5.86 µm pixels uses a 12 mm lens at 800 mm. (a) Sensor width? (b) FOV width (thin lens)? (c) mm/px? (d) Can it put 3 pixels on a 1 mm defect?

<Solution>

(a) 1920 × 0.00586 = **11.25 mm**.

(b) 11.25 × (800 − 12) / 12 ≈ **738.8 mm**.

(c) 738.8 / 1920 ≈ **0.385 mm/px**.

(d) A 1 mm defect covers 1 / 0.385 ≈ 2.6 pixels: **just short** of 3. Use a 16 mm lens (FOV ≈ 552 mm, 0.287 mm/px, 3.5 pixels) if 552 mm is wide enough.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Two calibration marks 500 mm apart are found at pixel positions (312.4, 518.0) and (1561.9, 521.5). (a) Compute mm/px from their distance. (b) A billet's edges are at x = 402.0 and x = 1478.5 on the same row. How long is it?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([312.4, 518.0])
b = np.array([1561.9, 521.5])
mm_per_px = 500.0 / np.linalg.norm(b - a)
print(f"scale: {mm_per_px:.4f} mm/px")
print(f"billet: {(1478.5 - 402.0) * mm_per_px:.1f} mm")
```

Output:

```text
scale: 0.4002 mm/px
billet: 430.8 mm
```

`np.linalg.norm` gives the straight-line distance between the marks in pixels (it also handles a slightly tilted pair). Sub-pixel positions like 312.4 come from edge or centroid fitting.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (by hand)

A camera 2.5 m above a roller table was calibrated on the rollers. A 600 mm wide plate is 40 mm thick. (a) What width does the camera read for its top surface? (b) What would you change?

<Solution>

(a) 600 × 2500 / (2500 − 40) ≈ **609.8 mm**: almost 10 mm too wide.

(b) Calibrate on a target at the plate-top height, or correct using the known thickness (scale × (WD − h) / WD), or use a telecentric or stereo/laser height measurement for varying thickness.

</Solution>
