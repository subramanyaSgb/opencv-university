# 35.6 Bag of visual words and image retrieval

<Definition term="Bag of visual words (BoVW)">a fixed-length histogram that describes a whole image by counting how many of its local descriptors (33.1–34.6) fall near each of a fixed set of reference descriptors ("visual words"), learned once from a collection of images by clustering. Two images with different numbers and positions of keypoints can still be compared directly, because both end up as histograms of the same fixed length.</Definition>

Modules 33–34 found individual points and matched them one-to-one between two specific images. Bag of visual words goes a different direction: throw away *where* each descriptor is, keep only *which kind* of descriptor it is, and count. The result is one short vector per image — exactly the ingredient a search engine or classifier (Module 36) needs.

## 1. An analogy

Describing a bookshelf by walking along it and, for every book, noting only its colour: so many red spines, so many blue, so many green — not which shelf, not the order. Two bookshelves with a similar mix of colours feel similar even if no two books are in the same position. BoVW does this with "colours of local image patches" instead of book colours, and the "colours" themselves are learned, not predefined.

## 2. Building a vocabulary and a histogram

<Flow

    { label: "Cluster them into K groups (k-means, 27.2) = the vocabulary", tone: "process" },

- **Vocabulary**: run k-means (27.2 covers the algorithm in full; here the "pixels" being clustered are descriptor vectors, not colours) on a large pool of descriptors gathered from many images. Each of the K cluster centres is a **visual word** — not a real word, just a representative descriptor.
- **Assignment**: for one image, take each of its own descriptors and find the nearest visual word (smallest distance to a cluster centre).
- **Histogram**: count how many descriptors landed on each word. Normalising this count (so it sums to 1) makes the histogram comparable between images with very different numbers of keypoints.

## 3. A worked example by hand

Three visual words (toy 2-D "descriptors" here, for arithmetic you can check by hand; real ones are 128-D SIFT vectors): word 0 = (0, 0), word 1 = (10, 0), word 2 = (5, 10).

An image's five descriptors, and the nearest word for each:

| Descriptor | Distance to word 0 | Distance to word 1 | Distance to word 2 | Nearest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1, 1) | 1.41 | 9.06 | 9.85 | word 0 |
| (9, 1) | 9.06 | 1.41 | 9.85 | word 1 |
| (4, 9) | 9.85 | 10.82 | 1.41 | word 2 |
| (2, 2) | 2.83 | 8.25 | 8.54 | word 0 |
| (11, −1) | 11.05 | 1.41 | 12.53 | word 1 |

Counting assignments: word 0 → 2, word 1 → 2, word 2 → 1. **BoVW histogram = [2, 2, 1]**, or normalised, **[0.4, 0.4, 0.2]**.

## 4. Retrieval: ranking a small database against a query

A vocabulary of 32 visual words, learned from SIFT descriptors of 5 database images (`sample-poster.png`, `sample-feat.png`, `sample-scene.png`, `sample-color.png`, `sample-board.png`), then every image's BoVW histogram compared with a query's by cosine similarity:

| Query | Rank 1 | Rank 2 | Rank 3 | Rank 4 | Rank 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| poster, transformed | **poster, 0.867** | colour scene, 0.512 | scene, 0.472 | feature scene, 0.363 | board, 0.162 |
| feature scene, transformed | **feature scene, 0.924** | poster, 0.468 | scene, 0.303 | colour scene, 0.167 | board, 0.062 |

Both queries are geometrically transformed views of a database image (34.5's homography), yet both correctly rank their true match first, by a wide margin (0.867 and 0.924, versus the next-best 0.512 and 0.468). This is the payoff: BoVW retrieval survived a transformation that would make a pixel-by-pixel image comparison fail completely, because it never looked at pixel positions — only at which visual words were present, and how often.

## 5. Industrial and real-world use

- **Image and video retrieval**: Sivic and Zisserman's "Video Google" [1] searched video frames for matching objects this way, inspiring the name.
- **Scene and object category recognition**: a BoVW histogram is a fixed-length feature vector, so Module 36's classifiers (SVM, kNN, and others) can be trained on it directly.
- **Duplicate/near-duplicate detection at a coarser, more viewpoint-tolerant level than perceptual hashing (35.5)** — useful when images of the *same object* are expected to differ in angle or scale, not just brightness.
- **Loop closure in visual SLAM** (43.3): recognising "I have been at this place before" from a BoVW-style comparison of the current view against previously visited ones.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Two visual words: word A = (0, 0), word B = (6, 0). A descriptor at (4, 1): which word is it nearest to?

<Solution>

Distance to A = √(16+1) = 4.12. Distance to B = √(4+1) = 2.24. Nearer to **word B**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Image 1's BoVW histogram (3 words) is [6, 2, 2] (10 descriptors total); Image 2's is [3, 1, 1] (5 descriptors total). Before comparing them, what should you do, and why?

<Solution>

**Normalise** each histogram by its own total (divide by 10 and by 5 respectively), giving [0.6, 0.2, 0.2] for both — they describe the *same mix* of visual words, just from different numbers of keypoints. Comparing the raw counts directly would make Image 1 look different from Image 2 purely because it had more keypoints, not because its content actually differed.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. The worked example, and a real vocabulary with cv2.BOWKMeansTrainer

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

words = np.array([[0, 0], [10, 0], [5, 10]], dtype=np.float64)
descriptors = np.array([[1, 1], [9, 1], [4, 9], [2, 2], [11, -1]], dtype=np.float64)
nearest = [int(np.argmin(np.linalg.norm(words - d, axis=1))) for d in descriptors]
hist = np.bincount(nearest, minlength=len(words))
print("nearest word per descriptor:", nearest, " histogram:", hist.tolist())

sift = cv2.SIFT_create()
names = ["sample-poster.png", "sample-feat.png", "sample-scene.png", "sample-color.png", "sample-board.png"]
imgs = {n: cv2.imread(n, cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE) for n in names}
descs = {n: sift.detectAndCompute(im, None)[1] for n, im in imgs.items()}

trainer = cv2.BOWKMeansTrainer(32)                   # K = 32 visual words
for d in descs.values():
    trainer.add(d)
vocab = trainer.cluster()                             # (32, 128): one SIFT-sized descriptor per word
print("vocabulary shape:", vocab.shape)
```

Output:

```text
nearest word per descriptor: [0, 1, 2, 0, 1]  histogram: [2, 2, 1]
vocabulary shape: (32, 128)
```

### 2. Histograms and retrieval with cv2.BOWImgDescriptorExtractor

```python
bow = cv2.BOWImgDescriptorExtractor(sift, cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_L2))
bow.setVocabulary(vocab)

def bow_hist(img):
    kp = sift.detect(img, None)
    return bow.compute(img, kp).flatten()

hist_db = {n: bow_hist(im) for n, im in imgs.items()}
query = cv2.imread("sample-poster-b.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)      # a transformed view, 34.5/34.6
hist_q = bow_hist(query)

def cosine(a, b):
    return float(np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b) + 1e-9))

ranking = sorted(((n, cosine(hist_q, h)) for n, h in hist_db.items()), key=lambda x: -x[1])
for name, sim in ranking:
    print(f"{name}: {sim:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
sample-poster.png: 0.8668
sample-color.png: 0.5123
sample-scene.png: 0.4717
sample-feat.png: 0.3630
sample-board.png: 0.1616
```

`sample-poster.png` — the true match — ranks first, clearly separated from the rest.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.BOWKMeansTrainer(clusterCount[, termcrit[, attempts[, flags]]])`**: wraps `cv2.kmeans` (27.2) for clustering descriptors. `.add(descriptors)` accumulates a training set (call once per image, or pool everything first); `.cluster()` runs k-means and returns the vocabulary, shape `(clusterCount, descriptorDim)`, `float32`.
- **`cv2.BOWImgDescriptorExtractor(dextractor, dmatcher)`**: needs a descriptor extractor (e.g. `cv2.SIFT_create()`) and a matcher (e.g. `cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_L2)`) to assign descriptors to the nearest vocabulary word. `.setVocabulary(vocab)` sets the words; `.compute(img, keypoints)` returns the normalised histogram as a `(1, K)` `float32` array.
- **Only works with the matcher's distance**: `cv2.NORM_L2` (float descriptors, e.g. SIFT) needs a matcher built for float descriptors; binary descriptors (ORB, BRIEF, AKAZE, 34.2–34.3) need `cv2.NORM_HAMMING` and k-means is not the natural clustering choice for them (see Where it fails).
- **Speed**: `.cluster()` (the k-means step) is the expensive one-time cost of building a vocabulary; `.compute()` on a new image is fast (33.1–34.6's extraction, then one nearest-word search per descriptor).

## Where it fails

- **Binary descriptors (ORB, AKAZE) don't cluster well with ordinary k-means**, which assumes a Euclidean (continuous) feature space; clustering binary descriptors properly needs Hamming-distance-aware methods (e.g. k-medoids, or hierarchical clustering on Hamming distance) — this is why the classic BoVW pipeline, and this chapter's example, use SIFT.
- **No spatial information at all**: two images with the same visual words in a completely different arrangement get similar (even identical) histograms — for tasks where layout matters, spatial verification (geometric consistency with RANSAC, 26.3) is usually added after an initial BoVW-based shortlist.
- **Vocabulary size (K) is a real trade-off**: too small merges genuinely different local patterns into one word (losing discriminative power); too large makes the histogram sparse and sensitive to noise, and the vocabulary expensive to build.
- **A vocabulary learned from one kind of image collection may not transfer well to a very different one** — it reflects whatever local patterns were common in the images used to build it.
- Superseded for many retrieval/recognition tasks today by learned embeddings (a CNN feature vector, Part J), which remove most of BoVW's hand-designed steps and tend to perform better when training data is available.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Comparing un-normalised histograms** (Mini exercise Q2) — always normalise before comparing images with different keypoint counts.
- **Building the vocabulary from only one image, or far too few images**, and then expecting it to generalise — a vocabulary needs a representative pool of descriptors to be useful.
- **Mixing descriptor types** (training the vocabulary on SIFT descriptors, then assigning ORB descriptors to it) — the vocabulary only means something in the feature space it was built from.
- **Treating BoVW as giving an exact match confidence**: a high cosine similarity (Section 4) means "very similar visual-word content," which is strong retrieval evidence, not photometric or geometric proof of a correct match (a few false positives are expected; that's what a later verification step, e.g. RANSAC-based matching, 34.5, is for).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Video Google and TF-IDF weighting

Sivic and Zisserman [1] borrowed a key idea from text retrieval: weight each visual word's contribution by **TF-IDF** (term frequency, inverse document frequency) — a word that appears in almost every image in the collection (e.g. one corresponding to a very common texture pattern) is down-weighted, because it carries little information about which specific image matches; a rarer, more distinctive word is up-weighted. This chapter's plain counts are the simpler version; TF-IDF weighting usually improves retrieval quality further.

### Spatial pyramids and VLAD

A **spatial pyramid** (Lazebnik, Schmid, Ponce) keeps a little position information by building separate BoVW histograms for coarse spatial regions of the image, then concatenating them — a middle ground between no spatial information (this chapter) and full keypoint positions. **VLAD** (Vector of Locally Aggregated Descriptors) replaces each word's count with the *sum of the residuals* (descriptor minus its assigned word's centre) in that word's bin — a richer, higher-dimensional summary than a simple histogram, generally more accurate for retrieval.

### From hand-built vocabularies to learned embeddings

Modern image retrieval increasingly replaces the whole cluster-and-histogram pipeline with a single learned embedding (a CNN trained so that similar images map to nearby vectors, Part J) — conceptually the same goal as BoVW (a fixed-length, comparable summary of an image) reached by a very different, learned route.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Two visual words: word A = (0, 0), word B = (4, 4). Three descriptors: (1, 0), (3, 5), (2, 2). Build the (unnormalised) BoVW histogram.

<Solution>

(1, 0): distance to A = 1, to B = √(9+16) = 5 → word A.
(3, 5): distance to A = √(9+25) = 5.83, to B = √(1+1) = 1.41 → word B.
(2, 2): distance to A = √(4+4) = 2.83, to B = √(4+4) = 2.83 → **tie** — conventionally assigned to the lower-indexed word, A (or either, consistently, in a real implementation `np.argmin` picks the first).
Histogram = **[2, 1]** (word A: (1,0) and (2,2); word B: (3,5)).

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A colleague wants to retrieve, from a database of 10 000 part photos, every photo containing the *same individual part* as a query photo, taken from a different angle. Is plain BoVW (as built in this chapter) the right tool, or does it need something added?

<Solution>

Plain BoVW is a reasonable **first-stage shortlist**: it is fast, handles viewpoint/scale changes well (Section 4), and narrows 10 000 images down to a short list of likely candidates. But a high BoVW similarity only means "similar mix of local patterns," not "verified to be the same object" — two different parts with similar surface texture could score highly. In practice this is combined with a **second, verification stage**: for the top candidates from BoVW, run actual feature matching with the ratio test and RANSAC (34.4–34.5) to confirm a geometrically consistent match, which plain histogram comparison cannot provide on its own.

</Solution>
