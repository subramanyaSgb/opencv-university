# 35.2 GLCM

<Definition term="Grey-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM)">a table that counts, over a whole region, how often grey level *i* occurs at a chosen distance and direction from grey level *j*. Statistics computed from this table (contrast, homogeneity, energy, correlation, entropy) are a classic texture descriptor, introduced by Haralick, Shanmugam and Dinstein.</Definition>

LBP (35.1) asks "what pattern of lighter/darker surrounds this pixel?" GLCM asks a related but different question: "across the whole region, when I see grey level *i*, how often is the pixel one step away grey level *j*?" It is one of the oldest texture descriptors in image processing (1973) and still a standard baseline.

## 1. An analogy

Imagine recording, for every pair of adjacent houses on a street, "house 1 is this colour, house 2 (one door further along) is that colour" — for every pair, not just the colour of each house alone. A street painted in alternating black and white has pairs that are almost always (black, white) or (white, black); a street painted in one flat grey has pairs that are almost always (grey, grey). The *pairs* reveal the pattern that the paint alone does not.

Why quantize first? With 256 grey levels the matrix would be 256×256 = 65 536 cells, and a small region would spread a few thousand pixel pairs so thinly across them that almost every cell would be 0 or 1 — too sparse to say anything. Reducing to `L` = 16 levels (dividing each grey value by 256/16 = 16) gives a 16×16 = 256-cell matrix with enough pairs per cell to be meaningful.

## 3. A worked example by hand

A tiny 1×4 strip of quantized grey levels (L = 4 levels: 0, 1, 2, 3), reading left to right: `0, 0, 2, 3`. Count every adjacent pair at distance 1, direction "right" (i, j) = (left pixel, right pixel):

| Pair (i → j) | Occurs at |
| --- | --- |
| (0, 0) | pixels 1→2 |
| (0, 2) | pixels 2→3 |
| (2, 3) | pixels 3→4 |

The 4×4 matrix has a 1 at (0,0), a 1 at (0,2), a 1 at (2,3), and 0 everywhere else (3 pairs total, matching 4 pixels − 1). Normalising by the total (divide by 3) gives the probabilities P(i,j) used in the formulas below. **Contrast** = Σ (i−j)² P(i,j) = (0−0)²·⅓ + (0−2)²·⅓ + (2−3)²·⅓ = 0 + 4/3 + 1/3 = **5/3 ≈ 1.67**: most of the "energy" in this tiny strip comes from the big (0, 2) jump.

## 4. The features, and why direction matters

<GlcmLab initialSource="woven" initialAngle="0" caption="Choose a texture and a direction. The woven fabric's matrix and features change noticeably between 0° and 90° — try both. Switch to 'fabric with a defect' to compare the defect patch with the rest."/>

Computing the GLCM at distance 1 in two directions (16 grey levels) on the three 72×72 textures from 35.1:

| Texture | Contrast (0°, horizontal) | Contrast (90°, vertical) | Correlation (0°) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Woven fabric | 4.31 | 1.58 | 0.51 |
| Smooth shading | 0.29 | 0.29 | 0.82 |
| Blotchy | 1.55 | 1.47 | 0.92 |

The woven fabric's contrast is **nearly 3× higher horizontally than vertically** (4.31 vs 1.58): this texture was built with stronger vertical lines (bigger brightness drop) than horizontal ones, so stepping sideways crosses a bigger jump more often than stepping downward does. Smooth and blotchy, which have no preferred direction, score almost the same both ways. **This is the point of computing a GLCM at several angles**: a big difference between directions is itself a measurement — it says the texture is anisotropic (directional), and roughly how strongly.

## 5. Catching a defect with contrast, not brightness

Reusing the "missing weave" patch from 35.1 (same mean grey level, 142.6 vs 142.3, as its surroundings): computing the horizontal (0°) GLCM separately inside the 24×24 defect patch and in a same-size normal patch elsewhere in the fabric —

| Region | Contrast | Homogeneity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defect patch (missing weave) | 0.49 | 0.76 |
| Normal fabric | 4.33 | 0.67 |

Contrast falls to about a ninth of its normal value, and homogeneity rises, because the patch is close to uniform noise: adjacent quantized levels are usually equal or one step apart, never the large jumps the weave produces elsewhere. A region with this GLCM signature can be flagged automatically, with no brightness threshold involved.

## 6. Industrial use

- **Textile and leather grading**: contrast and homogeneity by direction separate weave classes.
- **Wood and laminate inspection**: grain direction shows up directly as a directional contrast difference.
- **Satellite and aerial imagery**: classic use for classifying terrain (forest, farmland, urban) by texture.
- **Medical imaging**: GLCM features of tissue regions (e.g. in CT/MRI) have long been used as hand-built features for tissue classification, before CNN features became common (Part J).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A 1×5 quantized strip (4 levels): `1, 1, 1, 1, 1`. What is the contrast, computed the way Section 3 did?

<Solution>

Every adjacent pair is (1, 1), so (i−j)² = 0 for all of them: contrast = **0**. A perfectly flat region always has zero GLCM contrast, whatever its grey level.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why use 16 grey levels instead of 256 for a small region?

<Solution>

With 256 levels the matrix has 65 536 cells; a small region's handful of pixel pairs would be spread almost one-per-cell, giving a matrix too sparse for the statistics to mean anything. Quantizing to 16 (or similar) levels concentrates enough pairs per cell to compute stable contrast/homogeneity/energy numbers (Section 2).

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. GLCM from scratch with NumPy

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

LEVELS = 16

def quantize(img, levels=LEVELS):
    return (img.astype(np.int32) * levels // 256).astype(np.uint8)   # 0..255 -> 0..levels-1

def glcm_numpy(img, dx, dy, levels=LEVELS):
    q = quantize(img, levels)
    H, W = q.shape
    a = q[max(0, -dy):H - max(0, dy), max(0, -dx):W - max(0, dx)]     # the "i" pixel
    b = q[max(0, dy):H - max(0, -dy), max(0, dx):W - max(0, -dx)]     # the "j" pixel, offset by (dx, dy)
    idx = a.ravel().astype(np.int64) * levels + b.ravel().astype(np.int64)
    return np.bincount(idx, minlength=levels * levels).reshape(levels, levels).astype(np.float64)

def haralick(mat):
    P = mat / mat.sum()
    L = P.shape[0]
    i, j = np.arange(L).reshape(-1, 1), np.arange(L).reshape(1, -1)
    contrast = float(np.sum((i - j) ** 2 * P))
    homogeneity = float(np.sum(P / (1 + (i - j) ** 2)))
    energy = float(np.sum(P ** 2))
    mu_i, mu_j = float(np.sum(i * P)), float(np.sum(j * P))
    sd_i, sd_j = float(np.sqrt(np.sum((i - mu_i) ** 2 * P))), float(np.sqrt(np.sum((j - mu_j) ** 2 * P)))
    correlation = float(np.sum((i - mu_i) * (j - mu_j) * P) / (sd_i * sd_j)) if sd_i > 0 and sd_j > 0 else 1.0
    entropy = float(-np.sum(P[P > 0] * np.log2(P[P > 0])))
    return dict(contrast=contrast, homogeneity=homogeneity, energy=energy, correlation=correlation, entropy=entropy)

strip = np.array([[0, 0, 170, 255]], dtype=np.uint8)            # quantizes to levels 0,0,2,3 at L=4 (Section 3)
print("by-hand check, contrast:", round(haralick(glcm_numpy(strip, 1, 0, levels=4))["contrast"], 3))

woven = cv2.imread("sample-texture-woven.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
for name, (dx, dy) in [("0 deg (horizontal)", (1, 0)), ("90 deg (vertical)", (0, 1))]:
    feats = haralick(glcm_numpy(woven, dx, dy))
    print(f"woven, {name}: contrast={feats['contrast']:.2f}  correlation={feats['correlation']:.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
by-hand check, contrast: 1.667
woven, 0 deg (horizontal): contrast=4.31  correlation=0.51
woven, 90 deg (vertical): contrast=1.58  correlation=0.82
```

### 2. The joint histogram is the GLCM: cv2.calcHist

A GLCM is a 2-D joint histogram of (pixel value, shifted-neighbour value) — exactly what `cv2.calcHist` computed for hue and saturation in 12.6, here computed for an image and a shifted copy of itself:

```python
def glcm_cv2(img, dx, dy, levels=LEVELS):
    q = quantize(img, levels).astype(np.float32)
    H, W = q.shape
    a = q[max(0, -dy):H - max(0, dy), max(0, -dx):W - max(0, dx)]
    b = q[max(0, dy):H - max(0, -dy), max(0, dx):W - max(0, -dx)]
    return cv2.calcHist([a, b], [0, 1], None, [levels, levels], [0, levels, 0, levels])

m1 = glcm_numpy(woven, 1, 0)
m2 = glcm_cv2(woven, 1, 0)
print("cv2.calcHist matches the NumPy GLCM:", np.allclose(m1, m2), " total pairs:", int(m2.sum()))
```

Output:

```text
cv2.calcHist matches the NumPy GLCM: True  total pairs: 5112
```

5112 = 72 × 71: one column fewer than the image width, because a horizontal pair needs a pixel to its right.

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2.GLCM(...)` or `cv2.graycomatrix(...)`** exists in OpenCV (the name `graycomatrix` is scikit-image's, a different, non-pinned library) — build the matrix with `cv2.calcHist` on the image and a shifted view of itself, as above.
- **`cv2.calcHist(images, channels, mask, histSize, ranges)`**: `images` is a *list* of arrays (here two single-channel `float32` arrays of the same shape, one shifted); `channels=[0,1]` takes channel 0 from each; `histSize=[levels,levels]`; `ranges=[0,levels,0,levels]`. Returns a `(levels, levels)` `float32` array (row = value from the first image, column = value from the second) — see 12.6 for the general 2-D form.
- **Quantization**: `cv2.calcHist`'s own `histSize`/`ranges` can bin raw 0–255 values directly (`ranges=[0,256,0,256]`, `histSize=[16,16]`) instead of quantizing first — both give the same 16-level matrix; quantizing first (as above) makes the Haralick formulas' grey-level indices explicit.
- **Speed**: computing the matrix is one `cv2.calcHist` call per direction; the Haralick statistics are a handful of elementwise sums over a 16×16 (or similar) array — negligible compared to almost anything else in this course.

## Where it fails

- **Choice of `L` (grey levels) and distance matter a lot**: too few levels (e.g. 4) loses detail; too many (e.g. 64+) on a small region gives a sparse, noisy matrix. There is no single correct choice — it depends on region size and the texture scale you care about.
- **A single direction can miss anisotropic texture** (Section 4) — compute several angles, or average them, for a texture whose orientation is unknown or irrelevant.
- **Still a hand-built statistic**, like LBP: it summarises *some* texture structure, not all of it, and two visually different textures can occasionally share similar Haralick numbers.
- **No position information**: like any one global matrix, it describes the whole region as one texture; a region straddling two different textures gives a GLCM that is a meaningless mixture of both (always compute it over a region you believe is one texture).
- For a principled, learned alternative, see CNN texture/feature classification in Part J.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Comparing GLCM features computed with different `L` or different distances**: contrast and the other features scale with the grey-level range and the pixel step; only compare like with like.
- **Computing one direction only** for a texture that might be directional, then being surprised the feature doesn't separate two classes — try multiple angles (Section 4).
- **Running GLCM across a region boundary** (e.g. half background, half object): the result describes neither region correctly.
- **Forgetting to normalise the matrix** (divide by its total) before computing the Haralick formulas — unnormalised counts give contrast/energy/entropy values that depend on region size, not just texture.
- **Confusing `cv2.calcHist`'s `ranges` upper bound**: it is *exclusive* like a NumPy slice (`ranges=[0, levels, ...]` covers values 0 to levels−1), matching the already-quantized 0..levels−1 range used here.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Haralick's original features

Haralick, Shanmugam and Dinstein's 1973 paper [1] defined 14 statistics from the GLCM, for classifying aerial photographs into terrain types; contrast, homogeneity (inverse difference moment), energy (angular second moment), correlation and entropy, used in Sections 4–5, are five of the most commonly used today. The remaining nine (sum/difference statistics, cluster shade and prominence, maximum probability, and others) are less used but defined the same way: a sum over the normalised matrix P(i,j).

### Distance and direction as parameters

A GLCM is defined for one (distance, direction) pair; multi-angle or rotation-invariant texture analysis typically averages or otherwise combines the GLCMs (or their features) from several directions (0°, 45°, 90°, 135° is a common minimal set) — exactly what Section 4's lab lets you explore by hand.

### GLCM vs LBP

Both are hand-built texture descriptors from the same era of image processing. LBP (35.1) encodes a *local* 3×3 neighbourhood pattern per pixel and histograms the codes; GLCM encodes *pairwise* grey-level co-occurrence over a whole region directly, without an intermediate per-pixel code. Both are still used as fast, interpretable baselines, often alongside or instead of CNN features (Part J) when data is scarce or interpretability matters.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A 1×4 strip, 4 grey levels: `3, 1, 3, 1`. Build the distance-1, direction-"right" GLCM (as a 4×4 table of counts) and compute its contrast.

<Solution>

Adjacent pairs: (3,1), (1,3), (3,1) — three pairs, two of them identical. The matrix has a 2 at (3,1) and a 1 at (1,3), zero elsewhere.
Normalised: P(3,1) = 2/3, P(1,3) = 1/3.
Contrast = (3−1)²·(2/3) + (1−3)²·(1/3) = 4·(2/3) + 4·(1/3) = 8/3 + 4/3 = **4**.

</Solution>

blotchy = cv2.imread("sample-texture-blotchy.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

Two completely different textures happen to have the same contrast, homogeneity and energy at 0°, distance 1. Does that mean a classifier using only these three numbers at this one (angle, distance) will always confuse them?

<Solution>

Very likely yes, for *that* classifier with *those* features — three numbers cannot separate two classes that produce the same three numbers. The fix is not to abandon GLCM but to add more features to the vector: other angles (Section 4), other distances (Exercise 2), correlation and entropy, or combine with a different descriptor entirely (LBP, 35.1, or a learned feature, Part J). A hand-built descriptor is only as good as the specific statistics chosen from it.

</Solution>
