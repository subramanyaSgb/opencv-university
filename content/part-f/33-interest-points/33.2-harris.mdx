# 33.2 Harris

<Definition term="Harris corner detector">a detector that scores every pixel with R = det(M) − k·trace(M)², where M is the structure tensor of the surrounding window: R is large and positive at corners, negative along edges and near zero in flat areas. Corners are the local maxima of R above a threshold.</Definition>

Chapter 33.1 showed that a corner is a point where the structure tensor M has two large eigenvalues. Harris and Stephens (1988) found a way to test this without computing the eigenvalues at all, using only the determinant and the trace of M. It is still one of the most used corner detectors.

## 1. An analogy

A table on four legs. Push it in one direction and it slides; push it into a corner of the room and it cannot move in any direction. Harris asks: "Is this pixel locked in, like the table in the corner, or can the window slide along something?"

## 2. The Harris score

<Flow
  steps={[
    { label: "Grey image", tone: "data" },
    { label: "Sobel gx, gy", tone: "process" },
    { label: "Products gx², gx·gy, gy² summed over blockSize × blockSize", tone: "process" },
    { label: "R = det(M) − k·trace(M)²", tone: "process" },
    { label: "Threshold + 3 × 3 local maxima → corners", tone: "result" },
  ]}
/>

With M = [[A, B], [B, C]] and its eigenvalues λ1, λ2:

```text
det(M)   = A·C − B²  = λ1 · λ2
trace(M) = A + C     = λ1 + λ2
R        = det(M) − k · trace(M)²          k ≈ 0.04 … 0.06
```

- **Corner**: both λ large → the product dominates → **R large positive**.
- **Edge**: one λ ≈ 0 → product ≈ 0, the trace term wins → **R negative**.
- **Flat**: both λ small → **R ≈ 0**.

No square roots, no eigen-decomposition: in 1988 that mattered, and it still makes Harris cheap.

Measured on the feature scene (blockSize 3, k 0.04, plain Sobel sums):

| Point | R |
| --- | --- |
| flat background | 2.0 × 10⁵ (≈ 0 on this scale) |
| edge of the box | **−7.4 × 10⁹** |
| corner of the box | **+2.7 × 10¹⁰** |

## 3. A worked example by hand

Use the windows of 33.1 and k = 0.04:

| Window | M | det | trace | R |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| flat | [[5, 0], [0, 5]] | 25 | 10 | 25 − 0.04 × 100 = **21** |
| edge | [[0, 0], [0, 300]] | 0 | 300 | 0 − 0.04 × 90 000 = **−3 600** |
| corner | [[300, 0], [0, 300]] | 90 000 | 600 | 90 000 − 0.04 × 360 000 = **75 600** |

What does k do? For an edge with eigenvalues 0 and λ, R = −k·λ². For a "weak corner" with eigenvalues λ1 = a·λ2, R > 0 only if a / (1 + a)² > k. With k = 0.04 that needs a > 0.043 (the smaller eigenvalue at least about 4 % of the larger); with k = 0.15, a > 0.23. **Larger k rejects more edge-like points**; smaller k accepts more corners, including rounded and weak ones.

## 4. From the response map to corner points

R is a map: one value per pixel. Around each corner, several neighbouring pixels have a high R. Two steps turn the map into points:

1. **Threshold**: keep pixels with R > q · max(R), typically q = 0.01. Relative to the maximum, because R's absolute scale depends on contrast and on OpenCV's scaling.
2. **Non-maximum suppression**: keep a pixel only if it is the largest in its 3 × 3 neighbourhood (compare R with `cv2.dilate(R)`).

On the feature scene: 751 pixels are above 1 % of the maximum, and 61 survive non-max suppression. The bright box gives exactly 8: its 4 outer corners and the 4 corners of the dark hole. The disc gives none, and the long line gives none in its middle (only at its ends).

## 5. Choosing the parameters

Measured on the two views (threshold 1 % of max, 3 × 3 non-max, found again within 2 px):

| blockSize | Corners in A | Found again in B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 92 | 91 of 91 (100 %) |
| 3 | 61 | 57 of 59 (97 %) |
| 5 | 83 | 62 of 81 (77 %) |
| 7 | 72 | 53 of 70 (76 %) |

On this sharp, synthetic scene small windows work best; larger windows merge neighbouring structures (checker squares, letter strokes) and their maxima move more between views. Real, noisier images usually need a somewhat larger window or a slight blur first.

k changed less: 61 to 71 corners and 87–97 % repeatability for k from 0.04 to 0.15. Because the threshold is relative to max(R), changing k also changes the threshold, so the number of corners does not simply fall as k rises.

## 6. Sub-pixel accuracy

Detected corners are whole pixels. For measurement and calibration (Module 41) that is not enough. `cv2.cornerSubPix` refines each corner iteratively: at the true corner, every gradient in the window is perpendicular to the vector from the corner to that pixel, and it solves for the point that best satisfies this.

The box was drawn with pixel columns 20 to 90 and rows 20 to 70, so its true outer corners lie on pixel boundaries: (19.5, 19.5), (90.5, 19.5), (19.5, 70.5), (90.5, 70.5).

| Detected | Refined | True |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (20, 20) | (19.60, 19.60) | (19.5, 19.5) |
| (90, 20) | (90.25, 19.61) | (90.5, 19.5) |
| (21, 70) | (19.60, 70.38) | (19.5, 70.5) |
| (90, 70) | (90.32, 70.39) | (90.5, 70.5) |

From up to 1.5 px off to within about 0.25 px.

## 7. Industrial use

- **Calibration targets**: checkerboard corners are found with Harris-like detectors and refined with `cornerSubPix` (`cv2.findChessboardCorners` + `cornerSubPix`, 41.4).
- **Alignment of parts and sheets** by their corners (sheet metal, glass, PCBs).
- **Tracking**: Harris or Shi–Tomasi corners fed to optical flow (Module 40).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** M = [[400, 0], [0, 10]], k = 0.05. Compute R. Corner or edge?

<Solution>

det = 4 000, trace = 410, R = 4 000 − 0.05 × 168 100 = 4 000 − 8 405 = **−4 405**. Negative: an **edge**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why threshold at a fraction of max(R) and not at a fixed number?

<Solution>

R grows with the fourth power of contrast (it is a product of two squared-gradient sums), and OpenCV scales it. A fixed number would fail as soon as the lighting or the image changes.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. Harris from scratch (NumPy + Sobel)

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-feat.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
f = img.astype(np.float64)
gx = cv2.Sobel(f, cv2.CV_64F, 1, 0, ksize=3)
gy = cv2.Sobel(f, cv2.CV_64F, 0, 1, ksize=3)
S = lambda a: cv2.boxFilter(a, -1, (3, 3), normalize=False)
A, B, C = S(gx * gx), S(gx * gy), S(gy * gy)
k = 0.04
R = A * C - B * B - k * (A + C) ** 2
for name, (x, y) in {"flat": (250, 120), "edge": (55, 20), "corner": (20, 20)}.items():
    print(f"{name:6s} R = {R[y, x]:.3e}")
ref = cv2.cornerHarris(img, 3, 3, k).astype(np.float64)
scale = (4 * 3 * 255) ** 4                                       # OpenCV scales each derivative by 1/(4·3·255)
print("max relative difference to cv2.cornerHarris:", round(float(np.abs(R / scale - ref).max() / np.abs(ref).max()), 8))
```

Output:

```text
flat   R = 2.016e+05
edge   R = -7.397e+09
corner R = 2.724e+10
max relative difference to cv2.cornerHarris: 3.1e-07
```

The only difference is OpenCV's scaling: for 8-bit input each derivative is divided by 2^(ksize−1) · blockSize · 255 = 4 · 3 · 255, and R contains products of four derivatives.

### 2. cv2.cornerHarris, threshold and non-max suppression

```python
R = cv2.cornerHarris(img, blockSize=3, ksize=3, k=0.04)
print("R: dtype", R.dtype, "min", f"{R.min():.2e}", "max", f"{R.max():.2e}")
strong = R > 0.01 * R.max()
peaks = strong & (R == cv2.dilate(R, None))                      # 3 × 3 non-maximum suppression
print("pixels above 1 % of max:", int(strong.sum()), " after non-max suppression:", int(peaks.sum()))
ys, xs = np.nonzero(peaks)
box = [(x, y) for x, y in zip(xs, ys) if 10 <= x <= 100 and 10 <= y <= 80]
print("corners on the bright box:", sorted((int(x), int(y)) for x, y in box))
```

Output:

```text
R: dtype float32 min -2.27e-03 max 5.36e-03
pixels above 1 % of max: 751  after non-max suppression: 61
corners on the bright box: [(20, 20), (21, 70), (40, 54), (41, 36), (70, 35), (70, 55), (90, 20), (90, 70)]
```

### 3. Sub-pixel refinement

- `winSize=(5, 5)`: half the search window (the window is 11 × 11).
- `zeroZone=(-1, -1)`: no dead zone in the middle.
- `criteria`: stop after 40 iterations or when the point moves less than 0.001 px.

```python
pts = np.array([[20, 20], [90, 20], [21, 70], [90, 70]], np.float32).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
crit = (cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_EPS + cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_MAX_ITER, 40, 0.001)
ref = cv2.cornerSubPix(img, pts.copy(), winSize=(5, 5), zeroZone=(-1, -1), criteria=crit)
for p, q in zip(pts.reshape(-1, 2), ref.reshape(-1, 2)):
    print(f"({p[0]:.0f}, {p[1]:.0f}) -> ({q[0]:.2f}, {q[1]:.2f})")
```

Output:

```text
(20, 20) -> (19.60, 19.60)
(90, 20) -> (90.25, 19.61)
(21, 70) -> (19.60, 70.38)
(90, 70) -> (90.32, 70.39)
```

`cornerSubPix` writes into the array it is given, hence `pts.copy()`.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.cornerHarris(src, blockSize, ksize, k[, dst[, borderType]])`**: `src` 8-bit or float32, single channel. Returns float32 R of the same size. `blockSize`: neighbourhood for M; `ksize`: Sobel aperture (3, 5, 7; −1 = Scharr); `k`: Harris free parameter. Border: `BORDER_DEFAULT` (reflect-101).
- **Scaling**: derivatives are divided by 2^(ksize−1) · blockSize, and additionally by 255 for 8-bit input. Use relative thresholds.
- **`cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack(..., useHarrisDetector=True, k=0.04)`** does threshold, non-max suppression, minimum distance and sorting for you (33.3).
- **`cv2.cornerSubPix(image, corners, winSize, zeroZone, criteria)`**: corners float32 N × 1 × 2, refined in place and returned.
- **Speed**: about the cost of three box filters plus two Sobels: fine for video at VGA on a CPU.

## Where it fails

- **Scale**: Harris is not scale invariant; a corner seen at a different zoom may lose its maximum or split into two. Use multi-scale detection (Harris–Laplace, SIFT 34.1).
- **Rounded corners** of radius larger than the window: no clear maximum.
- **Noise and texture**: many weak, unstable corners; raise the threshold, blur slightly, or use minDistance (33.3).
- **Clusters**: several strong corners close together (text, checkerboards) without a minimum distance.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Forgetting non-max suppression**: hundreds of "corners" in clumps instead of one per corner.
- **A fixed absolute threshold** (e.g. R > 1000): R's scale depends on contrast and OpenCV's normalisation.
- **Passing a colour image**: convert to grey first.
- **Swapping x and y**: `np.argwhere` and `np.nonzero` return (row, column) = (y, x).
- **Calling cornerSubPix on int arrays or without copying**: it needs float32 and modifies its input.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Why det − k·trace²

Harris and Stephens [1] wanted a measure that is large only when both eigenvalues are large, without computing them. det = λ1λ2 and trace = λ1 + λ2 are invariant to rotation of the image. With λ1 = a·λ2: R = λ2²·(a − k(1 + a)²), positive only for a above a k-dependent limit. Noble [2] proposed det / (trace + ε) instead, which needs no k.

### Rotation, lighting and scale

R is computed from rotation-invariant quantities of M, so Harris corners are rotation invariant up to discretisation. An additive brightness change does not change gradients; a gain g multiplies R by g⁴, which the relative threshold absorbs. Scale is the weak point: Mikolajczyk and Schmid's Harris–Laplace [3] detects Harris corners at several scales and keeps those where the normalised Laplacian is maximal over scale.

### How cornerSubPix works

For the true corner q and any pixel p in the window with gradient ∇I(p), either p lies in a flat area (∇I = 0) or on an edge through q, so ∇I(p) · (p − q) = 0. Summing over the window gives the linear system (Σ ∇I∇Iᵀ) q = Σ ∇I∇Iᵀ p, which is solved and iterated with the window re-centred on the new q. It is the same idea as Förstner's operator [4].

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

For which ratio a = λ1/λ2 is R exactly zero when k = 0.06?

<Solution>

Solve a = 0.06·(1 + a)²: 0.06a² − 0.88a + 0.06 = 0 → a = (0.88 − √(0.7744 − 0.0144)) / 0.12 = (0.88 − 0.8718) / 0.12 ≈ **0.068**. Corners need λ1 above about 7 % of λ2.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Count the Harris corners (threshold 1 % of max, 3 × 3 non-max) for k = 0.04, 0.06, 0.1 and 0.15, and for blockSize 2, 3, 5 and 7.

<Solution>

```python
def harris_points(im, block=3, k=0.04, q=0.01):
    R = cv2.cornerHarris(im, block, 3, k)
    return np.argwhere((R > q * R.max()) & (R == cv2.dilate(R, None)))
for k in (0.04, 0.06, 0.1, 0.15):
    print(f"k = {k:4}: {len(harris_points(img, k=k))} corners")
for block in (2, 3, 5, 7):
    print(f"blockSize {block}: {len(harris_points(img, block=block))} corners")
```

Output:

```text
k = 0.04: 61 corners
k = 0.06: 71 corners
k =  0.1: 68 corners
k = 0.15: 66 corners
blockSize 2: 92 corners
blockSize 3: 61 corners
blockSize 5: 83 corners
blockSize 7: 72 corners
```

The counts do not change monotonically: the threshold follows max(R), which itself changes with k and blockSize. Judge parameters by repeatability, not by count.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A camera inspects punched sheet-metal parts whose corners have a 3 mm radius at 0.2 mm per pixel. Will Harris with blockSize 3 find the corners reliably?

<Solution>

The radius is 15 px, much larger than a 3 × 3 window: along the arc the window sees an almost straight edge, so R stays low or negative. Use a larger window (or a downscaled image), or fit lines to the straight sides and intersect them (Module 26).

</Solution>
