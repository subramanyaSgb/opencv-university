# 36.8 Clustering

<Definition term="Clustering">grouping data by similarity alone, with no predefined classes and no labelled examples — the opposite setup from every other classifier in this module, which all needed labelled training data to learn from.</Definition>

36.1–36.7 all answered "which of these known categories does this point belong to?", learned from labelled examples. Clustering asks a different question: "does this data have any natural groups at all, and if so, what are they?" — with nobody telling the algorithm in advance how many groups to expect or what they represent.

## 1. An analogy

Being handed a box of unsorted buttons with no instructions, and asked to group them sensibly. Nobody told you the categories in advance ("4-hole," "2-hole," "by colour"); you look at the buttons, notice they fall into a few natural groups, and sort them that way. A different box of buttons might naturally fall into a different number of groups — clustering doesn't assume the answer, it looks for it.

## 2. Two ways to cluster

<Flow

    { label: "Unlabelled feature vectors", tone: "data" },

    { label: "or: agglomerative — merge the closest pair of clusters, repeat, until one remains", tone: "process" },

  ]}
/>

- **k-means** (27.2 covers the algorithm in full, there applied to pixel colours for segmentation) needs `k` chosen in advance, and gives one flat partition of the data.
- **Agglomerative (hierarchical) clustering** starts with every point as its own cluster, and repeatedly merges the two *closest* clusters, one merge at a time, until only one cluster remains — recording the whole merge history (a **dendrogram**) rather than committing to one specific number of clusters upfront. **Single linkage** defines the distance between two clusters as the distance between their closest pair of members (other linkage choices exist — complete, average — defining "cluster distance" differently).
- Neither method uses any labels. Both can be checked afterwards, if labels happen to be available (as in Section 4), to see whether the discovered groups match something meaningful — but nothing about the clustering process itself depends on that.

## 3. A worked example by hand: a dendrogram by single linkage

Five points on a line: 1, 2, 5, 6, 10. Starting with five separate clusters, merge the closest pair at each step (single linkage: closest pair of actual points, one from each cluster):

| Step | Merge | Distance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | [1] + [2] → [1, 2] | 1 |
| 2 | [5] + [6] → [5, 6] | 1 |
| 3 | [1, 2] + [5, 6] → [1, 2, 5, 6] | 3 (closest pair: 2 and 5) |
| 4 | [1, 2, 5, 6] + [10] → [1, 2, 5, 6, 10] | 4 (closest pair: 6 and 10) |

Reading the merge distances (1, 1, 3, 4) is reading the dendrogram: two small, early merges (1 and 2; 5 and 6) happen almost immediately (they're genuinely close), then a bigger jump (distance 3) to join those two little groups, then the biggest jump (distance 4) to bring in the outlier, 10. **Cutting** the dendrogram at any height gives a clustering at that level of coarseness — cutting between heights 1 and 3 gives three clusters ([1,2], [5,6], [10]); cutting above height 4 gives one cluster (everything).

## 4. Rediscovering known classes, and choosing k

<ClusterLab caption="k-means run with NO labels on the same texture tile feature space as 36.1-36.7. Toggle between the discovered clusters and the true classes -- and try different k."/>

Running k-means with k=3 on all 243 tile feature vectors (mean, std; 35.1's woven/smooth/blotchy), **using no labels at all**, then matching each discovered cluster to whichever true class is most common inside it:

| k | WCSS (total within-cluster distance²) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 193,760 |
| 2 | 122,124 |
| 3 | 82,913 |
| 4 | 55,777 |
| 5 | 44,034 |
| 6 | 32,455 |

WCSS always falls as k grows (more clusters can only fit the data at least as well) — the **elbow method** looks for where the drop slows down noticeably, suggesting extra clusters are giving diminishing returns rather than finding real structure. Here the drop from k=2→3 (39,211) is a smaller step than k=1→2 (71,636), and the steps keep shrinking gradually after that without one single dramatic bend — a reminder that real data doesn't always produce the textbook-clean elbow, even when (as here) the true number of groups is actually known to be 3.

At k=3 specifically, matching clusters to true classes gives **72.4% agreement** — far better than chance (33% for 3 balanced classes), but far from perfect. k-means, using only (mean, std) and no labels, correctly recovers most of the three textures' structure, but the known overlap between Woven and Blotchy (36.2, 36.4) costs real agreement — clustering is only as good as the features it's given, exactly 36.1's lesson, now with no labels to fall back on at all.

## 5. Industrial use

- **Exploring new data with no ground truth**: before any labelling effort, clustering can reveal whether a sensor's readings naturally fall into groups worth investigating.
- **Pre-labelling at scale**: cluster first, then have a person assign a real label to each cluster (far cheaper than labelling every single example individually) — a common practical shortcut.
- **Anomaly flagging**: a point far from every cluster centre (unlike anything already seen) is a candidate anomaly, without ever having been given examples of what "anomalous" looks like (55.6).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Three points: 2, 3, 9. Using single-linkage agglomerative clustering, what merges first, and at what distance?

<Solution>

Pairwise distances: |2−3|=1, |2−9|=7, |3−9|=6. The smallest is 1, so **2 and 3 merge first, at distance 1**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** A dataset's WCSS drops from 1000 (k=1) to 400 (k=2) to 390 (k=3) to 380 (k=4). Where is the elbow, and what does it suggest?

<Solution>

The huge drop is between k=1 and k=2 (600); after that, the drops are tiny (10, then 10). The elbow is at **k=2** — the data likely has two real groups, and further splitting barely improves the fit at all.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. The dendrogram, from scratch with NumPy

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

points = [1, 2, 5, 6, 10]
clusters = [[p] for p in points]

def cluster_dist(a, b):
    return min(abs(x - y) for x in a for y in b)

merges = []
while len(clusters) > 1:
    best = None
    for i in range(len(clusters)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(clusters)):
            d = cluster_dist(clusters[i], clusters[j])
            if best is None or d < best[0]:
                best = (d, i, j)
    d, i, j = best
    merges.append((d, clusters[i][:], clusters[j][:]))
    merged = clusters[i] + clusters[j]
    clusters = [c for idx, c in enumerate(clusters) if idx not in (i, j)] + [merged]

for d, a, b in merges:
    print(f"merge {a} + {b} at distance {d}")
```

Output:

```text
merge [1] + [2] at distance 1
merge [5] + [6] at distance 1
merge [1, 2] + [5, 6] at distance 3
merge [10] + [1, 2, 5, 6] at distance 4
```

### 2. k-means with no labels, and the elbow

```python
TILE = 8
def tile_features(img):
    h, w = img.shape
    feats = []
    for ty in range(h // TILE):
        for tx in range(w // TILE):
            patch = img[ty * TILE:(ty + 1) * TILE, tx * TILE:(tx + 1) * TILE].astype(np.float64)
            feats.append((patch.mean(), patch.std()))
    return np.array(feats)

names = ["woven", "smooth", "blotchy"]
classes = {n: tile_features(cv2.imread(f"sample-texture-{n}.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)) for n in names}
X = np.vstack([classes[n] for n in names]).astype(np.float32)
y_true = np.array(sum(([i] * len(classes[n]) for i, n in enumerate(names)), []), dtype=np.int32)

crit = (cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_EPS + cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_MAX_ITER, 100, 0.001)
cv2.setRNGSeed(42)
wcss, labels, centers = cv2.kmeans(X, 3, None, crit, 10, cv2.KMEANS_PP_CENTERS)      # k=3, NO y_true used here
labels = labels.ravel()

from collections import Counter
correct = 0
for c in range(3):
    mask = labels == c
    majority = Counter(y_true[mask]).most_common(1)[0][0]
    correct += (y_true[mask] == majority).sum()
print(f"k=3 agreement with true classes: {correct / len(y_true):.3f}")

for k in range(1, 7):
    cv2.setRNGSeed(42)
    w, _, _ = cv2.kmeans(X, k, None, crit, 10, cv2.KMEANS_PP_CENTERS)
    print(f"k={k}: WCSS={w:.0f}")
```

Output:

```text
k=3 agreement with true classes: 0.724
k=1: WCSS=193760
k=2: WCSS=122124
k=3: WCSS=82913
k=4: WCSS=55777
k=5: WCSS=44034
k=6: WCSS=32455
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.kmeans(data, K, bestLabels, criteria, attempts, flags)`**: see 27.2 for the full signature; `data` here is feature vectors (mean, std per tile) rather than pixel colours — the algorithm itself is identical, only what counts as a "point" has changed.
- **No agglomerative/hierarchical clustering function in core OpenCV** (confirmed: no `cv2.hierarchicalClustering` or similar exists) — Section 3's dendrogram is built entirely from scratch; a general-purpose library like `scipy.cluster.hierarchy` (outside this course's pinned OpenCV/NumPy stack) would be the practical choice for real hierarchical clustering work.
- **`labels` from `cv2.kmeans` are arbitrary cluster indices**, not aligned with any true class numbering — matching clusters to known labels (as in Section 4 and the Code section) always needs an explicit step like majority voting, done only for *checking*, never used by the clustering algorithm itself.
- **`cv2.setRNGSeed`** makes `cv2.kmeans`'s random centre initialisation reproducible, used above and in the lab so the same run can be checked and discussed consistently.

## Where it fails

- **k-means needs `k` chosen in advance**: the elbow method (Section 4) gives a rough guide, not a certain answer, and real data (as seen there) doesn't always produce an unambiguous elbow.
- **k-means assumes roughly round, similarly-sized clusters** (same limitation as 36.1's nearest-centroid, since k-means is essentially iterated nearest-centroid classification) — exactly why it only reached 72.4% agreement here, given Blotchy's much larger spread than the other two classes.
- **Agglomerative clustering's naive implementation is slow for large datasets** (Section 3's approach checks every pair of clusters at every step); real implementations use more efficient data structures, and the full dendrogram can still be expensive to compute and store for very large datasets.
- **Clustering can find structure that doesn't mean anything useful**: it is purely a function of the chosen features and distance measure, with no concept of what a human would consider a meaningful group — exactly why checking against known labels, when available, matters (Section 4).
- Superseded in many modern settings by learned embeddings (Part J) combined with clustering in that learned space, rather than clustering raw hand-built features directly.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Treating cluster indices as meaningful labels**: cluster 0 from one run and cluster 0 from a different run (or a different k) have no guaranteed relationship to each other or to any real category.
- **Picking k purely by the elbow method without sanity-checking the result**: Section 4's elbow was genuinely ambiguous; always look at the actual clusters found, not just the WCSS numbers.
- **Expecting clustering to achieve the same accuracy as a trained classifier** on the same data: Section 4's 72.4% (no labels) is naturally well below what a supervised classifier achieves on the same features (e.g. 36.2's Bayes classifier, with labels, agreed with nearest-centroid on the easy cases and made principled calls on the hard ones) — clustering is solving a strictly harder problem (find the groups *and* implicitly their boundaries) with strictly less information (no labels at all).
- **Running k-means once and trusting the result**: different random initialisations can converge to different local optima; `attempts` (running several times and keeping the best) and a fixed seed for reproducibility (OpenCV API notes above) both matter.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Other linkage criteria

Single linkage (Section 3, closest pair of points) can produce long, straggly "chains" of merges. **Complete linkage** (farthest pair of points between two clusters) and **average linkage** (mean distance between all cross-cluster pairs) are common alternatives that tend to produce more compact, evenly-sized clusters, at the cost of being somewhat more expensive to update as clusters merge.

### Silhouette score

An alternative to the elbow method for choosing k: for each point, compare its average distance to points in its own cluster against its average distance to points in the nearest other cluster; a score near +1 means a point fits its cluster well and is far from others, near 0 means it's on a boundary, negative means it may be in the wrong cluster. Averaging this over all points gives one number per choice of k, often clearer to interpret than an ambiguous WCSS elbow.

### DBSCAN and density-based clustering

k-means and agglomerative clustering both struggle with clusters of very different shapes or densities. Density-based methods (DBSCAN and relatives, not in `cv2`) group points that are densely packed together, and explicitly label sparse points as noise rather than forcing them into the nearest cluster regardless — a genuinely different approach from "which centre is nearest" or "which pair is closest."

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Four points: 10, 11, 20, 30. Build the single-linkage dendrogram (list each merge and its distance).

<Solution>

Pairwise distances: |10-11|=1, |10-20|=10, |10-30|=20, |11-20|=9, |11-30|=19, |20-30|=10.
Merge 1: [10]+[11] at distance 1 (smallest).
Remaining distances: [10,11] to [20] = min(10,9) = 9; [10,11] to [30] = min(20,19) = 19; [20] to [30] = 10.
Merge 2: [10,11]+[20] at distance 9.
Remaining: [10,11,20] to [30] = min(19, 10) = 10.
Merge 3: [10,11,20]+[30] at distance 10.

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A colleague has 10,000 unlabelled images of manufactured parts and wants to know how many distinct part types are in the batch, with no existing catalogue to check against. How would you approach this using this chapter's tools, and what would you still need to be careful about?

<Solution>

Extract features from each image (any of Module 35's descriptors, depending on what distinguishes the part types visually), then run k-means across a range of k values and use the elbow method (Section 4) to narrow down a plausible range for the number of distinct types, possibly combined with the silhouette score (Go deeper) for a clearer signal than WCSS alone. The caveats from this chapter still apply directly: the elbow may be ambiguous (Section 4's own real example), k-means assumes round, similarly-sized clusters which may not match how the real part types vary in appearance, and the final answer should be spot-checked by actually looking at a sample of images from each discovered cluster — clustering suggests structure, it does not certify it.

</Solution>
