# 34.1 SIFT

<Definition term="SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform)">a method by David Lowe (1999, 2004) that finds keypoints as extrema of a difference-of-Gaussian scale space, gives each one a position, a scale and an orientation, and describes the patch around it with 128 numbers (histograms of gradient directions) that stay nearly the same when the image is rotated, scaled or re-lit.</Definition>

The corner detectors of Module 33 tell you **where** a good point is, at one fixed scale. To find the same point in another photo, taken closer or at an angle, you need two more things: the point's **own scale and orientation**, so the comparison can be done in the point's own frame, and a **descriptor**, a fingerprint of its neighbourhood. SIFT provides all three. Its patent expired in March 2020, and since OpenCV 4.4 it is in the main module as `cv2.SIFT_create`.

## 1. An analogy

Two photos of the same poster, one taken from far away and tilted. To recognise a small sticker on it, you first zoom each photo so the sticker has the same size, then turn it so its "up" points the same way, and only then compare. SIFT does exactly this for every keypoint, automatically: it measures each point's size and direction and compares neighbourhoods in that normalised frame.

## 2. The four stages

<Flow
  steps={[
    { label: "Grey image", tone: "data" },
    { label: "1. Scale space: blur more and more, subtract neighbours (DoG); find extrema in x, y and scale", tone: "process" },
    { label: "2. Refine position and scale to sub-pixel; drop low-contrast and edge points", tone: "process" },
    { label: "3. Orientation: dominant gradient direction around the point", tone: "process" },
    { label: "4. Descriptor: 4 × 4 cells × 8 directions = 128 numbers, in the point's own frame", tone: "result" },
  ]}
/>

**1. Scale space.** The image is blurred with Gaussians of increasing σ (1.6, 1.6·2^(1/3), …). Subtracting neighbouring blur levels gives the **difference of Gaussians** (DoG, 20.4), a band-pass that responds to blobs of a particular size. After every doubling of σ the image is halved (an **octave**). A keypoint is a pixel larger (or smaller) than all 26 neighbours: 8 in its own layer, 9 above, 9 below. Its σ is its **scale**.

**2. Refinement.** A quadratic fit around the extremum gives sub-pixel position and scale. Points with weak DoG response (contrast threshold) are dropped, and so are points on edges (the ratio of the principal curvatures, the same idea as Harris, 33.2).

**3. Orientation.** Gradient directions in a window around the point (scaled with σ) are collected in a 36-bin histogram weighted by magnitude. The peak is the keypoint's angle; any other peak above 80 % of it creates a second keypoint with that angle.

**4. Descriptor.** A 16 × 16 grid (in units of the keypoint's scale, rotated to its angle) is split into 4 × 4 cells. Each cell gets an 8-bin histogram of gradient directions: 4 × 4 × 8 = **128 numbers**. The vector is normalised, values above 0.2 are clipped and it is normalised again, which reduces the effect of lighting.

Because everything is measured relative to the keypoint's own scale and angle, the descriptor is the same (nearly) whether the poster is near or far, upright or turned.

## 3. A worked example by hand: the orientation

Six gradients around a keypoint (direction, magnitude), with a simplified 8-bin histogram (bins of 45°, centred on 0°, 45°, 90°, …):

| Direction | Magnitude | Bin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10° | 4 | 0° |
| 40° | 9 | 45° |
| 50° | 7 | 45° |
| 95° | 3 | 90° |
| 200° | 2 | 180° |
| 48° | 5 | 45° |

Histogram: 0° → 4, 45° → 9 + 7 + 5 = **21**, 90° → 3, 180° → 2. The keypoint's orientation is **45°**: rotate the descriptor grid by 45° before building it. (Real SIFT uses 36 bins of 10°, Gaussian weights, smooths the histogram and interpolates the peak.)

Descriptor size: 4 × 4 cells × 8 directions = **128**, each stored as a value 0 … 255 in OpenCV.

## 4. On the poster pair

The poster and the same poster seen again, rotated by 25°, scaled to 0.75, in perspective and darker:

- SIFT finds **386** keypoints in view A and **214** in view B (B is smaller, so it has less detail).
- Keypoint sizes range from 1.8 to 26.6 px (median 2.7): many small blobs, a few large ones.
- Matching with the ratio test (34.4) gives 114 matches, of which **100** are correct.
- For these 100 correct pairs, the median size ratio B / A is **0.72** (true scale 0.75) and the median angle change is **21.8°** (true rotation 25°; the perspective part shifts it a little).

The keypoints have "followed" the scale and rotation: that is scale and rotation invariance in practice.

## 5. Industrial use

- **Object recognition from one example**: recognising a product, a part or a document type from a reference image (34.4, 34.5).
- **Panoramas and mosaics** of large parts, walls, roads (34.6).
- **3D reconstruction** and camera pose (Modules 43 and 44): SIFT is still a standard front end of structure-from-motion tools such as COLMAP.
- **Where speed matters** (video, embedded), ORB or AKAZE usually replace it (34.2, 34.3).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A keypoint has σ = 3.2 in an octave where the image was halved once. How large is the corresponding structure in the original image?

<Solution>

σ in the original image = 3.2 × 2 = **6.4** px. (OpenCV's `size` is the diameter of the described region, a few times σ.)

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why is the SIFT descriptor insensitive to adding a constant to every pixel, and to multiplying every pixel by a gain?

<Solution>

It uses only **gradients**, which do not change when a constant is added. A gain change scales all gradients equally and is removed by the normalisation of the 128-vector.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. A difference-of-Gaussian octave from scratch

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-poster.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE).astype(np.float32)
sigma0, k = 1.6, 2 ** (1 / 3)
blurred = [cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (0, 0), sigma0 * k ** i) for i in range(5)]     # one octave, 5 scales
dog = np.stack([blurred[i + 1] - blurred[i] for i in range(4)])                   # 4 difference-of-Gaussian layers
print("DoG stack:", dog.shape, " value range", round(float(dog.min()), 1), "to", round(float(dog.max()), 1))
count = 0
for s in (1, 2):                                                                  # inner layers only
    for y in range(1, dog.shape[1] - 1):
        for x in range(1, dog.shape[2] - 1):
            v = dog[s, y, x]
            cube = dog[s - 1:s + 2, y - 1:y + 2, x - 1:x + 2]
            if abs(v) > 3 and (v == cube.max() or v == cube.min()):
                count += 1
print("scale-space extrema with |DoG| > 3 in the first octave:", count)
```

Output:

```text
DoG stack: (4, 240, 320)  value range -17.2 to 19.0
scale-space extrema with |DoG| > 3 in the first octave: 104
```

Real SIFT adds the doubled first octave, further octaves, sub-pixel refinement, the edge test and the descriptor; the idea is the same.

### 2. cv2.SIFT_create

```python
a = cv2.imread("sample-poster.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
b = cv2.imread("sample-poster-b.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
sift = cv2.SIFT_create()
ka, da = sift.detectAndCompute(a, None)
kb, db = sift.detectAndCompute(b, None)
print("keypoints:", len(ka), len(kb), " descriptors:", da.shape, da.dtype)
k0 = max(ka, key=lambda k: k.response)
print(f"strongest: pt=({k0.pt[0]:.1f}, {k0.pt[1]:.1f}) size={k0.size:.1f} angle={k0.angle:.1f} octave={k0.octave & 255}")
sizes = np.array([k.size for k in ka])
print("sizes: min", round(float(sizes.min()), 1), " median", round(float(np.median(sizes)), 1), " max", round(float(sizes.max()), 1))
```

Output:

```text
keypoints: 386 214  descriptors: (386, 128) float32
strongest: pt=(89.7, 229.6) size=1.9 angle=71.7 octave=255
sizes: min 1.8  median 2.7  max 26.6
```

`octave & 255` = 255 means octave −1: by default SIFT first doubles the image, and its smallest keypoints come from that enlarged copy.

### 3. Do keypoints follow scale and rotation?

```python
c, s = 0.75 * np.cos(np.radians(25)), 0.75 * np.sin(np.radians(25))
H = np.array([[c, -s, 95], [s, c, -10], [0.0004, -0.0003, 1.0]])         # true mapping A -> B
pairs = []
for m, n in cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_L2).knnMatch(da, db, k=2):
    if m.distance < 0.75 * n.distance:
        p = cv2.perspectiveTransform(np.array([[ka[m.queryIdx].pt]]), H).ravel()
        if np.hypot(*(p - kb[m.trainIdx].pt)) <= 3:
            pairs.append((ka[m.queryIdx], kb[m.trainIdx]))
scale = np.median([q.size / p.size for p, q in pairs])
turn = np.median([((q.angle - p.angle + 180) % 360) - 180 for p, q in pairs])
print(len(pairs), "correct matches; median size ratio B/A", round(float(scale), 2), "; median angle change", round(float(turn), 1), "deg")
```

Output:

```text
100 correct matches; median size ratio B/A 0.72 ; median angle change 21.8 deg
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.SIFT_create(nfeatures=0, nOctaveLayers=3, contrastThreshold=0.04, edgeThreshold=10, sigma=1.6)`**: `nfeatures` 0 = keep all, otherwise the best N by response. Lower `contrastThreshold` for more (weaker) points; higher `edgeThreshold` keeps more edge-like points.
- **`detect`, `compute`, `detectAndCompute(image, mask)`**: 8-bit grey input; descriptors float32 N × 128 (values 0–255). An optional `descriptorType=cv2.CV_8U` gives uint8 descriptors.
- **`cv2.KeyPoint`**: `pt` (x, y), `size` (diameter of the meaningful neighbourhood), `angle` (degrees, 0–360, measured in image coordinates), `response`, `octave` (packed: low byte octave, next byte layer), `class_id`.
- **`cv2.drawKeypoints(img, kp, None, flags=cv2.DRAW_MATCHES_FLAGS_DRAW_RICH_KEYPOINTS)`** draws size and orientation.
- **Speed**: tens of milliseconds for a VGA image on one CPU core; much slower than ORB.

## Where it fails

- **Strong viewpoint changes** (beyond about 40–50° out-of-plane): the patch is deformed, not just rotated and scaled. Use affine-adapted detectors (ASIFT) or learned features.
- **Repetitive patterns**: many identical descriptors; matching becomes ambiguous (34.4).
- **Textureless or specular surfaces**: few keypoints.
- **Real-time on small hardware**: too slow; use ORB (34.2).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Using `cv2.xfeatures2d.SIFT_create`**: since OpenCV 4.4 SIFT is in the main module as `cv2.SIFT_create`.
- **Matching SIFT with Hamming distance**: SIFT descriptors are float vectors: use `NORM_L2`.
- **Reading `octave` directly**: it is packed; mask with `& 255` and interpret 255 as −1.
- **Expecting the same number of keypoints in both images**: different content, size and contrast give different counts.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Why difference of Gaussians

The scale-normalised Laplacian σ²∇²G is the operator whose extrema over scale select a characteristic size (Lindeberg [3]). The DoG G(kσ) − G(σ) ≈ (k − 1)σ²∇²G, so the DoG pyramid approximates it almost for free, from blurred images that are needed anyway.

### Keypoint refinement

Lowe [2] fits a 3-D quadratic to the DoG values around the extremum (Taylor expansion in x, y, σ) and moves the keypoint to its peak; points with |D| at the peak below 0.03 (with values in 0–1) are rejected, and so are points where the ratio of principal curvatures of D (from the 2 × 2 Hessian) exceeds 10, which removes edge responses.

### RootSIFT

Arandjelović and Zisserman [4] showed that comparing SIFT descriptors with the Hellinger kernel works better than Euclidean distance: L1-normalise each descriptor, take the element-wise square root, then use L2 as usual. It is a two-line change that improves retrieval and matching.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

SIFT uses 3 layers per octave (k = 2^(1/3)) and σ0 = 1.6. What are the σ values of the 5 blurred images of one octave? Which two DoG layers can contain keypoints?

<Solution>

1.6, 2.02, 2.54, 3.2, 4.03 (each × 1.26). Four DoG layers result; extrema need a layer above and below, so only the **2 inner** DoG layers (Lowe uses s + 3 = 6 blurred images to get 3 usable layers; with 5 images there are 2).

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A robot must recognise boxes in a warehouse from one reference photo each; the boxes appear at different distances and turned on the shelf. Why is SIFT a better start than template matching?

<Solution>

Template matching needs the same scale and orientation (32.4). SIFT keypoints carry their own scale and angle, so descriptors compare in a normalised frame; a geometric check (34.5) then confirms the box. Plain template matching would need a search over many scales and angles.

</Solution>
