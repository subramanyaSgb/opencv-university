# 34.6 Stitching

<Definition term="Image stitching">combining overlapping images into one larger image (a panorama or mosaic): find matching features in the overlap, estimate the transformation between the images, warp them into a common frame, equalise their brightness and blend the overlap so no seam is visible.</Definition>

Stitching puts everything of this module together. It is also an everyday industrial task: one camera cannot see a 2 m steel sheet at 0.1 mm per pixel, but six overlapping shots can, if they are joined precisely.

## 1. An analogy

Laying overlapping photographs of a long wall on a table: you slide and turn each new print until the shared part lines up, then tape it down. If one print is darker, the join shows; you would lighten it and soften the edge so the eye does not notice the transition.

## 2. The pipeline

- **Model**: a homography is exact for a plane (a sheet, a wall, a floor) or for a camera that only rotates. For long scans, a camera moving along a flat object gives an **affine** model (OpenCV's SCANS mode).
- **Canvas**: transform the corners of each image to find the panorama's size.
- **Gain compensation**: the overlap is the same scene, so its brightness should match; scale each image by a gain that equalises the overlap means.
- **Blending**: overwrite (hard seam), average (ghosting if alignment is imperfect), **feathering** (weights that fall towards each image's border), or multi-band blending (20.3) for the best results.

## 3. A worked example by hand: canvas and feather weights

The right image (300 px wide) maps into the left image's frame with its corners at x = 144, 446, 442 and 132. The left image covers x = 0 … 299. The panorama must be **446 px** wide, and the overlap is about x = 132 … 299.

At a pixel in the overlap at x = 250 (row in the middle), the distances to the borders are:

| Image | Distance to its nearest border | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| left (border at x = 299) | 49 | 49 / (49 + 106) = 0.32 |
| right (border at x ≈ 144) | 106 | 106 / 155 = 0.68 |

With left = 120 and right (after gain) = 128: blended = 0.32 × 120 + 0.68 × 128 = **125.4**. Moving right, the left weight falls to 0 at x = 299, so there is no jump at the left image's edge.

## 4. Two views of a poster

The left view and a right view that is shifted by about 140 px, slightly rotated and in perspective, darker (gain 0.9, +18) and with its own noise:

- ORB with ratio test: **25** matches, **24** RANSAC inliers. The mean corner error against the true transformation is **2.2 px**: usable, but a few more matches spread over the overlap would make it tighter.
- In the overlap, the mean grey levels are 125.2 (left) and 130.8 (right). **Gain compensation** (0.958) cuts the mean difference from 6.47 to 4.49 grey levels.
- At the left image's edge (x = 300) a **hard paste** jumps by 7.01 grey levels on average; **feathering** reduces it to 2.58.
- `cv2.Stitcher` with all of this built in returns a 470 × 224 panorama (PANORAMA mode) or 428 × 223 (SCANS mode).

## 5. Industrial use

- **Large parts at high resolution**: sheets, coils, PCBs, castings imaged in tiles by a moving camera or stage, stitched for inspection.
- **Road, rail and tunnel inspection**: continuous mosaics from vehicle-mounted cameras (often with line-scan cameras, 59.7, and encoder-based positioning instead of features).
- **Microscopy and wafer inspection**: tile stitching with stage positions as a first guess and feature/phase correlation (21.2) to refine.
- **Panoramic site monitoring** from a rotating camera.

When the stage position is known precisely (encoder, motion controller), use it; features then only correct small errors. Feature-based stitching is for when the geometry is not known.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Ten images are stitched in a row, each homography with 0.5 px error at the far edge. What can happen at the end of the row?

<Solution>

Errors **accumulate**: the 10th image can be several pixels off, and a closed loop (360° panorama) will not close. Real stitchers solve all homographies jointly (bundle adjustment) instead of chaining them.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why does averaging cause "ghosting" while feathering mostly does not?

<Solution>

Averaging gives both images equal weight everywhere in the overlap, so any misalignment shows as a double image across the whole overlap. Feathering gives one image most of the weight near its centre, so double edges appear only in a narrow transition zone.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. Stitching by hand: matches and homography

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

left = cv2.imread("sample-pano-left.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
right = cv2.imread("sample-pano-right.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
orb = cv2.ORB_create(1500)
kl, dl = orb.detectAndCompute(left, None)
kr, dr = orb.detectAndCompute(right, None)
good = [m for m, n in cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_HAMMING).knnMatch(dr, dl, k=2) if m.distance < 0.75 * n.distance]
src = np.float32([kr[m.queryIdx].pt for m in good]).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
dst = np.float32([kl[m.trainIdx].pt for m in good]).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
cv2.setRNGSeed(1)
H, mask = cv2.findHomography(src, dst, cv2.RANSAC, 3.0)             # right image -> left image's frame
print(len(good), "matches,", int(mask.sum()), "inliers")
Hr = np.array([[0.98, 0.04, -140], [-0.03, 0.99, 6], [0.0, 0.00006, 1.0]])   # how the right view was made
H_true = np.linalg.inv(Hr)
corners = np.float32([[0, 0], [300, 0], [300, 220], [0, 220]]).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
print("mean corner error:", round(float(np.linalg.norm(cv2.perspectiveTransform(corners, H) - cv2.perspectiveTransform(corners, H_true), axis=2).mean()), 2), "px")
right_corners = cv2.perspectiveTransform(corners, H).reshape(-1, 2)
width = int(np.ceil(right_corners[:, 0].max()))
print("panorama width:", width, " right image's corners in the left frame:", np.round(right_corners).astype(int).tolist())
```

Output:

```text
25 matches, 24 inliers
mean corner error: 2.22 px
panorama width: 446  right image's corners in the left frame: [[144, -2], [446, 9], [442, 231], [132, 224]]
```

The right image's top-left corner lands at y = −2: a real stitcher would also extend the canvas upward (or translate everything down); here the 2 px are simply cut.

### 2. Warp, gain compensation and feathering

```python
width = 446
canvas_r = cv2.warpPerspective(right.astype(np.float32), H, (width, 220))
mask_r = cv2.warpPerspective(np.ones_like(right, np.float32), H, (width, 220)) > 0.999
canvas_l = np.zeros((220, width), np.float32)
canvas_l[:, :300] = left
mask_l = np.zeros((220, width), bool)
mask_l[:, :300] = True
overlap = mask_l & mask_r
gain = canvas_l[overlap].mean() / canvas_r[overlap].mean()
print("overlap", int(overlap.sum()), "px; mean left", round(float(canvas_l[overlap].mean()), 1), " mean right", round(float(canvas_r[overlap].mean()), 1), " gain", round(float(gain), 3))
for g, name in [(1.0, "no gain compensation"), (gain, "with gain compensation")]:
    print(f"{name}: mean |left - right| in the overlap = {np.abs(canvas_l - canvas_r * g)[overlap].mean():.2f}")
wl = cv2.distanceTransform(mask_l.astype(np.uint8), cv2.DIST_L2, 3)     # feather weights: distance to the border
wr = cv2.distanceTransform(mask_r.astype(np.uint8), cv2.DIST_L2, 3)
pano = (canvas_l * wl + canvas_r * gain * wr) / np.maximum(wl + wr, 1e-6)
pano = np.clip(np.rint(pano), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)
hard = np.where(mask_l, canvas_l, canvas_r).astype(np.float32)          # left image wins, right fills the rest
rows = mask_r[:, 300]                                                    # rows where both sides of x = 300 exist
for name, im in [("hard paste", hard), ("feathered", pano.astype(np.float32))]:
    print(f"{name:10s}: mean jump across x = 300 (edge of the left image): {np.abs(im[rows, 300] - im[rows, 299]).mean():.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
overlap 35255 px; mean left 125.2  mean right 130.8  gain 0.958
no gain compensation: mean |left - right| in the overlap = 6.47
with gain compensation: mean |left - right| in the overlap = 4.49
hard paste: mean jump across x = 300 (edge of the left image): 7.01
feathered : mean jump across x = 300 (edge of the left image): 2.58
```

The remaining 4.49 grey levels in the overlap come from noise (σ 3 in each image), the right image's interpolation and the 2 px alignment error.

### 3. cv2.Stitcher

```python
left_c = cv2.imread("sample-pano-left.png")                        # Stitcher expects colour (BGR) images
right_c = cv2.imread("sample-pano-right.png")
for name, mode in [("PANORAMA", cv2.Stitcher_PANORAMA), ("SCANS", cv2.Stitcher_SCANS)]:
    cv2.setRNGSeed(1)                                             # the Stitcher's RANSAC uses OpenCV's random generator
    st = cv2.Stitcher_create(mode)
    status, pano = st.stitch([left_c, right_c])
    print(name, "status", status, "(0 = OK)", None if pano is None else pano.shape)
```

Output:

```text
PANORAMA status 0 (0 = OK) (224, 470, 3)
SCANS status 0 (0 = OK) (223, 428, 3)
```

PANORAMA assumes a rotating camera and projects onto a sphere (the result is wider and slightly curved); SCANS uses an affine model on a plane, which fits a flat poster better.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.Stitcher_create(mode=cv2.Stitcher_PANORAMA)`**: `PANORAMA` (rotating camera, spherical warper, homographies, bundle adjustment of rotations, exposure compensation, graph-cut seams, multi-band blending) or `SCANS` (affine model, plane warper; for flat documents and scanned surfaces).
- **`status, pano = stitcher.stitch(images[, masks])`**: `status` 0 = `Stitcher_OK`, 1 = `ERR_NEED_MORE_IMGS` (not enough confident matches between images), 2 = `ERR_HOMOGRAPHY_EST_FAIL`, 3 = `ERR_CAMERA_PARAMS_ADJUST_FAIL`. `pano` is `None` unless status is 0.
- **Settings**: `setPanoConfidenceThresh` (lower to accept weaker image links, default 1.0), `setRegistrationResol`, `setCompositingResol`, `setWaveCorrection`.
- **Building blocks** in `cv2.detail` (warpers, exposure compensators, seam finders, blenders) for custom pipelines.
- **Manual pipeline**: `findHomography` + `warpPerspective` + your own blending, as in the code above; `cv2.distanceTransform` gives feather weights.

## Where it fails

- **Parallax**: a moving (not only rotating) camera in front of a 3D scene; objects at different depths cannot all align, causing ghosts or breaks.
- **Small or featureless overlaps**: too few matches (as when this pair had only 100 px of overlap: 18 matches and a 17 px error).
- **Moving objects** in the overlap: ghosts; seam finding routes the seam around them.
- **Accumulated drift** in long sequences without global optimisation.
- **Lens distortion**: undistort first (41.3), or the images bend against each other.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Too little overlap**: plan 30–50 % overlap between neighbouring shots.
- **Not checking `status`**: `pano` is `None` on failure.
- **Chaining pairwise homographies over long sequences** without global adjustment.
- **Hard pasting** images with different exposure: always compensate gain and blend.
- **Using PANORAMA mode for flat scans** taken by a translating camera; use SCANS (affine).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Automatic panoramas

Brown and Lowe [1] built a fully automatic pipeline: SIFT matches between all image pairs, a probabilistic check of which pairs really overlap, bundle adjustment of all camera rotations and focal lengths together, gain compensation by least squares over all overlaps, and multi-band blending. OpenCV's Stitcher follows this design.

### Seam finding

Instead of blending a wide overlap, a seam can be cut where the two images agree best, found by dynamic programming or graph cuts (Kwatra et al. [3]); objects that moved between the shots are then kept from one image only. OpenCV offers `GraphCutSeamFinder` and `DpSeamFinder`.

### Multi-band blending

Burt and Adelson's Laplacian-pyramid blending (20.3) blends low frequencies over a wide region and high frequencies over a narrow one, avoiding both visible seams and blurred detail [2].

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Shots of a 1 200 mm long part, each covering 300 mm, with 30 % overlap between neighbours. How many shots are needed?

<Solution>

Each new shot adds 300 × 0.7 = 210 mm. After the first 300 mm, 900 mm remain: 900 / 210 = 4.3 → 5 more shots: **6 shots** in total.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A robot camera on a linear axis photographs a flat 2 m steel plate in 8 tiles. The axis encoder gives the position to ±0.05 mm. Should you stitch with features?

<Solution>

Use the encoder positions as the primary geometry (they are more reliable than features on a low-texture plate) and refine only small residual offsets, for example with phase correlation in the overlaps (21.2). Pure feature-based stitching risks failing on bare metal and drifting over 8 tiles.

</Solution>
