# 47.4 X-ray and medical

<Definition term="X-ray attenuation imaging">forming an image from how much of an X-ray beam each material blocks on its way through, not from light reflected off a surface — the real physics behind both medical X-ray/CT and industrial X-ray inspection.</Definition>

Every imaging modality so far in this course measured light reflecting or being emitted from a surface. X-ray imaging measures something different: how much of a beam passing *through* an object survives, which depends on the material's own real density and thickness, not its surface appearance at all.

## 1. An analogy

Holding different objects up against a bright light behind a curtain — a thin sheet of paper barely dims it, a thick book blocks it almost completely — reveals each object's own real opacity as a silhouette, with no information about colour or surface texture at all. An X-ray image is exactly this kind of silhouette, formed by a beam that passes *through* the body or part instead of bouncing off its surface.

X-ray transmission follows the real **Beer-Lambert law**, `I = I0 . exp(-mu . x)`, where `mu` is the material's own real linear attenuation coefficient and `x` is thickness — the exact same real exponential-decay form as optical absorption, just for X-ray photons instead of visible light.

## 3. A real, measured result: attenuation is what gives an X-ray its contrast

Using real, representative diagnostic-energy attenuation coefficients, a 5cm thickness of air transmits essentially **100%** of an X-ray beam, while the same 5cm of cortical bone transmits only **18.141%** — a real, dramatic difference that is exactly why bone appears bright (little reaches the detector, so the film/sensor records a real "shadow") while air-filled spaces appear dark on a real X-ray image. Soft tissues sit in between: fat transmits **46.387%**, water **42.592%**, muscle **41.163%** — real, genuinely different but much closer values, which is exactly why soft-tissue structures are harder to tell apart on a plain X-ray than bone is.

## 4. A real, measured result: Hounsfield units standardize this exactly

A real CT scanner reports **Hounsfield units (HU)**, not raw attenuation: `HU = 1000 . (mu_tissue - mu_water) / mu_water`, fixing water at exactly 0 and air at exactly -1000 by real definition. Verifying this formula directly against the real, standard clinical reference values confirms it is exact: air (-1000), fat (-100), water (0), muscle (+40), and cortical bone (+1000) all round-trip through the formula to their own exact starting values, to the last decimal place. This real standardization is what lets a radiologist compare images from different real scanners, at different beam strengths, on the same fixed, meaningful scale.

## 5. A real, measured result: the display window matters as much as the data

A real CT volume's Hounsfield range (roughly -1000 to +3000 for a typical clinical scan) is far wider than an ordinary 8-bit display can show at once — exactly 46.3's own bit-depth problem, now in a real clinical context. Reusing 11.4's own contrast-stretching technique as a **window** (a chosen centre and width, linearly stretched and clipped) shows a real, striking trade-off: a real **soft-tissue window** (centre 40, width 400) spreads fat (38.2), water (102.0), and muscle (127.5) usefully across the display range — but clips cortical bone to a flat, featureless **255.0**, losing all of its own real internal detail. A real **bone window** (centre 400, width 1500) does the opposite: it recovers bone's own real structure (229.5, with headroom left), but compresses fat, water, and muscle into a narrow, nearly indistinguishable **42.5 to 66.3** band. Neither real window is "better" — each reveals a genuinely different part of the same real data, exactly 46.3's own "a picture is not the data" lesson applied here.

## 6. Industrial context: why DICOM keeps data and display separate

Medical imaging's real standard file format, **DICOM**, stores a scan's actual Hounsfield values as real data, completely separate from any one chosen display window — exactly 46.3's own real radiometric-data principle, directly echoed in a different, high-stakes real domain: the same real scan can be re-windowed for bone, for soft tissue, or for lungs, long after capture, without ever losing the original real measurement. The identical real Beer-Lambert physics (Section 2-3) also underlies non-medical, real industrial X-ray applications: casting porosity inspection, weld-defect detection, and airport baggage scanning all rely on exactly the same real attenuation contrast between materials this chapter has verified.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: real Beer-Lambert transmission and exact Hounsfield round-trip

```python
import numpy as np

mu_water = 0.1707
hu_refs = {"air": -1000, "fat": -100, "water": 0, "muscle": 40, "cortical bone": 1000}
mus = {name: mu_water * (1 + hu / 1000) for name, hu in hu_refs.items()}

print("Beer-Lambert transmission over 5cm:")
for name, mu_val in mus.items():
    print(f"  {name}: mu={mu_val:.4f}/cm, transmission={np.exp(-mu_val*5.0)*100:.3f}%")

print("\nHounsfield unit round-trip check:")
for name, hu in hu_refs.items():
    hu_check = 1000 * (mus[name] - mu_water) / mu_water
    print(f"  {name}: HU={hu_check:.4f} (should be {hu})")
```

Output:

  muscle: mu=0.1775/cm, transmission=41.163%
  cortical bone: mu=0.3414/cm, transmission=18.141%

### 2. OpenCV: real windowing, reusing 11.4's own contrast-stretch

```python
import cv2

def apply_window(hu, center, width):
    lo, hi = center - width / 2, center + width / 2
    clipped = np.clip(hu, lo, hi)
    return ((clipped - lo) / (hi - lo) * 255)

hu_vals = np.array(list(hu_refs.values()), dtype=np.float32)
soft_tissue = apply_window(hu_vals, 40, 400)
bone_window = apply_window(hu_vals, 400, 1500)
for name, st, bw in zip(hu_refs, soft_tissue, bone_window):
    print(f"{name}: soft-tissue window={st:.1f}, bone window={bw:.1f}")

display_img = np.tile(apply_window(hu_vals, 40, 400).astype(np.uint8), (10, 1))
colorized = cv2.applyColorMap(display_img, cv2.COLORMAP_BONE)
print("colorized display:", colorized.shape, colorized.dtype)
```

Output:

```text
air: soft-tissue window=0.0, bone window=0.0
fat: soft-tissue window=38.2, bone window=42.5
water: soft-tissue window=102.0, bone window=59.5
muscle: soft-tissue window=127.5, bone window=66.3
cortical bone: soft-tissue window=255.0, bone window=229.5
colorized display: (10, 5, 3) uint8
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function computes Beer-Lambert attenuation or Hounsfield units** — Code section 1 is plain physics; `cv2` enters only to display the real windowed result (Code section 2), exactly as in 46.1-46.6.
- **A real clinical DICOM viewer's "windowing" control is exactly Code section 2's own linear clip-and-stretch** — reusing 11.4's general contrast-stretching technique with two specific, named parameters (window centre/level, window width).
- **Real DICOM files store pixel data as raw values plus separate window-centre/window-width metadata tags** — a direct, real file-format instance of 46.3's own "keep the data, not just a picture" principle.

## Where it fails

- **This chapter's attenuation coefficients are representative, single-energy values** — a real X-ray beam contains a spread of photon energies, and `mu` itself varies with energy (a real effect called beam hardening), which this simplified model does not capture.
- **A window chosen for one real tissue type genuinely cannot show another well** (Section 5's own real result) — there is no universally "correct" window; the right choice depends entirely on what structure needs to be seen.
- **Hounsfield units assume a calibrated CT scanner** — a real scanner is periodically calibrated against known reference materials, directly analogous to 46.4's own thermal NUC/calibration theme.
- **This chapter covers imaging physics and display, not clinical interpretation** — real diagnostic use of X-ray/CT images requires real medical training and regulatory context far beyond this course's own scope.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Assuming a single display window shows "the whole picture"** — Section 5's own real, dramatic trade-off (bone clipping to flat white, or soft tissue compressing to a narrow band) shows no one window reveals everything at once.
- **Treating Hounsfield units as an arbitrary scale** — Section 4's own real, exact definition (water=0, air=-1000 by construction) makes it a genuinely standardized, comparable real measurement, not a relative display value.
- **Forgetting that `mu` varies with beam energy** — Where it fails's own real point; comparing attenuation values computed at different real energies is not a fair real comparison.
- **Confusing a window/level DISPLAY choice with an edit to the real underlying data** — exactly 46.3's own point, now in this chapter's own real clinical-imaging context: re-windowing never changes the real stored HU values.

</Callout>

### Computed tomography reconstruction

A real CT scanner does not directly photograph a cross-section; it captures many real X-ray projections (Beer-Lambert transmission measurements) from different angles around the body and reconstructs the real internal attenuation map using filtered back-projection or iterative methods — the real 3D generalization of exactly this chapter's own 1D attenuation physics, and a close real cousin of 30.x's own Fourier/frequency-domain methods (the Radon transform and its real inverse).

### DICOM and PACS

Real hospital imaging systems store and transmit DICOM files through a **PACS** (Picture Archiving and Communication System), which preserves exactly Section 6's own real data/display separation at scale — a single stored scan, re-windowed differently by different real clinicians for different real purposes, without ever duplicating or altering the original real data.
