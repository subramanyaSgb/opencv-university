# 47.2 Polarization

<Definition term="Polarization imaging">capturing which direction light's own electric field oscillates, not just how bright it is — real information an ordinary camera discards completely, but a polarizing filter (or several, at different angles) can recover.</Definition>

Every camera chapter so far measured only light's real intensity. Light also has a real, physical oscillation direction — its polarization — and this chapter covers how to capture it, and the real, often dramatic information it reveals that intensity alone cannot.

## 1. An analogy

Polarized sunglasses cut glare off a wet road or a lake's surface specifically, while leaving the rest of the scene largely unaffected — not a generic darkening, a real, *selective* removal of light that reflected off that surface in one particular real orientation. A machine-vision polarization filter does the exact same real physics, just placed in front of a camera instead of an eye, and this chapter makes that physics exact and quantitative.

A polarizing filter transmits light according to the real, exact **Malus's law**: `I = I0 . cos^2(theta)`, where `theta` is the angle between the filter's own axis and the incoming light's real polarization direction. Capturing a scene through a filter at four real angles (0°, 45°, 90°, 135° — exactly what a real polarization camera's per-pixel micro-polarizer array does) gives enough real information to recover the scene's own **Stokes parameters**, and from them its real degree and angle of linear polarization, at every pixel.

## 3. A real, measured result: the exact math every polarization camera runs

Capturing a known synthetic polarization state (degree of polarization 0.6, angle 35°, total intensity 200) through filters at 0°/45°/90°/135° gives real intensities of **120.52, 156.38, 79.48, 43.62**. Running the real, standard recovery formulas — `S0 = (I0+I45+I90+I135)/2`, `S1 = I0-I90`, `S2 = I45-I135`, `DoLP = sqrt(S1^2+S2^2)/S0`, `AoP = 0.5 . atan2(S2, S1)` — recovers the exact original values: **S0=200.0000**, **DoLP=0.600000**, **AoP=35.0000°**. This is the real, exact computation inside every commercial polarization camera, from 46.3's own kind of raw data through to a usable, quantitative image.

## 4. A real, measured, dramatic finding: why glare vanishes at one exact angle

Light reflecting off a real dielectric surface (water, glass, wet pavement) becomes genuinely polarized according to the **Fresnel equations** — and at one specific real angle, the **Brewster angle**, the component polarized *parallel* to the surface (`Rp`) drops to **exactly zero**. For air reflecting off water (`n=1.33`), that angle is a real **53.06°**: at that exact angle, `Rp = 0.000000` while the perpendicular component `Rs` stays a real, nonzero **0.0771** — meaning the reflected glare at that angle is **completely, genuinely polarized**, and a filter blocking that one real polarization direction removes it entirely, while ordinary diffuse light (largely unpolarized) passes through mostly unaffected.

## 5. Industrial context: real uses beyond glare removal

A real polarization camera reveals genuine information no ordinary camera can: **shape from polarization** recovers real surface orientation from how reflected light's polarization angle (`AoP`) varies across a curved surface; **material discrimination** separates metals (which polarize reflected light differently from dielectrics) from plastics or coatings on an otherwise visually similar production line; **scratch and crack detection** on shiny metal parts exploits the fact that a scratch changes local surface orientation, and therefore local `AoP`, well before it is visible in ordinary intensity; and **through-glass or through-water inspection** uses Section 4's own exact Brewster-angle physics to remove surface reflections a plain camera cannot avoid.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: Malus's law and the real Stokes/DoLP/AoP round trip

```python
import numpy as np

I0 = 100.0
for theta_deg in [0, 30, 45, 60, 90]:
    I = I0 * np.cos(np.radians(theta_deg)) ** 2
    print(f"theta={theta_deg}deg: I={I:.2f}")

def synth_intensities(S0, DoLP, AoP_deg):
    AoP = np.radians(AoP_deg)
    S1, S2 = DoLP * S0 * np.cos(2 * AoP), DoLP * S0 * np.sin(2 * AoP)
    I_at = lambda a: 0.5 * (S0 + S1 * np.cos(2 * np.radians(a)) + S2 * np.sin(2 * np.radians(a)))
    return I_at(0), I_at(45), I_at(90), I_at(135)

def recover_dolp_aop(I0_, I45, I90, I135):
    S0 = (I0_ + I45 + I90 + I135) / 2
    S1, S2 = I0_ - I90, I45 - I135
    return S0, np.sqrt(S1**2 + S2**2) / S0, 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(S2, S1))

I0_, I45, I90, I135 = synth_intensities(200.0, 0.6, 35.0)
print(f"synthetic intensities: {I0_:.2f}, {I45:.2f}, {I90:.2f}, {I135:.2f}")
S0_r, DoLP_r, AoP_r = recover_dolp_aop(I0_, I45, I90, I135)
print(f"recovered: S0={S0_r:.4f}, DoLP={DoLP_r:.6f}, AoP={AoP_r:.4f}deg")
```

Output:

```text
theta=60deg: I=25.00
theta=90deg: I=0.00
synthetic intensities: 120.52, 156.38, 79.48, 43.62
recovered: S0=200.0000, DoLP=0.600000, AoP=35.0000deg
```

### 2. OpenCV: the real Fresnel/Brewster-angle computation, visualized

```python
import cv2

n1, n2 = 1.0, 1.33

def fresnel_Rs_Rp(theta_i_deg, n1, n2):
    ti = np.radians(theta_i_deg)
    tt = np.arcsin(np.clip(n1 / n2 * np.sin(ti), -1, 1))
    Rs = ((n1 * np.cos(ti) - n2 * np.cos(tt)) / (n1 * np.cos(ti) + n2 * np.cos(tt))) ** 2
    Rp = ((n1 * np.cos(tt) - n2 * np.cos(ti)) / (n1 * np.cos(tt) + n2 * np.cos(ti))) ** 2
    return Rs, Rp

brewster_deg = np.degrees(np.arctan(n2 / n1))
angles = np.linspace(0, 85, 256)
Rs_curve, Rp_curve = fresnel_Rs_Rp(angles, n1, n2)
curve_img = np.tile(((1 - Rp_curve) * 255).astype(np.uint8), (30, 1))
heat = cv2.applyColorMap(curve_img, cv2.COLORMAP_BONE)
print(f"Brewster's angle: {brewster_deg:.2f} deg")
print(f"Rp at Brewster's angle: {fresnel_Rs_Rp(brewster_deg, n1, n2)[1]:.6f}")
print("transmission-through-filter heatmap:", heat.shape, heat.dtype)
```

Output:

```text
Brewster's angle: 53.06 deg
Rp at Brewster's angle: 0.000000
transmission-through-filter heatmap: (30, 256, 3) uint8
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function computes Stokes parameters or Fresnel reflectance** — this chapter's real physics (Code section 1-2) is plain NumPy; `cv2` enters only for ordinary image display once a polarization map has been computed.
- **A real polarization camera typically delivers four raw sub-images (one per micro-polarizer angle) in a single sensor read**, directly analogous to 2.7's own Bayer-filter spatial multiplexing — this chapter's `I0, I45, I90, I135` are exactly what such a sensor outputs per pixel, before Code section 1's own recovery formulas run.
- **`cv2.applyColorMap`** (Code section 2, reusing 46.6's own real safety lesson) is a reasonable way to visualize a computed DoLP or AoP map for a human, with the identical real caveats about palette choice from 46.6.

## Where it fails

- **A per-pixel micro-polarizer array trades spatial resolution for polarization information** — exactly 2.7's own Bayer-filter trade-off, now applied to polarization angles instead of colour channels.
- **Metals reflect light differently from dielectrics** — the real Fresnel equations this chapter used (Code section 2) assume a dielectric surface (like water or glass); a metal surface's own complex refractive index changes the real relationship, though metals vs dielectrics can still often be told apart by their different real polarization signatures.
- **Glare removal is strongest only near Brewster's angle** — Mini exercise's own real point: a filter helps far less at near-normal viewing angles, where reflected light is barely polarized at all.
- **This chapter assumed linear polarization only** — real circular/elliptical polarization (common in some scattering and biological-imaging contexts) needs additional real Stokes parameters (`S3`) beyond this chapter's own linear-only treatment.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Expecting a polarizing filter to remove all glare, at any viewing angle** — Section 4-5's own real result shows the effect is angle-dependent, strongest exactly at Brewster's angle.
- **Treating a DoLP or AoP map as just another colourized intensity image** — it is real, independent information (Section 3), not a re-coloured version of the same brightness data.
- **Forgetting the real resolution trade-off of a micro-polarizer sensor** — Where it fails's own real point, directly analogous to 2.7's Bayer-filter trade-off.
- **Assuming the Stokes/DoLP math (Section 3) needs anything beyond four real filtered intensities** — the recovery formulas are exact and simple; no iterative fitting or calibration curve is required for the core computation itself.

</Callout>

### Shape from polarization

A curved dielectric surface's real angle of polarization at each pixel is a direct, computable function of the real local surface normal (Atkinson, Hancock, "Recovery of Surface Orientation from Diffuse Polarization," IEEE Transactions on Image Processing, 2006) — a real, independent route to 44.1-style surface shape that needs no stereo baseline or structured light at all, just a single polarization camera.

### Mueller matrices

A full, general treatment of how any real optical element transforms a beam's complete polarization state uses a real 4x4 Mueller matrix acting on the full Stokes vector `(S0, S1, S2, S3)`; this chapter's Malus's law and Fresnel equations are specific, simpler real cases of that same, more general real framework.

</GoDeeper>
