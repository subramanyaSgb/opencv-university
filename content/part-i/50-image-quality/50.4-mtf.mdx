# 50.4 MTF

<Definition term="MTF (modulation transfer function)">how much of a scene's real contrast survives at each spatial frequency, from the coarsest stripes (low frequency) to the finest (high frequency) — the real, standard way to turn 19.3's own PSF into a single curve that says how sharp a lens, sensor or whole imaging chain actually is.</Definition>

19.3 already defined the point spread function (PSF), the edge spread function (ESF), and the line spread function (LSF), and said directly: measuring a tilted edge's width is the standard way to measure MTF. This chapter does exactly that, for real, on a synthetic edge with a known PSF, and checks the real result against the one case where the true answer is known exactly: a Gaussian blur.

## 1. An analogy

Imagine printing bar charts with stripes of different widths and asking a photocopier to reproduce each one. Wide, widely-spaced stripes copy almost perfectly — the copier can tell black from white with full contrast. Pack the stripes closer together (a higher spatial frequency) and the copy starts to grey out: black and white blur into the same mid-grey, and the copier has lost the ability to show that contrast at all. The MTF is the real curve of "how much contrast survives" plotted against how closely packed the stripes are.

Why tilt the edge at all? A single row of pixels only samples the edge at whatever sub-pixel phase that row happens to land on — one sample per pixel, at one offset. Tilt the edge by a few degrees and each row crosses the edge at a slightly different sub-pixel offset, so stacking many rows together builds up a single, finely-sampled ESF far denser than any one row could give alone. Section 5 shows, for real, what happens when the edge is not tilted.

## 3. A real worked check: does the measured MTF match the known answer?

A Gaussian PSF is the one case with an exact, closed-form MTF: MTF(f) = exp(−2π²σ²f²), where f is spatial frequency in cycles per pixel and σ is the PSF's standard deviation in pixels. Build a synthetic 100×100 image of a step edge tilted 5°, blur it with `cv2.GaussianBlur` at a *known* σ = 1.2 px, run the real slanted-edge pipeline above, and compare:

| frequency (c/px) | measured MTF | analytic MTF |
|---|---|---|
| 0.051 | 0.9296 | 0.9301 |
| 0.101 | 0.7467 | 0.7483 |
| 0.202 | 0.3104 | 0.3135 |
| 0.303 | 0.0721 | 0.0735 |
| 0.404 | 0.0092 | 0.0097 |

The measured curve, built entirely from real, binned pixel values with no knowledge of σ fed into the measurement itself, tracks the true analytic curve to within about 0.003 at every frequency tested — the slanted-edge method genuinely recovers the real MTF of a known blur.

## 4. MTF50: one real number instead of a whole curve

A full MTF curve is precise but inconvenient to compare at a glance, so systems often report **MTF50**: the frequency where MTF drops to 0.5. For a Gaussian PSF this has its own closed form, f₅₀ = √(ln 2 / (2π²σ²)), so it can be checked the same way:

```text
sigma=0.8: measured MTF50=0.2339 c/px, analytic=0.2342 c/px
sigma=1.2: measured MTF50=0.1559 c/px, analytic=0.1562 c/px
sigma=1.6: measured MTF50=0.1178 c/px, analytic=0.1171 c/px
sigma=2.0: measured MTF50=0.0934 c/px, analytic=0.0937 c/px
```

Larger blur (bigger σ) gives a *lower* MTF50 — exactly the real, intuitive relationship: a blurrier PSF loses contrast at lower spatial frequencies, so it cannot resolve as fine a detail.

## 5. A real, honest failure: why the edge really must be tilted

Section 2 asserted that tilting the edge matters. Here is what actually happens, measured directly, with no tilt at all (angle = 0°) against the same edge tilted 5°, both at σ = 1.2:

```text
angle=0.0: MTF at f=0.202 = inf (179/199 ESF bins empty, populated bin count=100)
angle=5.0: MTF at f=0.202 = 0.3104 (0/199 ESF bins empty, populated bin count=11)
```

With no tilt, every one of the 100 rows crosses the edge at the *identical* sub-pixel offset, so the supersampled ESF (binned every 0.1 px) only ever receives samples at one bin in ten — 179 of its 199 bins are completely empty. The result is not just a noisier curve: the empty bins create an artificially oscillating ESF whose derivative (the LSF) has a near-zero net sum, and dividing the measured MTF by that near-zero DC term produces an outright `inf`. Tilting by 5° spreads the 100 rows' sub-pixel offsets across the full range between adjacent pixels, so every bin gets real data (0 empty) and the measurement matches the analytic curve exactly as in Section 3. This is a genuine, reproducible numerical breakdown, not a contrived example — it is the real reason every slanted-edge standard insists on a tilted edge.

## 6. Industrial context: MTF as a spec, and why MTFs multiply

A lens datasheet's MTF curve, a sensor's own pixel-aperture MTF, and any softening from the digital processing chain each blur the image a little. 2.3 already stated the rule without deriving it: **the MTFs of cascaded, independent stages multiply** at each frequency (because convolving PSFs in space is the same as multiplying their Fourier transforms — the real reason 19.3's LSF → FFT route works at all). A vision system speccing "must resolve a 0.2 mm defect" is really specifying a minimum combined MTF at the spatial frequency that defect corresponds to, and a lens, sensor or focus error that drags the *combined* MTF below that point is a real, measurable failure the system shipped without ever running this exact slanted-edge measurement itself.

## Code

### 1. NumPy + OpenCV: the real slanted-edge pipeline, checked against the known analytic MTF

`xx >= edge_x` builds a sharp step tilted by `angle_deg`; `cv2.GaussianBlur` applies the one real PSF whose MTF is known exactly, so the measurement can be checked. `np.digitize` + `np.add.at` bin every pixel by its real sub-pixel offset from the edge into a supersampled ESF; `np.diff` gives the LSF; `np.fft.rfft` gives the real MTF, normalized by its own zero-frequency term.

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

H, W = 100, 100
angle_deg = 5.0
sigma_true = 1.2

yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(np.float64)
slope = np.tan(np.radians(angle_deg))
edge_x = W / 2 + slope * (yy - H / 2)
sharp_edge = np.where(xx >= edge_x, 255.0, 0.0)

k = int(sigma_true * 8) | 1
blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(sharp_edge, (k, k), sigma_true)

offsets = xx - edge_x
bin_width = 0.1
bins = np.arange(-10, 10, bin_width)
esf = np.zeros(len(bins) - 1)
counts = np.zeros(len(bins) - 1)
idx = np.digitize(offsets.ravel(), bins) - 1
valid = (idx >= 0) & (idx < len(esf))
np.add.at(esf, idx[valid], blurred.ravel()[valid])
np.add.at(counts, idx[valid], 1)
esf = esf / np.maximum(counts, 1)

lsf = np.diff(esf) / bin_width
MTF = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(lsf))
MTF = MTF / MTF[0]
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(lsf), d=bin_width)


def analytic_mtf(f, sigma):
    return np.exp(-2 * np.pi**2 * sigma**2 * f**2)


for f_test in [0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]:
    i = np.argmin(np.abs(freqs - f_test))
    print(f"f={freqs[i]:.3f} c/px: measured={MTF[i]:.4f}, analytic={analytic_mtf(freqs[i], sigma_true):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
f=0.051 c/px: measured=0.9296, analytic=0.9301
f=0.101 c/px: measured=0.7467, analytic=0.7483
f=0.202 c/px: measured=0.3104, analytic=0.3135
f=0.303 c/px: measured=0.0721, analytic=0.0735
f=0.404 c/px: measured=0.0092, analytic=0.0097
```

### 2. OpenCV: MTF50 across several real PSF blurs

`below = np.where(MTF2 < 0.5)[0][0]` finds the first frequency bin where the curve has dropped under 0.5, then linear interpolation between that bin and the one before it gives a sub-bin estimate of MTF50, since the true crossing rarely falls exactly on a sampled frequency.

```python
def slanted_edge_mtf(angle, sigma, H=100, W=100, bin_width=0.1):
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(np.float64)
    slope = np.tan(np.radians(angle))
    edge_x = W / 2 + slope * (yy - H / 2)
    sharp = np.where(xx >= edge_x, 255.0, 0.0)
    k = int(sigma * 8) | 1
    blur = cv2.GaussianBlur(sharp, (k, k), sigma) if sigma > 0 else sharp
    offsets = xx - edge_x
    bins = np.arange(-10, 10, bin_width)
    esf = np.zeros(len(bins) - 1)
    counts = np.zeros(len(bins) - 1)
    idx = np.digitize(offsets.ravel(), bins) - 1
    valid = (idx >= 0) & (idx < len(esf))
    np.add.at(esf, idx[valid], blur.ravel()[valid])
    np.add.at(counts, idx[valid], 1)
    esf = esf / np.maximum(counts, 1)
    lsf = np.diff(esf) / bin_width
    mtf = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(lsf))
    mtf = mtf / mtf[0]
    freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(lsf), d=bin_width)
    return freqs, mtf, counts


def mtf50_freq(sigma):
    return np.sqrt(np.log(2) / (2 * np.pi**2 * sigma**2))


for sigma in [0.8, 1.2, 1.6, 2.0]:
    freqs, MTF2, _ = slanted_edge_mtf(5.0, sigma)
    below = np.where(MTF2 < 0.5)[0][0]
    f1, f2 = freqs[below - 1], freqs[below]
    m1, m2 = MTF2[below - 1], MTF2[below]
    f_measured = f1 + (0.5 - m1) * (f2 - f1) / (m2 - m1)
    print(f"sigma={sigma}: measured MTF50={f_measured:.4f} c/px, analytic={mtf50_freq(sigma):.4f} c/px")
```

Output:

```text
sigma=0.8: measured MTF50=0.2339 c/px, analytic=0.2342 c/px
sigma=1.2: measured MTF50=0.1559 c/px, analytic=0.1562 c/px
sigma=1.6: measured MTF50=0.1178 c/px, analytic=0.1171 c/px
sigma=2.0: measured MTF50=0.0934 c/px, analytic=0.0937 c/px
```

### 3. Why the edge must be tilted: a real, measured breakdown at angle = 0

`np.errstate` only silences the warning printed to stderr; the real division by (near) zero still happens and the result is still `inf`, exactly as it would in production code that was not watching for this failure mode.

```python
with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
    for angle in [0.0, 5.0]:
        freqs, MTF3, counts = slanted_edge_mtf(angle, 1.2)
        i = np.argmin(np.abs(freqs - 0.2))
        populated = counts[counts > 0]
        print(f"angle={angle}: MTF at f={freqs[i]:.3f} = {MTF3[i]:.4f} "
              f"({int((counts == 0).sum())}/{len(counts)} ESF bins empty, "
              f"populated bin count={int(populated[0])})")
```

Output:

```text
angle=0.0: MTF at f=0.202 = inf (179/199 ESF bins empty, populated bin count=100)
angle=5.0: MTF at f=0.202 = 0.3104 (0/199 ESF bins empty, populated bin count=11)
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (k, k), sigma)`** (already covered in earlier modules) is used here only as a *known* PSF to blur a synthetic sharp edge with, so the slanted-edge measurement has a real, exact answer to be checked against.
- **`np.digitize`** (Code sections 1–2) assigns each real pixel offset to a bin index; combined with `np.add.at` it builds the supersampled ESF histogram without an explicit Python loop over pixels.
- **`np.fft.rfft`** (Code sections 1–2) is the real one-sided FFT for a real-valued signal (the LSF); dividing by its own zero-frequency term, `MTF[0]`, normalizes the curve to 1.0 at f = 0, as an MTF curve must be.
- No dedicated `cv2` function computes MTF or the slanted-edge method directly in this OpenCV build; the pipeline above (binning, `np.diff`, `np.fft.rfft`) is the real, standard way to get it from NumPy and OpenCV's own blur/image primitives.

## Where it fails

- **An untilted (or too-weakly-tilted) edge can break the measurement outright** — Section 5's own real result, a genuine divide-by-zero from an aliased, under-sampled ESF, not merely extra noise.
- **Real sensor noise was not modelled in this chapter's synthetic edge** — a real photographed edge has shot and read noise (48.4, 49.1–49.3) on top of the PSF blur, which a real MTF measurement must average over many edge samples to suppress.
- **The method measures MTF along one direction only** (perpendicular to the edge) — a real lens or sensor can have a different MTF horizontally and vertically (astigmatism, rolling shutter, Bayer demosaicing artifacts), so a full characterization needs edges in more than one orientation.
- **A Gaussian PSF has a convenient closed form; most real PSFs do not** — real lens PSFs (diffraction, aberrations) have no simple analytic MTF, which is precisely why the slanted-edge method is used on real hardware instead of trusting a formula.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Photographing an edge with no tilt at all** — Section 5's own real, measured breakdown; ISO 12233 (Go deeper) specifies a tilt range for exactly this reason.
- **Forgetting to normalize the MTF curve to 1.0 at f = 0** — an unnormalized `|DFT(LSF)|` curve cannot be compared to another measurement or to a datasheet value.
- **Confusing MTF with the sharpness metrics of 50.3** — 50.3's focus metrics are single numbers meant to *rank* images by sharpness for autofocus; MTF is a full curve describing *how much contrast survives at each spatial frequency*, a fundamentally different, more complete characterization.
- **Multiplying MTF curves that are not actually independent** — Section 6's cascading rule assumes the stages' blurs are statistically independent; a lens and a sensor usually are, but two stages sharing the same underlying cause (e.g., the same motion blur appearing in two processing steps) are not, and must not be multiplied as if they were.

</Callout>

### ISO 12233:2017 and the real slanted-edge standard

ISO 12233:2017 (already cited in 19.3) is the real, standard specification for the slanted-edge method used across the photographic and machine-vision industries: it specifies the edge tilt range, the minimum number of edge rows, and how to report the resulting spatial frequency response (SFR) curve — of which this chapter's own measurement is a simplified, directly verified implementation of the core idea.

### MTF is the magnitude of the OTF

The full, complex-valued Fourier transform of the PSF is the optical transfer function (OTF); its magnitude is the MTF, and its phase (the phase transfer function, PTF) describes any real spatial shift the system introduces at each frequency — not measured in this chapter, which (like most practical slanted-edge work) reports only the magnitude.

### Why MTFs really do multiply

Convolving two PSFs in the spatial domain (what happens when light passes through a lens and then a sensor's own pixel aperture) corresponds, by the real convolution theorem, to multiplying their Fourier transforms — which is exactly 2.3's own "MTFs of the parts multiply" rule, and the same real theorem that lets 19.3's LSF → FFT route recover the MTF directly from one edge.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

Based on Section 5's own real result, would a 1° tilt (instead of 5°) be safer or riskier than no tilt at all, and why?

<Solution>

Safer than zero tilt, but still risky. Any nonzero tilt spreads the rows' sub-pixel offsets across more of the gap between pixels than zero tilt does (which repeats the identical offset on every row), so it would avoid the complete, bin-starved breakdown Section 5 measured. But a 1° tilt spreads those offsets far less evenly than 5° does, so many ESF bins would still be thinly populated or empty, giving a measurement that is noisier and less reliable than the chapter's own 5° result — which is exactly why ISO 12233 (Go deeper) specifies a real, practical tilt range rather than leaving it arbitrary.

</Solution>

### Exercise 4 (coding)

Using Code section 1's pipeline, measure the MTF at f ≈ 0.15 c/px for σ = 1.2 and σ = 2.4 (double the blur), and check whether doubling sigma roughly squares the MTF value at that frequency (since the analytic form has sigma squared inside the exponent).

<Solution>

```python
for sig in [1.2, 2.4]:
    freqs5, MTF5, _ = slanted_edge_mtf(5.0, sig)
    i = np.argmin(np.abs(freqs5 - 0.15))
    print(f"sigma={sig}: MTF at f={freqs5[i]:.3f} = {MTF5[i]:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
sigma=1.2: MTF at f=0.152 = 0.5180
sigma=2.4: MTF at f=0.152 = 0.0721
```

0.5180² = 0.2683, not 0.0721 — doubling sigma does *not* roughly square the MTF value. The real reason is in the exponent: MTF(f, 2σ) = exp(−2π²(2σ)²f²) = exp(−2π²σ²f²)⁴ = MTF(f, σ)⁴, a *fourth* power, not a square, since σ appears squared inside the exponent and doubling it multiplies that squared term by 4. Checking: 0.5180⁴ = 0.0720, matching the measured 0.0721 almost exactly — doubling the blur sigma collapses the MTF at a fixed frequency far faster than a simple square would suggest.

</Solution>
