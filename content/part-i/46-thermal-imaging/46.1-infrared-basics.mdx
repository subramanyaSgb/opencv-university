# 46.1 Infrared basics

<Definition term="Thermal (infrared) imaging">forming an image from the infrared radiation a scene emits because of its own temperature — not from visible light it reflects, which is what every camera in this course up to now has used.</Definition>

Every camera chapter before this one (2.x onward) assumed a scene reflects light from some external source toward the lens. A thermal camera needs no external light at all: every object above absolute zero continuously radiates its own real electromagnetic energy, and a thermal camera simply detects a slice of that self-emitted spectrum.

## 1. An analogy

A stove burner glows visibly red once it gets hot enough — everyone has seen this. A person's hand, or a warm engine block, is also radiating energy the entire time, continuously, exactly the same real physical process — it is simply too cool for that radiation's peak to fall inside the narrow band of wavelengths human eyes can see. A thermal camera is a sensor built to see exactly that same real radiation, just shifted further into the infrared than the stove burner's visible glow.

## 2. The pipeline: temperature in, a detectable spectrum out

Planck's law gives the real spectral radiance any object at temperature `T` emits at each wavelength; Wien's displacement law (`lambda_peak = b / T`, with `b = 2.897771955 x 10^-3 m.K`, a real physical constant) gives the one wavelength where that spectrum is brightest. A thermal camera's detector is simply built to be sensitive over whichever real wavelength band matters for the temperatures it is meant to see.

## 3. A real, measured result: why thermal cameras use the band they do

<InfraredLab caption="Live, exact Planck's law: drag the temperature and watch the real peak wavelength shift between LWIR, MWIR and SWIR." />

| Scene | Temperature | Peak wavelength (Wien) | Total radiated power (Stefan-Boltzmann) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Room background | 20°C (293.15K) | 9.88 µm | 418.8 W/m² |
| Human skin | 33°C (306.15K) | 9.47 µm | 498.1 W/m² |
| Hot machine surface | 80°C (353.15K) | 8.21 µm | 882.0 W/m² |
| Molten metal | 800°C (1073.15K) | 2.70 µm | 75,206.2 W/m² |

Room-temperature and body-temperature scenes both peak real close to **9-10 µm** — squarely inside the real **LWIR** band (8-14 µm, "long-wave infrared"), which is exactly why uncooled microbolometer cameras built for building inspection, predictive maintenance, and body-temperature screening are tuned to that specific real wavelength range. Molten metal's peak shifts dramatically, to **2.70 µm** — inside the real **SWIR/MWIR** range (1-5 µm) — which is why furnace and casting-line cameras use a genuinely different detector technology tuned to that much shorter real wavelength.

## 4. A real, measured surprise: the real contrast is small

Comparing real skin radiance to real room-background radiance at a single representative LWIR wavelength (10 µm) gives a ratio of only **1.2338** — skin is just **23%** brighter than its background at that wavelength, a real, genuinely small contrast. This is why a thermal camera's detector needs real, careful sensitivity engineering (its **NETD**, noise-equivalent temperature difference, typically a real fraction of a degree) to turn this small a real physical difference into a usable image — nothing like the often much larger brightness contrasts an ordinary visible-light camera sees between a lit object and shadow.

## 5. Industrial context: picking the right band for the job

21.4's own real example — an electrical cabinet, where a thermal camera finds an overheating joint near ambient temperature — needs exactly Section 3's LWIR band; a steel mill watching a 1000°C casting process needs the shorter-wavelength MWIR/SWIR band instead, because Section 3's own real numbers show the emission has shifted that far by then. Choosing the wrong band for a given real temperature range (Go deeper) genuinely degrades or defeats a thermal measurement, not just its image quality.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: Planck's law, Wien's law, and Stefan-Boltzmann, for real

```python
import numpy as np

h = 6.62607015e-34    # Planck constant, J.s
c = 2.99792458e8       # speed of light, m/s
kB = 1.380649e-23      # Boltzmann constant, J/K
sigma = 5.670374419e-8 # Stefan-Boltzmann constant, W/(m^2.K^4)
WIEN_B = 2.897771955e-3  # Wien's displacement constant, m.K

def planck_spectral_radiance(wavelength_m, T):
    return (2 * h * c**2) / (wavelength_m**5 * (np.exp(h * c / (wavelength_m * kB * T)) - 1))

def wien_peak_wavelength(T):
    return WIEN_B / T

temps = {"room (20C)": 293.15, "skin (33C)": 306.15, "hot machine (80C)": 353.15, "molten metal (800C)": 1073.15}
for label, T in temps.items():
    peak_um = wien_peak_wavelength(T) * 1e6
    power = sigma * T**4
    print(f"{label}: peak={peak_um:.2f}um, power={power:.1f} W/m^2")

L_skin = planck_spectral_radiance(10e-6, temps["skin (33C)"])
L_room = planck_spectral_radiance(10e-6, temps["room (20C)"])
print(f"radiance at 10um: skin={L_skin:.4e}, room={L_room:.4e}, ratio={L_skin/L_room:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
room (20C): peak=9.88um, power=418.8 W/m^2
skin (33C): peak=9.47um, power=498.1 W/m^2
hot machine (80C): peak=8.21um, power=882.0 W/m^2
molten metal (800C): peak=2.70um, power=75206.2 W/m^2
radiance at 10um: skin=1.0937e+07, room=8.8641e+06, ratio=1.2338
```

### 2. OpenCV: displaying a synthetic radiance map

```python
import cv2

temps_grid = 293.15 + 15 * np.exp(-((np.arange(100)[:, None] - 50)**2 + (np.arange(100)[None, :] - 50)**2) / (2 * 15**2))
radiance_grid = planck_spectral_radiance(10e-6, temps_grid)
display_8bit = cv2.normalize(radiance_grid, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX).astype(np.uint8)
thermal_view = cv2.applyColorMap(display_8bit, cv2.COLORMAP_INFERNO)
print("radiance grid:", radiance_grid.shape, radiance_grid.dtype)
print("thermal_view:", thermal_view.shape, thermal_view.dtype)
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function computes real radiometric physics** — Planck's law, Wien's law, and Stefan-Boltzmann (Code section 1) are plain physics, independent of any imaging library; `cv2` enters only once there is an actual image to display or process (Code section 2).
- **`cv2.applyColorMap(..., cv2.COLORMAP_INFERNO)`** (also used in 21.4) is a common real choice for a quick thermal-style display; 46.6 covers the real, dedicated thermal-specific palettes (ironbow, white-hot, black-hot) a real thermal camera's own UI actually offers.
- **This chapter's radiance values are real physical units** (W/(m².sr.m)) — converting a real camera's raw sensor counts into either radiance or a calibrated temperature is 46.3-46.4's own real subject, not covered here.

## Where it fails

- **This chapter assumed every surface radiates exactly like an ideal blackbody** — real surfaces do not; 46.2 covers emissivity, the real correction every actual thermal measurement needs.
- **Raw detector output is not yet a calibrated temperature** — 46.3-46.4 cover converting a real sensor's raw counts into an actual, trustworthy temperature reading.
- **A shiny or reflective surface can show a reflected hot (or cold) object instead of its own real temperature** — a real, common thermal-imaging pitfall, covered properly in 46.2.
- **Atmospheric absorption and path length matter for real long-range thermal measurements** — this chapter assumed a close, clear path with no real atmospheric attenuation.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Assuming a thermal camera "sees temperature" directly** — it detects real radiance (Section 2's Planck's law), which depends on BOTH temperature and emissivity (46.2); the two are easy to conflate.
- **Using a visible-light-style camera band for a very hot process** — Section 3's own real result (molten metal peaking at 2.70µm, not LWIR's 8-14µm) shows why an LWIR sensor is the wrong real tool there.
- **Expecting large, visible-camera-like brightness contrasts in a thermal image** — Section 4's own real result (only a 1.2338x contrast between skin and background at 10µm) is a genuinely small real signal, not a display or sensor fault.
- **Forgetting that "infrared" covers a huge real wavelength range** — NIR, SWIR, MWIR, and LWIR are physically very different real bands, each needing different real detector technology.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Atmospheric windows

The real LWIR (8-14µm) and MWIR (3-5µm) bands are not arbitrary engineering choices — they correspond to real atmospheric transmission windows, gaps between the strong real absorption bands of water vapour and CO2 that otherwise block infrared radiation over any real distance (Vollmer, Möllmann, *Infrared Thermal Imaging: Fundamentals, Research and Applications*, 2nd ed., Wiley-VCH, 2017). A detector tuned to the real 5-8µm gap between these two windows would see almost nothing over any real distance, which is exactly why commercial thermal cameras are never built for that band.

### Cooled vs uncooled detectors

A real uncooled microbolometer (the common, cheaper LWIR technology) measures a tiny real resistance change as its own pixels heat up from absorbed radiation — simple, but with a real, slower response and fewer very hot/cold distinctions at the far ends. A real cooled detector (often MWIR, used in aerospace/furnace monitoring) is cryogenically chilled to let far more sensitive real photon-counting technology work, at real extra cost and complexity.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A facilities team wants to use a single thermal camera to monitor both a server room (around 22°C) and a nearby furnace exhaust (around 600°C) in the same system. Based on Section 3, what real problem should they expect?

<Solution>

Following Section 3's own real result, these two real temperatures peak in very different bands — the server room near 9.88µm (LWIR) and the furnace exhaust (873.15K) considerably shorter, well into SWIR/MWIR — so a single detector tuned for one band would see the other scene poorly or not at all. A real system needs either two different cameras with different detector bands, or a genuine trade-off in sensitivity across both real temperature ranges.

</Solution>
