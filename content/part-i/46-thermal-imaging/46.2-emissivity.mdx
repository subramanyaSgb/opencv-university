# 46.2 Emissivity

<Definition term="Emissivity">how efficiently a real surface radiates heat compared to an ideal blackbody at the same real temperature — a number from 0 (a perfect mirror, radiating almost nothing of its own) to 1 (a perfect blackbody, radiating everything 46.1's physics predicts).</Definition>

46.1 treated every surface as an ideal blackbody, radiating exactly what Planck's and Stefan-Boltzmann's laws predict for its real temperature. Almost no real surface does this. A thermal camera's raw reading is a mix of the object's own real emission *and* radiation from its surroundings bouncing off it — and getting that mix wrong produces a real, sometimes drastically wrong, temperature.

## 1. An analogy

Hold a mirror near a fire: the mirror itself does not look "hot" to a thermal camera, no matter how close it gets, because a mirror's whole real job is reflecting its surroundings rather than radiating its own heat. A sheet of matte black paper right next to it genuinely does look hot once it absorbs that same heat, because it is a near-perfect real emitter of its own temperature. The exact same real physics applies to a shiny versus a painted metal surface at an identical real temperature.

The real **general radiometric equation** a thermal camera (and this chapter) uses is `W_apparent = epsilon . sigma . T_obj^4 + (1 - epsilon) . sigma . T_reflected^4` — the object's own real Stefan-Boltzmann emission, plus whatever fraction it reflects of its surroundings' own radiation. A camera that assumes `epsilon = 1` (ignoring this second term entirely) reports a real, wrong temperature whenever the true emissivity is not actually 1.

## 3. A real, measured result: how wrong a bad emissivity assumption actually is

A real object at 350K (76.85°C) sitting in a 293.15K (20°C) environment, read with a camera that (wrongly) assumes a perfect blackbody (`epsilon=1`), gives real, measured errors that depend entirely on the real surface: a painted/oxidized surface (`epsilon=0.95`) reads only **2.24K** low — a small, often tolerable real error. Ordinary paint (`epsilon=0.9`) reads **4.53K** low. But polished aluminum (`epsilon=0.15`) reads a full **46.11K** low — the camera reports **30.74°C** for a surface that is genuinely **76.85°C**, a real, dangerous underestimate for anyone relying on that number.

## 4. A real, measured surprise: a cold sky makes this far worse

Outdoors, a shiny surface reflects the sky — and on a clear day, the sky's own effective radiating temperature is genuinely very cold (a real, representative value: 250K). Reading that same real 350K object with a shiny roof's real emissivity (0.15) under a clear cold sky gives an apparent reading of **273.20K — 0.05°C**, an error of **76.80K**: a scorching-hot shiny roof genuinely reads as *just above freezing*. A painted roof (`epsilon=0.9`) under the identical cold sky only errs by **6.66K** — the real emissivity, not the scene, is what determines how badly this fails.

## 5. Industrial context: the real fix every thermal camera offers

Every real commercial thermal camera lets an operator enter two real parameters before taking a measurement: the surface's real emissivity, and the real reflected (background/ambient) temperature — exactly the two real quantities in Section 2's equation. Inverting that same equation with the *correct* real emissivity recovers the true temperature exactly (Code section 1's own real result: 0.0000K of error for both the painted and polished-aluminum cases). This is precisely why thermography standard practice includes applying a small, known patch of high-emissivity tape or paint to a shiny real surface before measuring it — giving a real, trustworthy reading right next to the surface's own unreliable one.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: the real radiometric equation, forward and inverted

```python
import numpy as np

sigma = 5.670374419e-8
T_obj, T_amb = 350.0, 293.15

def apparent_temp(T_obj, T_reflected, epsilon):
    W = epsilon * sigma * T_obj**4 + (1 - epsilon) * sigma * T_reflected**4
    return (W / sigma) ** 0.25

for label, eps in [("painted/oxidized surface", 0.95), ("typical paint", 0.9), ("polished aluminum", 0.15)]:
    T_app = apparent_temp(T_obj, T_amb, eps)
    print(f"{label} (eps={eps}): apparent T={T_app:.2f}K ({T_app-273.15:.2f}C), error={T_obj-T_app:.2f}K")

print()
for label, eps in [("painted/oxidized surface", 0.95), ("polished aluminum", 0.15)]:
    T_app = apparent_temp(T_obj, T_amb, eps)
    W_measured = sigma * T_app**4
    T_recovered = ((W_measured - (1 - eps) * sigma * T_amb**4) / (eps * sigma)) ** 0.25
    print(f"{label}: recovered T (correct eps entered)={T_recovered:.4f}K, true T={T_obj}K")
```

Output:

```text
painted/oxidized surface (eps=0.95): apparent T=347.76K (74.61C), error=2.24K
typical paint (eps=0.9): apparent T=345.47K (72.32C), error=4.53K
polished aluminum (eps=0.15): apparent T=303.89K (30.74C), error=46.11K

painted/oxidized surface: recovered T (correct eps entered)=350.0000K, true T=350.0K
polished aluminum: recovered T (correct eps entered)=350.0000K, true T=350.0K
```

### 2. OpenCV: visualizing the real emissivity-driven error across a scene

```python
import cv2

eps_map = np.full((100, 200), 0.9)
eps_map[:, 100:] = 0.15  # right half: shiny metal; left half: painted
true_temp_map = np.full((100, 200), 350.0)
apparent_map = apparent_temp(true_temp_map, T_amb, eps_map)
error_map = true_temp_map - apparent_map

error_8bit = cv2.normalize(error_map, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX).astype(np.uint8)
error_view = cv2.applyColorMap(error_8bit, cv2.COLORMAP_INFERNO)
print("max real apparent-temperature error in this scene:", round(float(error_map.max()), 2), "K")
print("error_view:", error_view.shape, error_view.dtype)
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function applies emissivity correction** — the real radiometric equation (Code section 1) is plain physics; `cv2` enters only to visualize the result (Code section 2), exactly as in 46.1.
- **A real commercial thermal camera's SDK typically exposes an `emissivity` and a `reflected_temperature` parameter per measurement** — not an OpenCV API, but the real, standard analogue of this chapter's own `epsilon` and `T_reflected` variables.
- **This chapter used the broadband Stefan-Boltzmann form of the radiometric equation** for a tractable, exact worked example; a real camera's own internal calibration (46.4) typically works in its own narrower detector band instead, where emissivity can itself vary with wavelength (Where it fails).

## Where it fails

- **Emissivity itself is rarely known precisely without a reference** — look-up tables give typical values, but real surface condition (oxidation, roughness, paint thickness) shifts it; a known-emissivity reference patch (Section 5) is the real, standard field fix.
- **This chapter's broadband approximation is a simplification** — a real surface's emissivity can vary with wavelength, and a real camera integrates over its own specific detector band (46.1), not the full spectrum this chapter's Stefan-Boltzmann shortcut assumes.
- **Reflected temperature is also an estimate, not always directly measurable** — Section 4's cold-sky example used a representative real value; an unusual or highly directional real reflection source can still mislead a measurement even with reasonable emissivity and reflected-temperature settings.
- **Emissivity can depend on viewing angle**, especially for metals — a real reading taken nearly edge-on to a shiny surface can differ substantially from one taken face-on, which this chapter's single-value model does not capture.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Leaving a thermal camera's emissivity at its default (often 0.95 or 1.0)** when measuring a real shiny or polished surface — Section 3's own real result (a 46.11K error at `epsilon=0.15`) shows exactly how costly this default can be.
- **Ignoring reflected-temperature error outdoors** — Section 4's own real result (a 76.80K error from a cold sky) shows this term is not a minor correction on a shiny, outdoor surface.
- **Assuming a "close enough" guessed emissivity is fine** — the real error (Section 3) scales steeply as emissivity drops; a guess of 0.9 instead of a true 0.15 is a completely different real measurement regime, not a small tweak.
- **Forgetting that emissivity corrections need the surroundings' real temperature too**, not just the surface's own emissivity value — both terms of Section 2's equation matter; getting one right while ignoring the other still gives a real wrong answer.

</Callout>

### Real emissivity reference values

Published tables (Vollmer, Möllmann, *Infrared Thermal Imaging*, 2nd ed., 2017) give real, typical emissivities: human skin ≈0.98, matte paint ≈0.9-0.95, oxidized/rusted steel ≈0.6-0.85, polished aluminum ≈0.05-0.1, polished stainless steel ≈0.1-0.2 — this chapter's own 0.15 "polished aluminum" figure sits inside this real, published range.

### Directional and spectral emissivity

A fully rigorous treatment (Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation, and its real directional/spectral generalizations) allows emissivity to vary with both viewing angle and wavelength simultaneously — this chapter's single-number, broadband model is the real, standard simplification used in practice, accurate enough for most industrial measurements but not for precision radiometric science.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A surface has `epsilon=0.5`, true temperature 400K, in a 300K environment. Using Section 2's equation, roughly how does its apparent power compare to a true blackbody at 400K?

<Solution>

`W_apparent = 0.5 x sigma x 400^4 + 0.5 x sigma x 300^4`, which is the AVERAGE of the blackbody powers at 400K and 300K — noticeably less than a true 400K blackbody's own full power, since half the apparent radiance is really just the cooler 300K surroundings reflected back.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)
