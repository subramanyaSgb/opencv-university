# 49.4 Repeatability (gauge R&R)

<Definition term="Gauge R&R (repeatability and reproducibility)">a real, formal study that decomposes a measurement system's total real variation into part-to-part (real, wanted) variation and measurement-system (real, unwanted) variation — and confirms, with real numbers, whether the system can be trusted at all.</Definition>

10.3 named a real gauge R&R study as the proper next step without performing one. This chapter does: the real, standard ANOVA-based method that tells a team, with real numbers rather than a guess, whether their vision measurement system is trustworthy.

## 1. An analogy

Handing the same real part to three different inspectors, each measuring it three times, gives nine real readings. If all nine cluster tightly together, the measurement *system* — camera, lighting, operator, software — is trustworthy, regardless of which inspector or which repeat produced any one reading. If they scatter widely, the system itself is the real problem, and no individual reading can be trusted until it is fixed.

A real Gauge R&R study measures several real parts (spanning the system's real working range), has several real operators each measure every part several real times, then decomposes the total real variance into **equipment variation (EV)** — repeatability, how much a single operator's own repeated readings scatter — **appraiser variation (AV)** — reproducibility, how much different operators disagree — and **part variation (PV)** — the real, wanted differences between parts themselves.

## 3. A real, measured result: a trustworthy system, confirmed exactly

Simulating 10 real parts (spanning a real 10-20mm range), 3 operators, 3 repeats each, with a known real repeatability noise (std 0.03mm) and no real operator bias, the real ANOVA decomposition recovers the known noise almost exactly: estimated EV **0.0301mm** against a true **0.03mm** — and estimated AV comes out small too (**0.0050mm**), correctly reflecting that there was no real operator bias to find. Because the real parts themselves vary so much more (a real part-to-part std of **3.1871mm**) than the measurement noise does, the real combined uncertainty (**GRR = 0.0305mm**) is a tiny **0.96%** of the total real variation — a genuinely excellent, trustworthy real measurement system.

## 4. A real, measured, dramatic contrast: borderline vs genuinely poor

Testing the identical real method on parts with a much tighter real tolerance (10.0-10.3mm, representative of an actual precision part) changes the real picture completely, even with *small* real measurement noise (std 0.01mm): the real %GRR rises to **10.35%** — right at the real, standard boundary between "excellent" and merely "acceptable." Adding a real, larger equipment noise (std 0.06mm) *and* real operator bias (-0.08mm, 0.0mm, +0.09mm across three operators) to that same tight-tolerance part gives a dramatically worse real result: **%GRR = 68.63%** — a measurement system that should not be trusted for this real part tolerance at all.

## 5. Real, established practice: the standard thresholds

The real, standard AIAG Measurement Systems Analysis (MSA) manual gives widely-used real thresholds for %GRR: **below 10%** is excellent, **10% to 30%** is acceptable (often with real caveats depending on the application), and **above 30%** is unacceptable — the measurement system itself must be improved before its numbers can be trusted. This chapter's own real results span all three real zones: 0.96% (Section 3, excellent), 10.35% (Section 4, borderline), and 68.63% (Section 4, unacceptable).

## 6. Industrial context: closing the loop on 49.1-49.3

A real Gauge R&R study is where 49.1's calibration, 49.2's sub-pixel precision, and 49.3's error analysis all meet their real, final test: if a vision system's calibration target is uncertified (49.1), if its sub-pixel estimator is far from the Cramer-Rao bound (49.2), or if its real uncertainty sources were mis-combined (49.3), the real symptom shows up directly here, as a real, measurably high %GRR — exactly the kind of real result that tells a team their measurement system, not their parts, needs attention.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: the real ANOVA-based Gauge R&R, from scratch

```python
import numpy as np

def gauge_rr(true_parts, operator_bias, repeat_noise_std, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_parts, n_ops, n_rep = len(true_parts), len(operator_bias), 3
    m = np.zeros((n_parts, n_ops, n_rep))
    for p in range(n_parts):
        for o in range(n_ops):
            for r in range(n_rep):
                m[p, o, r] = true_parts[p] + operator_bias[o] + rng.normal(0, repeat_noise_std)

    grand_mean = m.mean()
    part_means, op_means = m.mean(axis=(1, 2)), m.mean(axis=(0, 2))
    part_op_means = m.mean(axis=2)

    SS_part = n_ops * n_rep * np.sum((part_means - grand_mean) ** 2)
    SS_op = n_parts * n_rep * np.sum((op_means - grand_mean) ** 2)
    SS_int = n_rep * np.sum((part_op_means - part_means[:, None] - op_means[None, :] + grand_mean) ** 2)
    SS_rep = np.sum((m - part_op_means[:, :, None]) ** 2)

    MS_part, MS_op = SS_part / (n_parts - 1), SS_op / (n_ops - 1)
    MS_int, MS_rep = SS_int / ((n_parts - 1) * (n_ops - 1)), SS_rep / (n_parts * n_ops * (n_rep - 1))

    EV_var = MS_rep
    AV_var = max((MS_op - MS_int) / (n_parts * n_rep), 0)
    PV_var = max((MS_part - MS_int) / (n_ops * n_rep), 0)
    GRR_var = EV_var + AV_var
    TV_var = GRR_var + PV_var
    return tuple(np.sqrt(v) for v in (EV_var, AV_var, GRR_var, PV_var, TV_var))

true_parts = np.random.default_rng(1).uniform(10.0, 20.0, 10)
EV, AV, GRR, PV, TV = gauge_rr(true_parts, np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0]), 0.03, seed=60)
print(f"Good system: EV={EV:.4f} AV={AV:.4f} GRR={GRR:.4f} PV={PV:.4f} TV={TV:.4f} %GRR={100*GRR/TV:.2f}%")
```

Output:

```text
Good system: EV=0.0301 AV=0.0050 GRR=0.0305 PV=3.1871 TV=3.1872 %GRR=0.96%
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function performs ANOVA or Gauge R&R** — Code section 1-2's own real statistics are plain NumPy; `cv2` enters only to visualize the real result (the zone bar).
- **A real vision-system Gauge R&R study treats "operator" broadly** — it can mean different human operators placing a part under the camera, or simply different real camera/lighting setups measuring the identical part, whichever real source of reproducibility variation actually matters for the application.
- **This chapter's own real ANOVA formulas are the standard AIAG MSA method** — the identical real approach used across most manufacturing quality systems, not something specific to vision.

## Where it fails

- **A Gauge R&R study needs parts that genuinely span the real working range** — testing only near-identical parts (as this chapter's own "tight tolerance" test showed) makes PV artificially small, inflating %GRR even for an otherwise decent system.
- **This chapter's model assumed independent, additive error sources** — a real system with correlated errors (e.g., the same lighting drift affecting every operator identically) needs a more careful real analysis than the standard ANOVA decomposition provides.
- **%GRR alone does not say WHICH source (EV or AV) is the real problem** — Section 3-4's own real decomposition into separate EV and AV values is what actually points to a real fix (equipment vs training/procedure).
- **A real Gauge R&R study is a snapshot** — a measurement system that passes today can drift (49.1's own real calibration-target and thermal-drift themes) and needs real periodic re-verification.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Running a Gauge R&R study on parts that don't span the real working range** — Where it fails's own real point: this artificially shrinks PV and inflates %GRR, giving a falsely pessimistic real verdict.
- **Treating %GRR as the only real number that matters** — Section 6's own real point: a high %GRR needs the real EV/AV breakdown to actually diagnose and fix.
- **Assuming a vision system has no real "operator" variation because it's automated** — Section 6's own real point: differences in part placement, lighting setup, or ROI selection between real setups can reproduce the identical real reproducibility problem a human-operator study would catch.
- **Treating a passing Gauge R&R result as permanent** — Where it fails's own real point about drift and the need for periodic real re-verification.

</Callout>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A Gauge R&R study finds EV=0.04mm, AV=0.03mm, PV=0.5mm. What is the real %GRR?

<Solution>

`GRR = sqrt(0.04^2+0.03^2) = sqrt(0.0025) = 0.05mm`. `TV = sqrt(GRR^2+PV^2) = sqrt(0.0025+0.25) = sqrt(0.2525) ≈ 0.5025mm`. `%GRR = 100 x 0.05/0.5025 ≈ 9.95%` — just under the real "excellent" threshold.

</Solution>
