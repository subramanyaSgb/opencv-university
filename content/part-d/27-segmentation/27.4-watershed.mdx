# 27.4 Watershed

<Definition term="Watershed segmentation">segmentation that treats the image (usually its gradient) as a landscape and floods it with water rising from marker points; where water from two different markers would meet, a dam is built. The dams are the region borders.</Definition>

Region growing (27.1) grows one region from one seed. The watershed grows **all** regions at once from their seeds, always extending the lowest available pixel first, so regions meet exactly on the "ridges" of the image: the edges. It is the standard way to split touching objects (25.4) and to turn a few clicks into a full segmentation.

## 1. An analogy

A mountain landscape with lakes. Drill a hole at the bottom of every valley that should become a region and let water rise slowly from below. Each lake grows uphill. Where two lakes are about to merge, build a dam. When the water reaches the top of the highest mountain, the dams draw the borders of every region: they lie on the crests between valleys.

## 2. The idea and OpenCV's version

On the **gradient** magnitude, object interiors are low (flat colour) and edges are high, so regions fill objects first and meet on the edges. Without markers, every local minimum of the relief starts its own region; on a real image that means thousands of tiny regions (**over-segmentation**). That is why OpenCV's `cv2.watershed` (Meyer 1992 [2]) always starts from **markers** you provide.

OpenCV does not need you to compute the gradient: it orders pixels by the largest B, G or R difference between a pixel and its already-labelled neighbour, which is a gradient measured on the fly.

## 3. A worked example by hand

A 1D relief (heights, e.g. gradient values) with two markers, A at position 2 and B at position 6:

| Position | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Height | 5 | 3 | **1** | 3 | 6 | 2 | **1** | 4 |
| Label | A | A | A | A | dam | B | B | B |

Flooding level by level:

1. Level 1: the markers themselves.
2. Level 2: B reaches position 5.
3. Level 3: A reaches positions 1 and 3.
4. Level 4: B reaches position 7.
5. Level 5: A reaches position 0.
6. Level 6: position 4 is reached by A (from 3) and B (from 5) at once: it becomes the **watershed line**.

The border sits on the highest point between the two markers, the edge, not halfway between them.

## 4. On the scene

One marker (a disc of radius 3) per object and one on the cloth:

- All six regions are found; IoU 0.95 to 0.97 (the remainder is the 1 687 watershed-line pixels, which belong to no region).
- **Remove the crimson marker**: the red disc's region floods the crimson disc too (1 452 of its 1 516 pixels). Every region needs a marker; the watershed never invents one.
- **Markers from every gradient minimum** instead of seeds: 4 663 regions; even after smoothing the gradient (σ = 2) still 459. This is the classic over-segmentation.

## 5. Separating touching objects

The OpenCV tutorial recipe for touching objects builds markers from the binary mask:

1. **Sure background**: dilate the mask; everything outside it is background.
2. **Sure foreground**: threshold the distance transform (23.5) at a fraction of its maximum; what remains are the cores of the objects.
3. **Unknown**: sure background minus sure foreground (marker 0): the watershed decides.
4. Label the cores (`connectedComponents`), add 1 so the background becomes 1, set the unknown zone to 0, run `cv2.watershed`.

On the 40 particles (25.4), with a global threshold at 0.3 or 0.5 of the maximum distance (12 pixels): **38** markers and 38 regions; at 0.7: 21. A fraction of the **global** maximum suits objects of one size; here small particles never reach it, and touching pairs keep one shared core. Local maxima of the distance transform (25.4) gave all **40**. Choose markers to match the objects' size range.

## 6. Industrial use

- **Touching objects**: grains, pellets, cells, coins, bubbles: count and measure each one.
- **Interactive segmentation**: a few clicks or strokes as markers (as in many labelling tools).
- **Boundaries of known parts**: markers from a template or the previous frame; the watershed snaps the borders to the edges.
- **Microscopy**: nuclei as markers, cell borders by watershed on the membrane image.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Relief 2, 1, 4, 3, 7, 3, 1 with markers at positions 1 (A) and 6 (B). Where is the watershed line?

<Solution>

Flooding order: A takes position 0 (height 2); B takes 5 (height 3); A takes 2 (height 4) and then 3 (height 3, reachable only through 2). Position 4 (height 7) now touches A and B: the watershed line is at **position 4**, the highest point between the markers.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Your watershed gives one region for two touching coins. What is wrong?

<Solution>

The two coins share one marker (or one has none). Make sure each coin has its own marker, e.g. local maxima of the distance transform.

</Solution>

## Code

Three examples: markers on the scene, a missing marker, and touching particles.

### 1. One marker per object

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

scene = cv2.imread("sample-seg.png")
ids = cv2.imread("sample-seg-ids.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
seeds = [(225, 15), (55, 55), (120, 50), (165, 65), (60, 125), (205, 112)]   # cloth, orange, red, crimson, box, label

markers = np.zeros(ids.shape, np.int32)                                      # int32, 0 = unknown
for k, (x, y) in enumerate(seeds):
    cv2.circle(markers, (x, y), 3, k + 1, -1)                                # labels 1 … 6
result = cv2.watershed(scene, markers.copy())                                # 8-bit 3-channel image
print("labels:", np.unique(result).tolist(), "| line pixels:", int((result == -1).sum()))
print("IoU:", [round(float(((result == k + 1) & (ids == k)).sum() / ((result == k + 1) | (ids == k)).sum()), 3) for k in range(6)])
```

Output:

```text
labels: [-1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] | line pixels: 1687
IoU: [0.953, 0.971, 0.963, 0.971, 0.945, 0.965]
```

- `cv2.watershed` modifies the markers array in place and also returns it; pass a copy to keep your markers.
- The image border is always set to −1.

### 2. A missing marker

```python
m2 = markers.copy()
m2[m2 == 4] = 0                                                              # remove the crimson seed
r2 = cv2.watershed(scene, m2)
print("red region:", int((r2 == 3).sum()), "px | crimson pixels in it:", int(((r2 == 3) & (ids == 3)).sum()), "of", int((ids == 3).sum()))
```

Output:

```text
red region: 3281 px | crimson pixels in it: 1452 of 1516
```

### 3. Touching particles: the tutorial recipe

```python
img = cv2.imread("sample-particles.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
_, mask = cv2.threshold(img, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
mask = cv2.morphologyEx(mask, cv2.MORPH_OPEN, np.ones((3, 3), np.uint8))
sure_bg = cv2.dilate(mask, np.ones((3, 3), np.uint8), iterations=3)
dist = cv2.distanceTransform(mask, cv2.DIST_L2, 5)
for frac in (0.3, 0.5, 0.7):
    _, sure_fg = cv2.threshold(dist, frac * dist.max(), 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)
    sure_fg = sure_fg.astype(np.uint8)
    unknown = cv2.subtract(sure_bg, sure_fg)
    n, mk = cv2.connectedComponents(sure_fg)
    mk = mk + 1                                                              # background = 1, cores = 2 …
    mk[unknown == 255] = 0
    ws = cv2.watershed(cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_GRAY2BGR), mk)
    print(f"fraction {frac}: {n - 1} markers -> {len(np.unique(ws[ws > 1]))} regions (true 40)")
```

Output:

```text
fraction 0.3: 38 markers -> 38 regions (true 40)
fraction 0.5: 38 markers -> 38 regions (true 40)
fraction 0.7: 21 markers -> 21 regions (true 40)
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.watershed(image, markers)`** → markers (in place). `image`: 8-bit, 3 channels. `markers`: int32, same size; > 0 = seed labels, 0 = to be decided. Output: labels, −1 on watershed lines and the image border.
- **Markers from**: clicks/strokes (`cv2.circle`, `cv2.line` into the marker image), `cv2.connectedComponents` of sure-foreground cores, local maxima of the distance transform (25.4).
- **Grey images**: convert to 3 channels with `cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_GRAY2BGR)`.
- **Speed**: linear in the number of pixels (one priority queue with 256 levels).

## Where it fails

- **Missing or extra markers**: merged or split objects; the watershed trusts the markers completely.
- **Weak edges**: a region leaks through the gap at the lowest crossing point.
- **Noisy gradients**: thin, wiggly borders; smooth first.
- **Watershed lines**: the −1 pixels belong to no region; assign them to a neighbour if you need areas.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Markers as uint8**: they must be int32.
- **Background labelled 0**: 0 means "unknown"; give the sure background its own label (usually 1).
- **Grey input**: `cv2.watershed` needs a 3-channel 8-bit image.
- **Counting −1 as a region**: exclude it (and the background label) when counting.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Definitions of the watershed

Beucher and Lantuéjoul [1] introduced the watershed for contour detection; Vincent and Soille [3] gave a fast immersion algorithm; Meyer [2] the marker-controlled flooding with a priority queue that OpenCV follows. Different definitions (by flooding, by topographic distance, as a minimum spanning forest) give slightly different lines on plateaus. The **h-minima** transform (suppressing minima shallower than h) is a classical way to reduce over-segmentation without explicit markers.

### Compact watershed

Plain watershed regions can be very irregular in flat areas. The compact watershed adds a spatial term to the flooding priority (Neubert and Protzel 2014), giving more regular regions and a simple superpixel method (27.6).

</GoDeeper>
