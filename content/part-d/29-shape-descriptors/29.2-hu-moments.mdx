# 29.2 Hu moments

<Definition term="Hu moments">seven numbers computed from the moments of a shape that stay (almost) the same when the shape is moved, scaled or rotated (Hu 1962 [1]). The seventh changes sign when the shape is mirrored.</Definition>

Moments (24.6) describe how a shape's area is distributed: its size (m00), centre (m10/m00, m01/m00), spread and orientation (second order), skew (third order). Each depends on position, size and rotation. Hu combined the normalised second- and third-order moments into seven expressions that do not, so two copies of the same part give the same seven numbers wherever and however they lie.

## 1. An analogy

Describing a person's build without a photo: height and weight change with age and units, but proportions such as "legs are half the height" stay. Hu moments are proportions of a shape: they ignore where it is, how big it is and which way it faces.

## 2. From moments to invariants

The first two invariants show the idea:

- **h1 = η20 + η02**: the total spread (like a moment of inertia), the same in every direction you measure it.
- **h2 = (η20 − η02)² + 4 η11²**: how elongated the shape is, regardless of the direction of the elongation.
- h3 … h6 use third-order moments (skew, asymmetry); **h7** is a "skew" invariant whose **sign flips under mirroring**, so it can tell a shape from its mirror image.

The values span many orders of magnitude, so they are usually shown as −sign(h) · log₁₀|h|.

## 3. A worked example by hand

A region of 2 × 3 pixels: columns x = 2, 3 and rows y = 1, 2, 3 (6 pixels).

- m00 = 6, m10 = Σx = 3 · 2 + 3 · 3 = 15, m01 = Σy = 2 · (1 + 2 + 3) = 12. Centroid (2.5, 2).
- μ20 = Σ(x − 2.5)² = 6 · 0.25 = **1.5**; μ02 = Σ(y − 2)² = 2 · (1 + 0 + 1) = **4**; μ11 = Σ(x − 2.5)(y − 2) = 0.
- η20 = 1.5 / 6² = 0.042, η02 = 4 / 36 = 0.111.
- **h1** = 0.042 + 0.111 = **0.153**; **h2** = (0.042 − 0.111)² + 0 = **0.0048**.

Rotate the region by 90° (3 wide, 2 high): μ20 and μ02 swap, and h1, h2 are unchanged. Move it anywhere: the central moments do not change. Double its size: η stays the same because of the division by m00.

## 4. On the shape image

Each shape appears twice: upright in the top row, rotated and scaled (and, for the bracket, mirrored) in the bottom row. Log-scaled Hu moments from `cv2.moments` on the contours:

| arrow | 0.53 | 1.26 | 2.73 | 3.25 | 6.23 | 3.88 | 8.49 |

- For the bracket and the arrow, h1 to h6 agree to within 0.16 and 0.38 on the log scale (the higher orders differ most); h7 flips sign for the mirrored bracket, as it should. (The arrow's h7 also flips: for nearly symmetric shapes h7 is tiny, and its sign is decided by pixel effects.)
- For the **cross**, h2 jumps from 9.71 to 6.84 (a factor of 740 in the raw value). A cross is symmetric, so η20 = η02 and η11 = 0 in theory and h2 is nearly zero. Small pixel errors then dominate the logarithm. The same happens with the star and the gear.

Hu moments are robust for **asymmetric**, reasonably **large** shapes. For symmetric shapes, rely on h1 and on the non-zero invariants only, or use other descriptors (29.3).

## 5. Industrial use

- **Part identification** on a conveyor: compare the Hu vector of each blob with stored references (29.4).
- **Orientation-free sorting**: shapes in random orientation, as long as they are not too symmetric.
- **Mirror check**: h7's sign tells a left-hand part from a right-hand part.
- **Feature vectors** for classifiers (36.x), usually together with area, perimeter, circularity and convexity.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A 4 × 1 bar of pixels (x = 0…3, y = 0). Compute μ20, μ02 and h1.

<Solution>

Centroid x = 1.5. μ20 = 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 = 5; μ02 = 0. η20 = 5 / 16 = 0.3125; **h1 = 0.3125**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Two parts have identical h1–h6 but opposite h7. What are they?

<Solution>

</Solution>

## Code

Three examples: the worked example, Hu moments of the shapes, and contour versus image moments.

### 1. The worked example

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

region = np.zeros((5, 5), np.uint8)
region[1:4, 2:4] = 1                                                # rows 1-3, columns 2-3
m = cv2.moments(region, binaryImage=True)
print({k: round(m[k], 3) for k in ("m00", "m10", "m01", "mu20", "mu02", "mu11", "nu20", "nu02")})
h = cv2.HuMoments(m).ravel()
print("h1:", round(float(h[0]), 4), "| h2:", round(float(h[1]), 4))
h_rot = cv2.HuMoments(cv2.moments(np.rot90(region).copy(), binaryImage=True)).ravel()
print("rotated h1:", round(float(h_rot[0]), 4), "| h2:", round(float(h_rot[1]), 4))
```

Output:

```text
{'m00': 6.0, 'm10': 15.0, 'm01': 12.0, 'mu20': 1.5, 'mu02': 4.0, 'mu11': 0.0, 'nu20': 0.042, 'nu02': 0.111}
h1: 0.1528 | h2: 0.0048
rotated h1: 0.1528 | h2: 0.0048
```

### 2. Hu moments of the shapes

```python
img = cv2.imread("sample-shapes.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
contours = cv2.findContours(img, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_NONE)[0]
contours = sorted(contours, key=lambda c: (int(cv2.boundingRect(c)[1] > 90), cv2.boundingRect(c)[0]))
names = ["star", "gear", "bracket", "cross", "oval", "arrow"]

def log_hu(c):
    h = cv2.HuMoments(cv2.moments(c)).ravel()
    return np.round(-np.sign(h) * np.log10(np.abs(h)), 2).tolist()

for i in (2, 3, 5):
    print(names[i], log_hu(contours[i]))
    print(names[i], "copy", log_hu(contours[6 + i]))
```

Output:

```text
bracket [0.44, 1.43, 1.5, 2.5, -4.65, -3.27, -4.67]
bracket copy [0.43, 1.39, 1.44, 2.45, -4.49, -3.18, 4.62]
cross [0.7, 9.71, 4.65, 6.09, -11.46, -10.94, -21.22]
cross copy [0.7, 6.84, 6.32, 8.38, 15.77, -13.04, -16.17]
arrow [0.53, 1.26, 2.73, 3.25, 6.23, 3.88, 8.49]
arrow copy [0.5, 1.21, 2.55, 3.05, 5.85, 3.65, -8.24]
```

### 3. Contour moments vs region moments

```python
c = contours[2]
x, y, w, h = cv2.boundingRect(c)
m_contour = cv2.moments(c)                                           # polygon through the boundary pixel centres
m_region = cv2.moments(img[y:y + h, x:x + w], binaryImage=True)       # every pixel of the region
print("area: contour", m_contour["m00"], "| pixels", m_region["m00"])
print("h1: contour", round(float(cv2.HuMoments(m_contour)[0, 0]), 4), "| pixels", round(float(cv2.HuMoments(m_region)[0, 0]), 4))
```

Output:

```text
area: contour 1197.5 | pixels 1300.0
h1: contour 0.3602 | pixels 0.3423
```

- A contour is a polygon through the centres of the boundary pixels, so its area misses half a pixel all round. For small shapes this changes the moments noticeably; compare shapes with one method only.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.moments(array, binaryImage=False)`** → dict with m00…m03, mu20…mu03, nu20…nu03. `array`: a contour (N × 1 × 2, int32 or float32) or a single-channel image (`binaryImage=True` treats every non-zero pixel as 1).
- **`cv2.HuMoments(m)`** → (7, 1) float64.
- **Contour moments** use Green's theorem on the polygon (exact for polygons, fast); image moments sum over pixels.
- **Log scaling**: `-np.sign(h) * np.log10(np.abs(h))`; guard against h = 0.

## Where it fails

- **Symmetric shapes**: several invariants are theoretically zero; their logs are noise.
- **Small shapes** (tens of pixels): discretisation changes the higher moments.
- **Occlusion**: moments are global; a missing piece changes all seven.
- **Discrimination**: different shapes can have similar low-order moments; seven numbers cannot separate many classes.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Comparing raw Hu values**: they differ by orders of magnitude; use the log scale (or matchShapes).
- **Mixing contour and region moments** between references and test images.
- **Expecting mirror invariance**: h7 flips sign; use |h7| if mirrored parts count as the same.
- **Using Hu moments on near-circular or symmetric parts**: most invariants are near zero.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Where the invariants come from

Hu [1] derived the seven invariants from the theory of algebraic invariants, using the second- and third-order normalised central moments. Flusser [3] showed that Hu's set is incomplete and dependent (h3 can be expressed by the others) and derived complete, independent rotation invariants of any order; Zernike moments, built on orthogonal polynomials on the unit disc, give rotation invariants with less redundancy and better noise behaviour.

### Affine invariants

Under a slanted view, shapes undergo affine, not similarity, transformations. Flusser and Suk's affine moment invariants handle that case.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A 2 × 2 square of pixels and a 4 × 4 square: compute η20 for both and show that h1 is the same.

<Solution>

2 × 2: centroid 0.5; μ20 = 4 · 0.25 = 1; m00 = 4; η20 = 1/16. By symmetry η02 = 1/16: h1 = **0.125**. 4 × 4: μ20 = 4 · (2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25) = 20; m00 = 16; η20 = 20/256 = 0.078; h1 = 0.156. Not equal: pixel moments of tiny squares are not exactly scale-invariant (the continuous square gives 1/6 ≈ 0.167 for both); the difference shrinks as the squares grow.

</Solution>
