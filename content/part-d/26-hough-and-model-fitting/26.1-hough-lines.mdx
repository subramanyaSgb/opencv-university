# 26.1 Hough lines

<Definition term="Hough transform (lines)">a voting method that finds straight lines in a set of edge points: every edge point votes for all the lines that could pass through it, and the lines with the most votes are the lines in the image.</Definition>

Edge detection (Module 22) gives pixels, not lines. To measure the angle of a part, find the edge of a conveyor belt or check that a label is straight, you need the line itself: its position and direction. The Hough transform (Hough 1962 [1]; in the (ρ, θ) form by Duda and Hart 1972 [2]) finds it even when the edge is broken, noisy, or partly hidden.

## 1. An analogy

An election. Each edge point is a voter who cannot see the whole picture: it only knows that "the line goes through me". So it votes for every line through itself. Points on the same real line all vote for that one line, and it wins with many votes. Random points spread their votes over many different lines, and none of those wins.

## 2. Describing a line by (ρ, θ)

The school form y = a x + b fails for vertical lines (a is infinite). Hough uses the **normal form**:

```text
x · cos θ + y · sin θ = ρ
```

- **θ** is the angle of the line's normal (the perpendicular from the origin), 0° to 180°.
- **ρ** is the signed distance from the origin (the top-left pixel) to the line.

Every line has one (ρ, θ) with θ in [0°, 180°) and ρ between −diagonal and +diagonal. A vertical line x = 50 is (ρ = 50, θ = 0°); a horizontal line y = 30 is (ρ = 30, θ = 90°).

The accumulator is a 2D array of counters: one row per θ step, one column per ρ step. With 1° and 1 pixel steps on a 320 × 200 image, OpenCV uses 180 angles × 1 041 ρ bins.

## 3. A worked example by hand

Three edge points: (x, y) = (0, 4), (2, 2), (4, 0). Each votes at four angles (45° steps):

| θ | ρ for (0, 4) | ρ for (2, 2) | ρ for (4, 0) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0° | 0 | 2 | 4 |
| 45° | **2.83** | **2.83** | **2.83** |
| 90° | 4 | 2 | 0 |
| 135° | 2.83 | 0 | −2.83 |

At θ = 45° all three points give ρ = 4 / √2 = 2.83: the cell (2.83, 45°) gets **3 votes**, every other cell at most 1. The line is x cos 45° + y sin 45° = 2.83, i.e. x + y = 4, which indeed passes through all three points. With ρ steps of 1 pixel, OpenCV rounds 2.83 into the bin ρ = **3**: `cv2.HoughLines` on these three pixels with threshold 2 returns (ρ = 3, θ = 45°).

Each point's votes form a sinusoid ρ(θ) = x cos θ + y sin θ in the accumulator. Points on one line give sinusoids that all cross at the same (ρ, θ).

## 4. On a real image

The new test image has a plate rotated by 12°, 170 × 110 pixels. Canny (50, 150) gives **1 364** edge pixels. The plate's long sides have the normal angle θ = 102°, the short sides θ = 12°.

| (ρ, θ) peak | Votes | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 17, 102° | 100 | top long side |
| 128, 102° | 99 | bottom long side |
| 223, 12° | 79 | right short side |
| 20, 101° | 66 | top side again (neighbouring θ) |
| 224, 13° | 63 | right side again |
| 53, 12° | 60 | left short side |

Two effects are visible:

- **Split votes.** The top side lies at ρ = 17.87, between two bins. Its about 180 edge pixels split into 100 votes for ρ = 17 and 77 for ρ = 18. A line's peak height depends on where it falls in the grid, not only on its length.
- **Duplicates.** A real edge also produces strong cells at neighbouring angles (ρ = 20, θ = 101°: 66 votes). OpenCV only rejects a cell if one of its four direct neighbours is higher, so near-duplicates survive.

The threshold (OpenCV keeps cells with **more than** `threshold` votes) decides what you get:

| Threshold | Lines | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 40 | 12 | all four sides, each 3 times |
| 60 | 5 | three sides plus two duplicates; the left side (60 votes) is missing |
| 80 | 2 | the two long sides |
| 100 | 0 | nothing |

There is no threshold that gives exactly the four sides. The usual fix is a low threshold and then merging lines that are close in ρ and θ (Exercise 2), or the probabilistic version below.

## 5. Probabilistic Hough: segments

`cv2.HoughLinesP` (Matas, Galambos and Kittler 2000 [3]) votes with a random subset of edge points, and when a cell reaches the threshold it walks along the line in the image to find the actual **segment** (with end points). Segments shorter than `minLineLength` are dropped; gaps up to `maxLineGap` pixels are bridged.

With threshold 50, `minLineLength = 40` and `maxLineGap = 5`, it returns **6** segments on the plate: the two long sides twice each (169 and 165 pixels long) and the two short sides (107 and 106 pixels). The duplicates come from the two parallel rows of edge pixels along the anti-aliased edge.

## 6. Industrial use

- **Alignment**: the angle of a plate, a label or a PCB from its edges (here 12°, from θ = 102° − 90°).
- **Web and conveyor edges**: the position of a material's edge for edge guiding.
- **Lane and track detection**: segments of road markings or rails.
- **Measuring**: distances between parallel lines (here the long sides: 128 − 17 = 111 pixels in the coarse bins; the true width is 110).

For precise measurement, use the Hough result only to find the edge, then fit a line to the edge points near it (26.4) for sub-pixel accuracy.

## Code

Three examples: the accumulator from scratch on the worked example, `HoughLines` on the plate, and `HoughLinesP`.

### 1. The accumulator from scratch

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

points = [(0, 4), (2, 2), (4, 0)]                                   # (x, y)
thetas = np.deg2rad([0, 45, 90, 135])
for x, y in points:
    print((x, y), "rho:", np.round(x * np.cos(thetas) + y * np.sin(thetas), 2).tolist())

acc = {}                                                            # (rho bin, theta in degrees) -> votes
for x, y in points:
    for t in thetas:
        key = (int(np.rint(x * np.cos(t) + y * np.sin(t))), int(round(np.degrees(t))))
        acc[key] = acc.get(key, 0) + 1
print("best cell:", max(acc, key=acc.get), "votes:", max(acc.values()))

img = np.zeros((5, 5), np.uint8)
for x, y in points:
    img[y, x] = 255
print("cv2.HoughLines:", cv2.HoughLines(img, 1, np.pi / 4, 2)[:, 0].tolist())
```

Output:

```text
(0, 4) rho: [0.0, 2.83, 4.0, 2.83]
(2, 2) rho: [2.0, 2.83, 2.0, 0.0]
(4, 0) rho: [4.0, 2.83, 0.0, -2.83]
best cell: (3, 45) votes: 3
cv2.HoughLines: [[3.0, 0.7853981852531433]]
```

- `cv2.HoughLines` returns an array of shape (N, 1, 2) with (ρ, θ), θ in radians (0.785 = 45°).
- The `threshold` is compared with `>`: with threshold 2, a cell needs at least 3 votes.

### 2. HoughLines on the plate

```python
img = cv2.imread("sample-hough.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
edges = cv2.Canny(img, 50, 150)
print("edge pixels:", cv2.countNonZero(edges))

lines = cv2.HoughLinesWithAccumulator(edges, 1, np.pi / 180, 60)  # like HoughLines, plus the votes
for rho, theta, votes in lines[:, 0]:
    print(f"rho {rho:5.1f}  theta {np.degrees(theta):5.1f} deg  votes {int(votes)}")
for t in (40, 60, 80, 100):
    found = cv2.HoughLines(edges, 1, np.pi / 180, t)
    print("threshold", t, "->", 0 if found is None else len(found), "lines")
```

Output:

```text
edge pixels: 1364
rho  17.0  theta 102.0 deg  votes 100
rho 128.0  theta 102.0 deg  votes 99
rho 223.0  theta  12.0 deg  votes 79
rho  20.0  theta 101.0 deg  votes 66
rho 224.0  theta  13.0 deg  votes 63
threshold 40 -> 12 lines
threshold 60 -> 5 lines
threshold 80 -> 2 lines
threshold 100 -> 0 lines
```

- Lines come sorted by votes, strongest first.
- `HoughLines` returns `None`, not an empty array, when nothing passes the threshold.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.HoughLines(image, rho, theta, threshold, srn=0, stn=0, min_theta=0, max_theta=CV_PI)`** → (N, 1, 2) float32 (ρ, θ) or `None`. `image`: 8-bit single-channel binary (non-zero = edge). `rho` in pixels, `theta` in radians. `srn`/`stn` > 0 switch to the multi-scale variant. `min_theta`/`max_theta` restrict the angles (e.g. near-vertical lines only).
- **`cv2.HoughLinesWithAccumulator(...)`**: same arguments; returns (ρ, θ, votes).
- **`cv2.HoughLinesP(image, rho, theta, threshold, minLineLength=0, maxLineGap=0)`** → (N, 1, 4) int32 (x1, y1, x2, y2) or `None`.
- **Cost**: proportional to edge pixels × number of angles. Keep the edge image clean (Canny thresholds, a ROI) and restrict θ when you know the direction.
- **`cv2.createLineSegmentDetector()`** (LSD) finds line segments without voting or parameters; it is back in OpenCV since 4.5.1.

## Where it fails

- **Curved or slightly bent edges**: votes spread over many cells; no clear peak.
- **Dense texture** (fabric, grass, text): many accidental peaks.
- **Precise measurement**: the result is quantised to the ρ and θ steps; refine with a least-squares fit on the edge points (26.4).
- **Many short lines**: a long line collects more votes than several short ones, so a single threshold cannot suit both.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Passing a grey image instead of edges**: every non-zero pixel votes, so the whole image votes.
- **Forgetting θ is in radians** (both as input step and in the output).
- **Expecting one line per edge**: anti-aliased edges and coarse bins create duplicates; merge them.
- **Not checking for `None`** when nothing is found.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Point–line duality

In (ρ, θ) space a point becomes a sinusoid and a line becomes a point; collinear points become sinusoids through one common point. Hough's original patent [1] used the slope–intercept form, in which a point becomes a line; Duda and Hart [2] introduced the bounded normal form, which made the transform practical. The generalised Hough transform (Ballard 1981 [4]) extends the voting idea to arbitrary shapes with an R-table of edge offsets.

### Accuracy and resolution

The ρ step limits how well a line's position can be found; votes split across bins when a line falls between them. Smaller steps reduce quantisation but spread the votes thinner (each bin gets fewer), so peaks are lower and noisier. Kernel-based voting, where each point votes with a small weight spread over neighbouring cells, and peak interpolation give sub-bin accuracy.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Run `HoughLinesWithAccumulator` with threshold 40, then merge lines whose ρ differ by at most 5 pixels and whose θ differ by at most 3° (keep the strongest of each group). How many lines remain, and what is the plate's rotation?

<Solution>

```python
lines = cv2.HoughLinesWithAccumulator(edges, 1, np.pi / 180, 40)[:, 0]   # sorted by votes
kept = []
for rho, theta, votes in lines:
    if all(abs(rho - r) > 5 or abs(np.degrees(theta - t)) > 3 for r, t, _ in kept):
        kept.append((rho, theta, votes))
for rho, theta, votes in kept:
    print(f"rho {rho:5.1f}  theta {np.degrees(theta):5.1f} deg  votes {int(votes)}")
print("lines before:", len(lines), "| after merging:", len(kept))
print("rotation:", round(float(np.degrees(kept[0][1])) - 90, 1), "deg")
```

Output:

```text
rho  17.0  theta 102.0 deg  votes 100
rho 128.0  theta 102.0 deg  votes 99
rho 223.0  theta  12.0 deg  votes 79
rho  53.0  theta  12.0 deg  votes 60
lines before: 12 | after merging: 4
rotation: 12.0 deg
```

The four sides of the plate, each once. This simple greedy merging works when lines are well separated; near θ = 0° / 180° remember that (ρ, θ) and (−ρ, θ ± 180°) are the same line.

</Solution>
