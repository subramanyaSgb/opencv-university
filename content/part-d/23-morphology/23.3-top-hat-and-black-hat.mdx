# 23.3 Top-hat and black-hat

<Definition term="Top-hat and black-hat">two grey-image transforms built from opening and closing. The top-hat (white top-hat) is the image minus its opening: it keeps bright details smaller than the structuring element and removes everything else, including a slowly varying background. The black-hat (bottom-hat) is the closing minus the image: it does the same for dark details.</Definition>

Module 15 removed uneven illumination by dividing or subtracting an estimate of the background (15.3). Morphology offers another estimate: on a grey image, an opening with an element larger than the bright details removes them and leaves the background (23.6 explains grey opening in detail); the difference is the details alone. Meyer named it "top-hat" in 1979 [1] after the shape of the profile it extracts: a peak on a flat brim.

## 1. An analogy

Pour sand over a bumpy floor with a few nails sticking up, then drag a wide board over it. The board rests on the floor's broad bumps but cannot follow the thin nails: the sand surface is the "opening". The height of each point above the sand is the top-hat: only the nails stick out, whatever the floor's slope.

A worked example on a row with a dark mark on a sloping background, closing with a 1 × 5 element:

| Pixel | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Image | 100 | 110 | 120 | 40 | 45 | 150 | 160 | 170 |
| Closing (background) | 120 | 120 | 120 | 120 | 120 | 150 | 160 | 170 |
| Black-hat | 20 | 10 | 0 | **80** | **75** | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The closing fills the 2-pixel dark mark with the level of its surroundings (120); subtracting gives the mark as a strong positive response. The small values at pixels 1–2 come from the slope at the left border (the closing cannot follow a ramp perfectly there), one reason to threshold the result rather than use it as is.

On the unevenly lit print (dark text, light falling from 100 % to 35 % across the page; background values range from 65 to 223), measured against the ground-truth text mask:

| Method | Precision | Recall | F1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Otsu on the raw image | 0.26 | 1.00 | 0.41 |
| Black-hat 5 × 5, then Otsu | 0.98 | 0.83 | 0.90 |
| **Black-hat 9 × 9, then Otsu** | 0.98 | 0.97 | **0.975** |
| Black-hat 21 × 21, then Otsu | 0.98 | 0.97 | 0.976 |

A global threshold fails completely (the dark corner of the page is darker than the text in the bright corner). After the black-hat, one Otsu threshold separates text and paper almost perfectly. The element must be wider than the strokes (about 6 pixels here): at 5 × 5 the closing cannot fill the widest strokes, so their centres are lost (recall 0.83). Beyond that, the size hardly matters.

## 3. Practical rules

- **Bright details → top-hat; dark details → black-hat.** Which is which depends on polarity, not on what is "object".
- **Element larger than the details, smaller than the background variations.** If the background changes over distances similar to the details, no element separates them.
- **Element shape**: a disc (ellipse) for round or arbitrary details; a line for scratches of known direction (a line element longer than the scratch is wide but along the background trend).
- **Noise**: top-hats keep noise (it is a small detail too); smooth slightly first, or threshold with a margin.
- **Compared with 15.3**: division by a smooth background model corrects gain (multiplicative) changes; top-hat subtracts, so text contrast still varies with illumination (fainter text in the dark corner). For thresholding a clear foreground, the top-hat is simple and robust; for photometric measurements, use flat-field correction.

## 4. Industrial example

Top-hat and black-hat transforms are standard for detecting small defects on large, unevenly lit surfaces: dark pits and inclusions on steel strip (black-hat), bright particles on a filter membrane or glass (top-hat), and scratches on painted parts (black-hat with a line element). They are also the usual pre-processing for reading dot-peen or laser-etched codes on curved metal, where lighting can never be perfectly uniform.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** You look for bright solder balls about 8 pixels wide on a board with uneven lighting. Which transform and element?

<Solution>

**Top-hat** with an element wider than the balls, e.g. an 11 × 11 or 15 × 15 ellipse.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why does a black-hat on a flat background with no dark details give (nearly) zero?

<Solution>

The closing of a flat (or smoothly varying) image is the image itself, so closing − image ≈ 0. Only structures the element cannot enter (dark details smaller than it) give a response.

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: black-hat thresholding of the uneven print, then top-hat for bright particles.

### 1. Black-hat on uneven print

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-print-uneven.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
truth = cv2.imread("sample-print-uneven-gt.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE) > 0

def scores(mask):
    tp = (mask & truth).sum()
    p, r = tp / max(mask.sum(), 1), tp / truth.sum()
    return round(float(p), 3), round(float(r), 3), round(float(2 * p * r / (p + r)), 3)

_, raw = cv2.threshold(img, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY_INV + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
print("Otsu on raw (precision, recall, F1):", scores(raw > 0))
for size in (5, 9, 21):
    k = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (size, size))
    bh = cv2.morphologyEx(img, cv2.MORPH_BLACKHAT, k)                # closing - image
    t, m = cv2.threshold(bh, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
    print(f"black-hat {size}x{size}, Otsu T={t:.0f}:", scores(m > 0))
```

Output:

```text
Otsu on raw (precision, recall, F1): (0.261, 1.0, 0.414)
black-hat 5x5, Otsu T=45: (0.984, 0.828, 0.899)
black-hat 9x9, Otsu T=57: (0.977, 0.972, 0.975)
black-hat 21x21, Otsu T=60: (0.978, 0.973, 0.976)
```

### 2. Top-hat for bright particles

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:200, 0:320]
background = 60 + 120 * (xx / 320) + 30 * np.sin(yy / 40)            # strong uneven background
particles = np.zeros((200, 320))
for x, y in rng.integers(10, [310, 190], size=(25, 2)):
    cv2.circle(particles, (int(x), int(y)), 3, 40, -1)                # 25 bright particles, +40 grey levels
img2 = np.clip(background + particles + rng.normal(0, 3, (200, 320)), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)
th = cv2.morphologyEx(img2, cv2.MORPH_TOPHAT, cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (15, 15)))
_, m = cv2.threshold(th, 20, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)
m = cv2.morphologyEx(m, cv2.MORPH_OPEN, np.ones((3, 3), np.uint8))
n = cv2.connectedComponents(m)[0] - 1
_, raw = cv2.threshold(img2, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
print("particles found with top-hat:", n, "| raw Otsu marks", int((raw > 0).mean() * 100), "% of the image")
```

Output:

```text
particles found with top-hat: 22 | raw Otsu marks 49 % of the image
```

- 22 blobs for 25 particles: the positions are random, so a few particles overlap and merge into one blob. 25.4 deals with counting touching objects. The raw image cannot be thresholded at all: Otsu splits it into the bright and the dark half of the background.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.morphologyEx(src, cv2.MORPH_TOPHAT, kernel)`** = src − open(src); **`cv2.MORPH_BLACKHAT`** = close(src) − src. Both work on 8-bit, 16-bit and float images (results are non-negative for these definitions).

**Element**: `cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (k, k))` with k larger than the detail width; for large k on large images, rectangular elements are much faster (separable).

**After the transform**: `cv2.threshold` (often Otsu, 13.3) or a fixed threshold in grey levels above the background.

## Where it fails

- **Details as wide as the background structures**: no element separates them.
- **Large defects**: anything bigger than the element is treated as background and vanishes.
- **Noise and texture**: also small and bright/dark; they pass through.
- **Multiplicative illumination**: subtraction leaves contrast differences; divide (15.3) if amplitudes must be comparable.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Top-hat for dark details** (or black-hat for bright ones): wrong polarity, nothing found.
- **Element smaller than the details**: their centres disappear (recall drops).
- **Huge elements "to be safe"**: slow and they flatten real background structure into the result.
- **Thresholding the raw image afterwards** instead of the top-hat result.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Origin and theory

Meyer [1] introduced the top-hat transform for detecting cell nuclei and grains in microscope images. In Serra's framework it is a residual: the part of the image removed by a morphological filter. The family of residuals over increasing element sizes (a granulometry) measures the size distribution of bright or dark structures, used for particle sizing and texture analysis [2].

### Top-hat by reconstruction

Using opening by reconstruction (23.2 Go deeper) instead of a plain opening keeps the outlines of large bright structures exact, so they cancel completely and only the truly small details remain, with fewer artefacts at the borders of large objects.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Row 50, 60, 70, 140, 90, 100, 110 (a bright spike on a ramp). Compute the opening with a 1 × 3 element and the top-hat.

<Solution>

Erosion (min of 3): 50, 50, 60, 70, 90, 90, 100. Dilation of that (max of 3): 50, 60, 70, 90, 90, 100, 100. Top-hat = image − opening: 0, 0, 0, **50**, 0, 0, 10. The spike stands out at 50; the last pixel's 10 comes from the border.

### Exercise 2 (coding)

How sensitive is the black-hat result to the threshold? For the 15 × 15 black-hat of the print, compute F1 for fixed thresholds 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2
```
