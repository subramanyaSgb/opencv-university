# 22.5 Sub-pixel edges

<Definition term="Sub-pixel edge location">estimating the position of an edge with a precision finer than the pixel grid, using the grey values of several pixels across the edge. A blurred edge spreads over a few pixels, and the exact shape of that spread tells where the edge lies between pixel centres.</Definition>

Canny (22.4) marks whole pixels: an edge position is uncertain by up to half a pixel. For gauging, half a pixel can be the whole tolerance: at 0.05 mm per pixel it is 25 µm. Yet machine-vision tools routinely measure edges to 1/10 or 1/20 of a pixel. This is not magic: a pixel is not a point sample of a perfectly sharp edge; it averages the light over its area, and the optics blur the edge over two or three pixels (19.3). The grey values across the edge encode where the edge is.

## 1. An analogy

A thermometer with marks only every degree. If the liquid ends exactly at a mark, you read 20 °C. If it ends a third of the way to the next mark, you read 20.3 °C even though there is no mark there: you interpolate between the marks. Sub-pixel edge methods interpolate between pixels in the same way.

## 2. Three estimators

- **Parabola fit**: fit a parabola through the gradient magnitudes at the peak pixel k and its neighbours (a, b, c). Its vertex lies at k + ½ · (a − c) / (a − 2b + c). Example: gradients 40, 100, 70 → offset ½ · (40 − 70) / (40 − 200 + 70) = 0.17: the edge is 0.17 pixel to the right of the peak pixel.
- **Threshold (50 %) crossing**: find where the profile crosses the level halfway between the dark and bright plateaus, by linear interpolation between the two pixels around it.
- **Gradient centroid**: the weighted mean position of the gradient values in a small window (the centre of mass of the line spread function, 19.3).

Simulated edges (a step from 50 to 200 blurred with σ = 1, each pixel averaging its area) at true positions 19.0, 19.1, …, 19.9:

| Noise σ | Whole pixel (RMS error) | Parabola | 50 % crossing | Centroid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.292 | 0.022 | 0.010 | 0.008 |
| 2 | 0.298 | 0.066 | 0.043 | 0.044 |
| 5 | 0.326 | 0.153 | 0.108 | 0.114 |

Without noise every method reaches a few hundredths of a pixel; the whole-pixel answer is off by 0.29 on average, as expected for a uniform error of ±0.5. Noise limits the precision: with σ = 5 grey levels on an edge of 150, single-row estimates scatter by about a tenth of a pixel.

## 3. Averaging along the edge

A straight edge is measured in many rows at once. The errors of independent rows average out by √N: with noise σ = 5, single rows scatter with a standard deviation of 0.08 pixel; the mean of 50 rows is off by 0.003 pixel. This is why caliper tools measure over a band tens of pixels long, and why fitting a line to many edge points (26.4) is more precise than any single point.

For **corners**, `cv2.cornerSubPix` uses the same idea in 2-D: every gradient vector near a corner is perpendicular to the line from that pixel to the corner, and it solves for the point that best satisfies all of them. On a synthetic chessboard corner at (30.3, 29.6) it returns (30.33, 29.57).

## 4. What limits real precision

Sub-pixel precision (repeatability) is not the same as accuracy:

- **The edge model**: methods assume a symmetric blur; asymmetric PSFs, sharpening halos (19.1) or saturated pixels shift the estimate.
- **Illumination and the object**: a rounded or chamfered edge, a shadow or a reflection moves the visible edge away from the physical one.
- **Lens distortion and calibration** (41.3, 49.1): a pixel position becomes millimetres only through a calibrated model, which has its own error.
- **Pixel response non-uniformity and quantization**: limit the best case to roughly 1/50 to 1/100 of a pixel in practice.

Validate with a reference: measure a calibrated gauge block many times (repeatability) and compare with its certified size (accuracy). Chapter 49 covers measurement error analysis.

## 5. Industrial example

A shaft-diameter gauge with back-lighting images the shaft as a dark silhouette. Two caliper bands perpendicular to its sides each find 200 edge points with sub-pixel interpolation; a line fit to each side gives the two edges to about 1/20 of a pixel, and their distance, converted with the calibrated scale, gives the diameter. At 20 µm per pixel the measurement repeats within about 1 µm, well inside a typical tolerance of ±10 µm, even though each pixel is 20 µm wide.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Gradient values 60, 90, 90 at pixels 11, 12, 13. Where is the parabola vertex?

<Solution>

Peak pixel 12 (or 13, equal): offset ½ · (60 − 90) / (60 − 180 + 90) = ½ · (−30) / (−30) = 0.5 → **12.5**, exactly between the two equal values.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Single-row edge estimates scatter with σ = 0.1 px. How many rows are needed for σ = 0.01 px?

<Solution>

√N = 10 → **N = 100** rows (if their noise is independent).

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: the three estimators on simulated edges, then averaging rows and refining a corner.

### 1. Sub-pixel estimators

```python
import numpy as np
from math import erf

def edge_row(position, sigma=1.0, lo=50, hi=200, n=40):
    """A step at `position`, blurred with sigma, each pixel averaging its area (pixel i covers i-0.5 .. i+0.5)."""
    sub = np.linspace(-0.5, 0.5, 41)
    v = [np.mean([0.5 * (1 + erf((i + d - position) / (np.sqrt(2) * sigma))) for d in sub]) for i in range(n)]
    return lo + (hi - lo) * np.array(v)

def estimate(r):
    g = np.abs(np.convolve(r, [0.5, 0, -0.5], mode="same"))           # central difference
    g[0] = g[-1] = 0
    k = int(np.argmax(g))
    a, b, c = g[k - 1], g[k], g[k + 1]
    parabola = k + 0.5 * (a - c) / (a - 2 * b + c)
    half = (r[2] + r[-3]) / 2                                        # halfway between the plateaus
    j = int(np.nonzero(r > half)[0][0])
    crossing = j - 1 + (half - r[j - 1]) / (r[j] - r[j - 1])
    w = g[k - 3:k + 4]
    centroid = (w * np.arange(k - 3, k + 4)).sum() / w.sum()
    return k, parabola, crossing, centroid

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for noise in (0, 2, 5):
    errors = []
    for t in np.arange(19.0, 20.0, 0.1):
        for _ in range(20 if noise else 1):
            r = edge_row(t) + (rng.normal(0, noise, 40) if noise else 0)
            errors.append(np.array(estimate(r)) - t)
    rms = np.sqrt((np.array(errors) ** 2).mean(axis=0))
    print(f"noise {noise}: RMS error  pixel {rms[0]:.3f} | parabola {rms[1]:.3f} | 50% {rms[2]:.3f} | centroid {rms[3]:.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
noise 0: RMS error  pixel 0.292 | parabola 0.022 | 50% 0.010 | centroid 0.008
noise 2: RMS error  pixel 0.298 | parabola 0.066 | 50% 0.043 | centroid 0.044
noise 5: RMS error  pixel 0.326 | parabola 0.153 | 50% 0.108 | centroid 0.114
```

### 2. Averaging rows and refining a corner

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
t = 19.37
rows = [estimate(edge_row(t) + rng.normal(0, 5, 40))[2] for _ in range(50)]   # 50% crossing per row
print(f"single rows: std {np.std(np.array(rows) - t):.3f} px | mean of 50 rows: error {np.mean(rows) - t:+.3f} px")

cx, cy, S = 30.3, 29.6, 8                                            # true corner, supersampling factor
y, x = np.mgrid[0:60, 0:60].astype(np.float64)
cover = np.zeros((60, 60))
for dy in (np.arange(S) + 0.5) / S - 0.5:
    for dx in (np.arange(S) + 0.5) / S - 0.5:
        cover += ((x + dx) > cx) ^ ((y + dy) > cy)                   # chessboard quadrants
corner_img = (50 + 150 * cover / S ** 2).astype(np.float32)
start = np.array([[[30.0, 30.0]]], np.float32)
criteria = (cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_EPS | cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_COUNT, 50, 0.001)
refined = cv2.cornerSubPix(corner_img, start, (5, 5), (-1, -1), criteria)
print("cornerSubPix:", np.round(refined.ravel().astype(float), 3).tolist(), "| true:", [cx, cy])
```

Output:

```text
single rows: std 0.081 px | mean of 50 rows: error -0.003 px
cornerSubPix: [30.326, 29.574] | true: [30.3, 29.6]
```

- `cornerSubPix` modifies and returns the corner array; its window (5, 5) means an 11 × 11 search area.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.cornerSubPix(image, corners, winSize, zeroZone, criteria)`**: refines corners (chessboards, 41.4; Shi–Tomasi, 33.3) to sub-pixel accuracy; image 8-bit or float32, corners float32 N × 1 × 2.
- **Edges**: OpenCV has no sub-pixel Canny; take profiles with `cv2.remap` or `cv2.getRectSubPix` perpendicular to the edge, or refine Canny pixels with a parabola on the gradient magnitude along the gradient direction.
- **Line and circle fits**: `cv2.fitLine`, `cv2.fitEllipse` on many sub-pixel points (26.4).
- **`cv2.find4QuadCornerSubpix`**: another corner refinement used in calibration.

## Where it fails

- **Noise and low contrast**: precision degrades roughly in proportion to noise / contrast.
- **Very sharp edges** (no blur, pixel-aligned): a step that falls exactly on a pixel boundary gives no information about where inside the transition the edge lies; some optical blur is needed.
- **Nearby edges**: two edges closer than the blur width bias each other.
- **Wrong edge model**: halos, saturation, chamfers, shadows.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Reporting sub-pixel precision as accuracy** without a reference measurement.
- **Sharpening the image before sub-pixel measurement**: halos shift the gradient peak.
- **Profiles along rows when the edge is tilted**: measure perpendicular to the edge.
- **Mixing pixel conventions** (pixel centres at integers vs at +0.5) between tools.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Moment-based and fitting methods

Tabatabai and Mitchell (1984) [1] located edges from the first three grey-value moments of a profile, an exact method for an ideal blurred step. Model fitting (fitting an error-function profile with position, contrast and blur as parameters) reaches the noise-limited precision (the Cramér–Rao bound) and also returns the blur.

### Sub-pixel Canny

Devernay (1995) [2] added a parabola correction along the gradient direction to Canny's non-maximum suppression, with almost no extra cost; Trujillo-Pino et al. (2013) [3] derived a correction based on the partial area effect that also handles curved edges.

### Precision limit

For a blurred step with contrast C, noise σ and blur width w, the standard deviation of the best position estimate from one profile is roughly proportional to (σ / C) · √w. Contrast matters most, which is why gauging uses back-lighting.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A profile across an edge: 50, 52, 80, 160, 196, 200. Find the 50 % crossing by linear interpolation (plateaus 50 and 200).

<Solution>

Half level: 125. It lies between 80 (index 2) and 160 (index 3): 2 + (125 − 80) / (160 − 80) = 2 + 45/80 = **2.56**.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

How does the blur width affect precision? Repeat the noise σ = 2 experiment with edge blur σ = 0.5, 1 and 2 for the 50 % crossing estimator.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
for blur in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    errs = [estimate(edge_row(t, sigma=blur) + rng.normal(0, 2, 40))[2] - t
            for t in np.arange(19.0, 20.0, 0.1) for _ in range(20)]
    print(f"blur sigma {blur}: RMS error {np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(errs))):.3f} px")
```

Output:

```text
blur sigma 0.5: RMS error 0.040 px
blur sigma 1.0: RMS error 0.042 px
blur sigma 2.0: RMS error 0.073 px
```

Wider edges are measured less precisely: the transition is shallower, so the same noise moves the crossing further (0.073 px at σ = 2 against 0.04 px at σ = 0.5–1). Very sharp edges carry another risk not visible here: with almost no blur, the transition covers only one or two pixels and the interpolation becomes biased (Where it fails). A blur of about one pixel is a safe target for gauging optics.

</Solution>
