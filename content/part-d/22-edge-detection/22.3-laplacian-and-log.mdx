# 22.3 Laplacian and LoG

<Definition term="Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) edge detector">smooth the image with a Gaussian of σ, take the Laplacian (the sum of second derivatives, 19.2), and mark the pixels where the result changes sign. Those zero crossings lie on the edges, at the scale set by σ.</Definition>

22.1 showed two ways to locate an edge in a profile: the peak of the first derivative and the zero crossing of the second. Gradient methods (22.2, 22.4) use the first. Marr and Hildreth (1980) [1] built an edge detector on the second, using the Laplacian because it needs no direction: one rotation-invariant operator instead of two directional ones. It also connects edges to blob detection (20.4, 25.3).

## 1. An analogy

Driving over a hill: going up, the road curves upwards (you are pressed into the seat), then near the top it starts curving downwards (you feel lighter). The point where the curvature changes sign is the steepest part of the slope. The LoG finds edges as the places where the brightness "curvature" changes sign.

On the dark side of an edge the Laplacian is positive (its neighbours are brighter on average), on the bright side negative. A worked example with the 4-neighbour Laplacian on the dark corner pixel of a bright region:

<PixelMatrix values={[[50, 50, 50], [50, 50, 150], [50, 150, 150]]} highlight={[[1, 1]]} />

∇² = 50 + 150 + 50 + 150 − 4 · 50 = **200**: strongly positive. The bright pixel diagonally below-right has a strongly negative value; between them the sign changes, and that is where the edge is.

Real numbers: σ²·LoG with σ = 2 along row 100 of the scene, across the left edge of the white disc (columns 30–39):

```text
4.3, 14.2, 29.4, 40.4, 33.7, 6.1, −27.1, −45.0, −41.5, −26.7
```

Positive on the dark side, negative on the bright side, crossing zero between columns 35 and 36.

## 3. Too many zero crossings

Every tiny wobble of noise also changes the sign of the Laplacian. Counting zero-crossing pixels on the scene:

| σ | All sign changes | Jump across the crossing > 4 | Jump > 10 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 34 049 | 6 904 | 1 506 |
| 2 | 17 663 | 1 422 | 1 249 |
| 4 | 7 435 | 1 218 | 673 |

(For comparison, `cv2.Canny(scene, 50, 150)` marks 1 490 pixels.) Without a slope test, half the image is "edge": flat regions with noise cross zero everywhere. Requiring a minimum jump between the positive and negative side keeps only real edges. Larger σ removes fine structure and noise; at σ = 4 even parts of the letters disappear.

## 4. Properties and caveats

- **Closed contours**: zero crossings of a smooth function form closed curves (or end at the image border), which is attractive for outlining objects; but they also close around every noise blob.
- **Curved edges and corners move**: for a straight edge the zero crossing lies exactly on the step; on curved edges it is displaced by a fraction of a pixel, by an amount that depends on σ and the curvature, and corners are rounded off. Along row 100 of the noisy scene the steepest grey-value step lies at 34.5 and the zero crossing at 35.2; on a clean synthetic disc (Exercise 2) the displacement is up to about half a pixel. For precise measurement, check the bias on your own geometry.
- **No direction**: the LoG does not give an edge orientation; take the gradient if you need it.
- **One operator for edges and blobs**: the same filter detects edges (zero crossings) and blobs (extrema, 20.4, 25.3).

In practice Canny (22.4) gives thinner, better-localised and less noisy edges for the same effort, and is the default; the LoG survives in blob detection, in sharpening, and as a focus measure.

## 5. Industrial example

LoG filtering is used to find small round features, such as solder paste deposits, holes in perforated sheet, or particles on a filter: their extrema at the matching σ are strong and the response is the same in every direction. As an edge detector it is used on images of objects with smooth, closed outlines (cells, pellets) where closed contours are wanted, with a slope threshold to suppress the background.

**Q2.** Why multiply the LoG by σ²?

<Solution>

The Laplacian of a blurred image gets weaker as σ grows; σ² normalises it (20.4) so that the same slope threshold works at every scale.

## Code

Two examples: the LoG and its zero crossings, then the shift of a curved edge.

### 1. LoG zero crossings

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

scene = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE).astype(np.float32)

def log_response(img, sigma):
    return cv2.Laplacian(cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (0, 0), sigma), cv2.CV_32F) * sigma ** 2

def zero_crossings(L, min_jump):
    a, right, down = L[:-1, :-1], L[:-1, 1:], L[1:, :-1]
    zc = ((a * right < 0) & (np.abs(a - right) > min_jump)) | ((a * down < 0) & (np.abs(a - down) > min_jump))
    return zc

for sigma in (1, 2, 4):
    L = log_response(scene, sigma)
    print(f"sigma {sigma}: all {int(zero_crossings(L, 0).sum()):6d} | jump > 4 {int(zero_crossings(L, 4).sum()):5d} | jump > 10 {int(zero_crossings(L, 10).sum()):5d}")
print("Canny(50, 150):", int((cv2.Canny(scene.astype(np.uint8), 50, 150) > 0).sum()))
```

Output:

```text
sigma 1: all  34049 | jump > 4  6904 | jump > 10  1506
sigma 2: all  17663 | jump > 4  1422 | jump > 10  1249
sigma 4: all   7435 | jump > 4  1218 | jump > 10   673
Canny(50, 150): 1490
```

- `cv2.Laplacian` uses the 4-neighbour kernel for `ksize=1` (the default); `ksize=3` gives a different 3 × 3 kernel (19.2).

### 2. Where the zero crossing lies

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

row = scene[100, 30:40]
step = int(np.argmax(np.diff(row))) + 30                            # largest grey-value step: between step and step + 1
L = log_response(scene, 2)[100, 30:40]
k = int(np.nonzero(np.sign(L[:-1]) != np.sign(L[1:]))[0][0])
zero = 30 + k + L[k] / (L[k] - L[k + 1])                             # linear interpolation
print("sigma^2 LoG:", np.round(L.astype(float), 1).tolist())
print(f"steepest step at {step + 0.5:.1f}, LoG zero crossing at {zero:.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
sigma^2 LoG: [4.3, 14.2, 29.4, 40.4, 33.7, 6.1, -27.1, -45.0, -41.5, -26.7]
steepest step at 34.5, LoG zero crossing at 35.18
```

## OpenCV API notes

OpenCV has no LoG or zero-crossing function; combine:

- **`cv2.GaussianBlur(src, (0, 0), sigma)`** then **`cv2.Laplacian(src, cv2.CV_32F, ksize=1)`** (or `ksize=3, 5` for larger, smoother Laplacian kernels).
- Zero crossings with NumPy (sign changes between neighbours plus a slope test), as in example 1.
- A LoG kernel can also be built explicitly and applied with `cv2.filter2D`, or approximated by a difference of Gaussians (20.4).

## Where it fails

- **Noise**: many spurious zero crossings without a slope threshold.
- **Corners and curved edges**: rounded and shifted.
- **Thin lines**: a line produces two zero crossings, one on each side, not one at the line.
- **No orientation information**.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Laplacian without smoothing** on noisy images.
- **Thresholding the LoG magnitude** instead of finding zero crossings: thick, double edges.
- **Uint8 output depth**: the negative half is lost and no zero crossings remain.
- **Comparing thresholds across σ** without σ² normalisation.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Marr–Hildreth theory

Marr and Hildreth [1] argued that intensity changes occur at many scales and should be detected separately at each, with zero crossings of ∇²G; edges that appear at several neighbouring scales are physically meaningful. Their model connected to the centre-surround receptive fields of the retina (20.4).

### Why the zero crossing moves

For a straight edge, the LoG zero crossing lies exactly at the step. For a curved edge, the Laplacian also contains the curvature of the iso-brightness lines, which displaces the zero crossing; Berzins (1984) [2] derived the localisation error for corners and curved edges as a function of σ. Gradient-maximum methods (Canny) have biases at curves too, generally smaller.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Measure the shift of the LoG zero crossing on a synthetic disc of radius 20 (value 200 on 50) along its horizontal diameter, for σ = 1, 2 and 4, compared with the true edge at radius 20.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

disc = np.full((101, 101), 50, np.float32)
cv2.circle(disc, (50, 50), 20, 200, -1, cv2.LINE_AA)
for sigma in (1, 2, 4):
    L = log_response(disc, sigma)[50, 50:80]                         # from the centre outwards
    k = int(np.nonzero((np.sign(L[:-1]) != np.sign(L[1:])) & (np.abs(L[:-1] - L[1:]) > 1))[0][0])   # ignore rounding wiggles
    zero = k + L[k] / (L[k] - L[k + 1])
    print(f"sigma {sigma}: zero crossing at radius {zero:.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
sigma 1: zero crossing at radius 20.48
sigma 2: zero crossing at radius 19.90
sigma 4: zero crossing at radius 20.16
```

The anti-aliased disc edge lies at about 20.5 (the pixel centre at 20 is still inside). The zero crossing is close to it at σ = 1 and displaced by 0.3–0.6 pixel at σ = 2 and 4, not in a simple monotonic way: the bias depends on how the Gaussian's width compares with the radius. A sub-pixel measurement with LoG needs this kind of check.

</Solution>
