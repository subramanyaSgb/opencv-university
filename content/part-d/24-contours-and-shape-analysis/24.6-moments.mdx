# 24.6 Moments

<Definition term="Image moments">weighted sums over a shape's pixels: m_pq = Σ x^p · y^q (times the pixel value, for grey images). The zeroth moment is the area, the first moments give the centroid, the second moments describe the spread: orientation and elongation. Normalised combinations of them do not change when the shape moves, grows or turns.</Definition>

24.3 used m00, m10 and m01 for the area and centroid. The same function returns more: second- and third-order moments that describe how a shape's mass is distributed. They are cheap (one pass over the contour or the pixels), give a sub-pixel orientation for any blob, and are the basis of invariant shape descriptors such as Hu moments (29.2).

## 1. An analogy

Balancing a flat cut-out shape on a pencil point. The point where it balances is the centroid (first moments). Spinning it around different axes through that point, it turns most easily around its long axis and resists most around the short one: the second moments measure that resistance, the "moment of inertia" of physics, and its easy axis is the shape's orientation.

## 2. Raw, central, normalised

A worked example: four pixels at (x, y) = (1, 1), (2, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2), a small diagonal step.

- m00 = 4 (area); m10 = 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 8, m01 = 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 6 → centroid (**2, 1.5**).
- μ20 = Σ(x − 2)² = 1 + 0 + 0 + 1 = **2**; μ02 = Σ(y − 1.5)² = 4 · 0.25 = **1**; μ11 = Σ(x − 2)(y − 1.5) = 0.5 + 0 + 0 + 0.5 = **1**.
- Orientation θ = ½ · atan2(2μ11, μ20 − μ02) = ½ · atan2(2, 1) = **31.7°**: the pixels run down and to the right (y points down), at about 30°.

The second central moments form a covariance matrix [[μ20, μ11], [μ11, μ02]] / m00 (5.4, 10). Its eigenvectors are the shape's principal axes; for a filled ellipse, 4 · √eigenvalue is exactly its full axis length. That defines the "equivalent ellipse" of any shape.

On the parts:

| Part | Orientation from moments | Equivalent-ellipse axes (4√λ) | Elongation √(λ1/λ2) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oval (drawn −15°, axes 76 × 44) | −14.76° | 76.1 × 43.9 | 1.73 |
| Rotated bar (drawn 25°, 90 × 34) | 25.29° | 103.3 × 39.3 | 2.63 |
| L-bracket | −60.03° | 97.7 × 51.3 | 1.91 |
| Washer | (undefined) | 74.9 × 74.9 | 1.00 |

For the oval the moments recover the drawn ellipse exactly. For the bar, the orientation is right, and the axes are those of the ellipse with the same spread (a rectangle of length L has 4σ = 1.155 L), which is why they are larger than the bar. For a round shape the orientation is undefined: both eigenvalues are equal.

## 3. Invariance

| Moments | Change with | Do not change with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw m_pq | position, size, rotation | — |
| Central μ_pq | size, rotation | position |
| Normalised ν_pq | rotation | position, size |
| Hu moments h1…h7 (29.2) | — (h7 changes sign under mirroring) | position, size, rotation |

Doubling the star's size multiplies its μ20 by 16 (181 355 → 2 901 674), but its ν20 stays 0.09776. Hu (1962) [1] combined the normalised moments of orders 2 and 3 into seven values that are also invariant to rotation: h1 = ν20 + ν02 is the simplest (the "spread" relative to area).

## 4. Industrial example

Moment-based orientation is the standard way to align blobs before further processing: a part is rotated so that its principal axis is horizontal before OCR or before comparing it with a template, and the angle goes to the robot. The equivalent ellipse's axes and elongation classify particles (round, elongated, fibrous) in powder and grain analysis. Hu moments, combined with area, separate a handful of part types on a sorting conveyor without training a classifier (29.2, 29.4).

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Three pixels at (0, 0), (1, 0), (2, 0). Compute the centroid, μ20, μ02 and the orientation.

<Solution>

Centroid (1, 0). μ20 = 1 + 0 + 1 = **2**, μ02 = **0**, μ11 = 0. θ = ½ atan2(0, 2) = **0°**: a horizontal line.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why is the orientation of a square undefined from second moments?

<Solution>

For a square μ20 = μ02 and μ11 = 0: the covariance is a multiple of the identity, all directions are principal axes, and atan2(0, 0) has no meaning.

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: moments by hand, then orientation and invariance on the parts.

### 1. Moments by hand

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

a = np.zeros((4, 5), np.uint8)
for x, y in [(1, 1), (2, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2)]:
    a[y, x] = 1
m = cv2.moments(a, binaryImage=True)
cx, cy = m["m10"] / m["m00"], m["m01"] / m["m00"]
theta = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * m["mu11"], m["mu20"] - m["mu02"]))
print("m00, m10, m01:", m["m00"], m["m10"], m["m01"], "| centroid:", (cx, cy))
print("mu20, mu02, mu11:", m["mu20"], m["mu02"], m["mu11"], f"| orientation {theta:.1f} deg")

ys, xs = np.nonzero(a)
print("check mu20 with NumPy:", float(((xs - cx) ** 2).sum()))
```

Output:

```text
m00, m10, m01: 4.0 8.0 6.0 | centroid: (2.0, 1.5)
mu20, mu02, mu11: 2.0 1.0 1.0 | orientation 31.7 deg
check mu20 with NumPy: 2.0
```

### 2. Orientation, axes and invariance

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

parts = cv2.imread("sample-parts.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
contours, _ = cv2.findContours(parts, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_NONE)
for name, i in [("oval", 0), ("bar", 2), ("L-bracket", 3), ("washer", 4)]:
    m = cv2.moments(contours[i])
    theta = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * m["mu11"], m["mu20"] - m["mu02"]))
    cov = np.array([[m["mu20"], m["mu11"]], [m["mu11"], m["mu02"]]]) / m["m00"]
    small, large = np.linalg.eigvalsh(cov)
    print(f"{name:9s} orientation {theta:7.2f} deg | axes {4 * np.sqrt(large):5.1f} x {4 * np.sqrt(small):5.1f} | elongation {np.sqrt(large / small):.2f}")

star = contours[1]
m1, m2 = cv2.moments(star), cv2.moments(star * 2)                    # the same star, twice as large
print(f"star mu20: {m1['mu20']:.0f} -> {m2['mu20']:.0f} | nu20: {m1['nu20']:.5f} -> {m2['nu20']:.5f}")
print("Hu moments of the star:", [float(f"{v:.4g}") for v in cv2.HuMoments(m1).ravel()[:3]])
```

Output:

```text
oval      orientation  -14.76 deg | axes  76.1 x  43.9 | elongation 1.73
bar       orientation   25.29 deg | axes 103.3 x  39.3 | elongation 2.63
L-bracket orientation  -60.03 deg | axes  97.7 x  51.3 | elongation 1.91
washer    orientation    0.00 deg | axes  74.9 x  74.9 | elongation 1.00
star mu20: 181355 -> 2901674 | nu20: 0.09776 -> 0.09776
Hu moments of the star: [0.1966, 1.215e-06, 3.195e-06]
```

- The washer's "0.00 deg" is meaningless (equal eigenvalues). Check the elongation before trusting an orientation.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.moments(array, binaryImage=False)`** → dict with `m00…m03`, `mu20…mu03`, `nu20…nu03`:
- On a **contour** (N × 1 × 2 points): exact moments of the polygon (Green's theorem); fast, ignores holes.
- On an **image** (8-bit or float, single channel): sums over all pixels weighted by value; `binaryImage=True` counts every non-zero pixel as 1. Holes are excluded automatically.
- Contour moments and pixel moments differ slightly (the polygon runs through pixel centres, 24.3).

**`cv2.HuMoments(moments)`** → 7 × 1 array of Hu invariants (29.2). Often used as −sign(h)·log10|h| because their magnitudes span many orders.

## Where it fails

- **Round or symmetric shapes**: orientation undefined or unstable (squares, regular stars).
- **Noise and attached debris**: moments weigh distant pixels heavily (x², x³); a speck far from the part shifts the orientation.
- **Higher orders**: third and higher moments are very sensitive to noise and digitisation.
- **Holes with contour moments**: the outer contour's moments ignore holes; use image moments or subtract the holes' moments.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Forgetting y points down**: angles come out mirrored compared with a maths-style y-up convention.
- **Dividing by m00 = 0**.
- **Comparing raw Hu values** without the log transform: tiny numbers dominated by the first one.
- **Using contour moments for parts with holes** when the holes matter.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Moment invariants

Hu [1] derived seven invariants from algebraic invariants of ν20…ν03. Flusser and Suk (1993) showed that Hu's set is incomplete and dependent, and derived complete sets of rotation invariants, as well as affine moment invariants that also survive shear and non-uniform scaling [2].

### Orthogonal moments

Zernike and Legendre moments use orthogonal polynomials instead of x^p y^q: they are less redundant, more robust to noise and allow reconstruction of the shape from a finite set. Zernike moments are rotation-invariant by construction and are used in OCR and logo recognition (Teague 1980 [3]).

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Pixels at (0, 0), (0, 1), (0, 2), (0, 3) (a vertical line). Orientation from moments, and the elongation?

<Solution>

Centroid (0, 1.5); μ20 = 0, μ02 = 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 = 5, μ11 = 0. θ = ½ atan2(0, −5) = **90°** (vertical). Eigenvalues 5/4 and 0: elongation **infinite**, a perfect line.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Rotate the oval part around its centroid by 40° (warpAffine on the mask), compute the orientation from image moments before and after, and the first three Hu moments before and after.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

mask = np.zeros_like(parts)
cv2.drawContours(mask, contours, 0, 255, cv2.FILLED)                  # the oval
m = cv2.moments(mask, binaryImage=True)
c = (m["m10"] / m["m00"], m["m01"] / m["m00"])
rotated = cv2.warpAffine(mask, cv2.getRotationMatrix2D(c, 40, 1.0), (mask.shape[1], mask.shape[0]))
for name, img in [("original", mask), ("rotated", rotated)]:
    mm = cv2.moments(img, binaryImage=True)
    theta = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * mm["mu11"], mm["mu20"] - mm["mu02"]))
    print(f"{name:8s} orientation {theta:7.2f} | Hu {[float(f'{v:.4g}') for v in cv2.HuMoments(mm).ravel()[:3]]}")
```

Output:

```text
original orientation  -14.90 | Hu [0.183, 0.008155, 4.223e-08]
rotated  orientation  -54.84 | Hu [0.1826, 0.008009, 3.102e-08]
```

The orientation changes by 39.9° (warpAffine's positive angle turns the image counter-clockwise as displayed, which is −40° in y-down coordinates). h1 and h2 change by less than 2 %: the residue comes from resampling the mask on the pixel grid, not from the invariants. h3, a third-order invariant that is almost zero for a symmetric oval, changes by a quarter, because tiny resampling errors dominate it: higher-order moments are fragile.

</Solution>
