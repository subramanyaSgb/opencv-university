# 21.4 Multi-camera and thermal-visible fusion

<Definition term="Multimodal registration and fusion">aligning images from different cameras or different kinds of sensors (visible, thermal, near-infrared, depth, X-ray) so that each pixel refers to the same scene point, then combining them into one picture or one set of measurements. The difficulty: the two images can look completely different, so their grey values cannot simply be compared.</Definition>

A thermal camera shows where a motor, a bearing or an electrical joint is hot; a visible camera shows which part it is and where its label is. Combined, they answer "which component is overheating". The same need arises with a colour camera and a depth sensor, two inspection cameras looking at one part from different sides, or a camera and an X-ray image. Part I treats thermal imaging in depth (46); this chapter covers the alignment.

## 1. An analogy

Two people sketch the same street, one from a window on the first floor and one from the pavement, one in pencil and one in watercolour. Their drawings differ in style and in viewpoint. To overlay them you cannot match shades of grey to shades of colour; you match *where things are*: the corners of buildings, the edges of windows, the line of the kerb.

## 2. Two sources of difference

**Geometry.** Two cameras side by side see the scene from points a baseline B apart. A point at depth Z appears shifted between them by the disparity d = f·B / Z (f in pixels, 42.1). Example: f = 800 px, B = 50 mm:

| Depth Z | Disparity d |
| --- | --- |
| 1.0 m | 40.0 px |
| 2.0 m | 20.0 px |
| 2.2 m | 18.2 px |
| 5.0 m | 8.0 px |

A single transform (a homography, 16.3) aligns the images exactly only for one depth (or one plane). Calibrated for 2 m, an object at 2.2 m is misaligned by 1.8 pixels, at 1 m by 20 pixels. Keep the baseline small (co-located lenses or a beam splitter), work at a fixed distance, or use depth (stereo, 42) for a per-pixel correction.

**Appearance.** In a thermal image, hot objects are bright, whatever their colour; a bright white surface in visible light can be cold and dark in thermal, and a black surface hot. The relationship between the two images' grey values can be non-linear, inverted, or absent. Methods that compare grey values directly (SSD, the correlation coefficient of ECC) then fail.

## 3. Registering on structure

Three ways to compare images that look different:

| Measure | What it compares | Handles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Correlation of gradient magnitudes | where edges are, not their sign | inversion, non-linear mappings |
| Mutual information (MI) | how well one image's grey value *predicts* the other's, any relationship | any consistent mapping, including many-to-one |
| Features (edges, corners, line segments) | geometry of structures | large motions; descriptors may fail across modalities |

On a thermal-like version of the scene (inverted, non-linear, blurred, with noise), rotated by 2° and shifted by (5.5, −3.0):

| Method | Result |
| --- | --- |
| ECC on grey values | correlation at the true alignment is **−0.94**; ECC does not converge (OpenCV raises an error) |
| ECC on the inverted thermal image | works here: (5.47, −2.97), because this mapping happens to be monotonic |
| ECC on gradient magnitudes | angle 2.009°, shift (5.50, −3.01): correct to 0.01 pixel |

Inverting works only if you know the mapping is monotonic and decreasing; real thermal/visible pairs are not that simple. Gradient magnitudes ignore the sign and size of the brightness change and keep its position: the robust default. Mutual information is the classic measure for medical multimodal registration (CT/MRI/PET, Viola and Wells, Maes et al. [1, 2]): on the aligned pair it is 1.74, and it falls steadily with misalignment (about 1.72 at 1 px, 1.67 at 2 px, 1.58 at 4 px, 1.46 at 8 px), so maximising it finds the alignment without assuming any grey-value relationship.

## 4. Calibration in practice and fusion

For a fixed two-camera rig, registration is usually done once, with a target visible in both modalities:

- **Thermal-visible targets**: a checkerboard of materials with different emissivity (black paint on polished metal), or a board with heated or cooled squares, or small lamps/resistors at known positions. Corners or centres are found in both images, and a homography (planar scene, fixed distance) or a full stereo calibration (41.8) is computed.
- **At run time**: warp one image into the other's frame with the stored transform. Refine per frame with gradient-based ECC only if the rig moves or the working distance varies.

Fusion for display is then simple: colour-map the aligned thermal image (inferno, 11.4) and blend it with the visible luminance, or show it only where the temperature exceeds a threshold. For analysis, keep the two aligned images separate and read temperatures in regions defined on the visible image (46.5).

## 5. Industrial example

Electrical cabinet and switchgear monitoring combines a thermal camera with a visible camera in one housing: hot spots are found in the thermal image, and the visible image shows which terminal, breaker or cable they belong to, with labels readable. Because both lenses sit a few centimetres apart and the cabinet is at a known distance, a factory-calibrated homography aligns them; for measurements at other distances, the parallax error (Section 2) is the main source of misplaced hot spots. Similar dual setups are used for furnace and kiln monitoring, battery production lines and food processing.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** f = 600 px, baseline 30 mm, calibrated at 1.5 m. What is the misalignment of an object at 1.2 m?

<Solution>

d(1.5 m) = 600 · 0.03 / 1.5 = 12 px; d(1.2 m) = 600 · 0.03 / 1.2 = 15 px. Misalignment **3 pixels**.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why does mutual information work when the thermal image is the inverted, non-linear version of the visible one?

<Solution>

MI measures how predictable one image's value is from the other's at the same pixel, whatever the function between them. When aligned, each visible grey value goes with one narrow range of thermal values: high MI. Misaligned, the pairs are mixed and the joint histogram spreads out: low MI.

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: registration of a thermal-like image, then fusion for display.

### 1. Multimodal registration

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

vis = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE).astype(np.float32)
theta = np.deg2rad(2)
M = np.float32([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta), 5.5], [np.sin(theta), np.cos(theta), -3.0]])
ir = 255 * (1 - (vis / 255) ** 0.6) * 0.8 + 30                      # thermal-like: inverted, non-linear
ir = cv2.GaussianBlur(ir, (0, 0), 1.5)
ir = cv2.warpAffine(ir, M, (320, 200), flags=cv2.INTER_LINEAR, borderMode=cv2.BORDER_REPLICATE)
ir = (ir + np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 3, ir.shape)).astype(np.float32)
criteria = (cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_EPS | cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_COUNT, 200, 1e-6)

try:
    cv2.findTransformECC(vis, ir, np.eye(2, 3, dtype=np.float32), cv2.MOTION_EUCLIDEAN, criteria, None, 5)
    print("ECC on grey values: converged")
except cv2.error:
    print("ECC on grey values: failed to converge")

def grad_mag(x):
    x = cv2.GaussianBlur(x, (0, 0), 1.5)
    return cv2.magnitude(cv2.Sobel(x, cv2.CV_32F, 1, 0), cv2.Sobel(x, cv2.CV_32F, 0, 1))

cc, W = cv2.findTransformECC(grad_mag(vis), grad_mag(ir), np.eye(2, 3, dtype=np.float32), cv2.MOTION_EUCLIDEAN, criteria, None, 5)
print(f"ECC on gradients: rho {cc:.3f} | angle {np.degrees(np.arctan2(W[1, 0], W[0, 0])):.3f} deg | shift ({W[0, 2]:.2f}, {W[1, 2]:.2f})")

def mutual_information(a, b, bins=32):
    h, _, _ = np.histogram2d(a.ravel(), b.ravel(), bins=bins)
    p = h / h.sum()
    pa, pb = p.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True), p.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    nz = p > 0
    return float((p[nz] * np.log(p[nz] / (pa @ pb)[nz])).sum())

aligned = cv2.warpAffine(ir, W, (320, 200), flags=cv2.INTER_LINEAR | cv2.WARP_INVERSE_MAP)
inner = (slice(10, -10), slice(10, -10))
for dx in (0, 2, 8):
    shifted = np.roll(aligned, dx, axis=1)
    print(f"misaligned by {dx} px: MI {mutual_information(vis[inner], shifted[inner]):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
ECC on grey values: failed to converge
ECC on gradients: rho 0.946 | angle 2.009 deg | shift (5.50, -3.01)
misaligned by 0 px: MI 1.742
misaligned by 2 px: MI 1.674
misaligned by 8 px: MI 1.455
```

- Gradient magnitude after a small blur is a good "modality-free" image: it keeps where structures are and discards their polarity.
- MI is computed from the joint histogram of the two images; 32 bins is a common choice for 8-bit data.

### 2. Fusion for display

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

heat = cv2.normalize(aligned, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX).astype(np.uint8)
colour = cv2.applyColorMap(heat, cv2.COLORMAP_INFERNO)
visible_bgr = cv2.cvtColor(vis.astype(np.uint8), cv2.COLOR_GRAY2BGR)
fused = cv2.addWeighted(visible_bgr, 0.5, colour, 0.5, 0)
hot = heat > 200                                                     # show only the "hottest" areas in colour
highlight = visible_bgr.copy()
highlight[hot] = colour[hot]
print("fused", fused.shape, fused.dtype, "| hot pixels:", int(hot.sum()))
```

Output:

```text
fused (200, 320, 3) uint8 | hot pixels: 8921
```

- In a real system `aligned` holds temperatures (46.3); normalise with fixed limits (for example 20–80 °C) rather than min–max so that colours mean the same temperature in every frame.

## OpenCV API notes

- **Calibration of a camera pair**: `cv2.findChessboardCorners` / `cv2.findCirclesGrid` in both images (thermal targets need emissivity contrast), then `cv2.findHomography` (planar, fixed distance) or `cv2.stereoCalibrate` (41.8).
- **Registration**: `cv2.findTransformECC` on gradient images; `cv2.Sobel`, `cv2.magnitude`.
- **Mutual information**: no OpenCV function; compute it from `np.histogram2d` (or `cv2.calcHist` with two channels) as above.
- **Warping**: `cv2.warpPerspective` / `cv2.warpAffine`; `cv2.remap` for per-pixel maps.
- **Fusion**: `cv2.applyColorMap`, `cv2.addWeighted`, masks with `np.where` or `cv2.copyTo`.

## Where it fails

- **Depth variation with a large baseline**: one homography cannot align near and far objects.
- **Different resolutions and blur**: thermal cameras often have far fewer pixels and softer images; register at the coarser resolution.
- **Missing structure**: an edge visible in one modality may be absent in the other (a cold label on a cold box); register on structures present in both.
- **Thermal reflections**: shiny metal reflects heat from elsewhere, producing edges that do not exist in the visible image.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Running ECC or SSD on raw thermal and visible images**.
- **Calibrating at one distance and measuring at another** without accounting for parallax.
- **Using a printed paper checkerboard for thermal calibration**: it is nearly invisible in thermal; use emissivity or temperature contrast.
- **Min–max normalising temperatures for display**: colours change meaning from frame to frame.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Mutual information registration

Viola and Wells [1] and Maes et al. [2] introduced mutual information for multimodal medical registration in 1995–1997. Normalised MI (Studholme et al.) reduces the bias from changing overlap. Optimising MI needs smooth histogram estimates (Parzen windows, partial-volume interpolation) and a search strategy that tolerates its local maxima.

### Learned and descriptor-based alignment

Hand-crafted descriptors (SIFT, ORB) often fail across modalities because gradient directions flip. Modality-independent descriptors such as MIND (Heinrich et al., 2012) [3] compare local self-similarity patterns, which survive inversion and non-linear mappings; learned cross-modal feature matchers do the same with networks.

### Optical co-alignment

Some dual cameras avoid parallax physically: a beam splitter (a germanium or dichroic window) sends visible and infrared light from one aperture to two sensors, so both see from the same point and one homography is exact at all depths.

</GoDeeper>

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A thermal camera has 640 × 480 pixels and a 32° horizontal field of view; the visible camera has 1920 × 1080 and 60°. How many visible pixels correspond to one thermal pixel horizontally (centre of the image, small-angle approximation)?

<Solution>

Thermal: 32° / 640 = 0.05° per pixel. Visible: 60° / 1920 = 0.03125° per pixel. One thermal pixel covers 0.05 / 0.03125 = **1.6 visible pixels**. The thermal image must be upsampled by 1.6 (plus the geometric warp) to overlay it; its detail stays coarser.

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Measure the parallax problem: compute the disparity for depths of 0.5 to 5 m (f = 800 px, B = 50 mm), and the misalignment relative to a calibration at 2 m. Up to which nearer and farther depths is the error below 2 pixels?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

f, B, Zc = 800, 0.05, 2.0
Z = np.round(np.arange(0.5, 5.01, 0.1), 2)
err = np.abs(f * B / Z - f * B / Zc)
ok = Z[err < 2]
print(f"error at 0.5 m: {err[0]:.1f} px | at 5 m: {err[-1]:.1f} px")
print(f"error below 2 px from {ok.min():.1f} m to {ok.max():.1f} m")
```

Output:

```text
error at 0.5 m: 60.0 px | at 5 m: 12.0 px
error below 2 px from 1.9 m to 2.2 m
```

With a 50 mm baseline, a single calibration holds to 2 pixels only within about ±0.2 m of the calibration distance. Smaller baselines or depth-dependent correction are needed for scenes with depth.

</Solution>
