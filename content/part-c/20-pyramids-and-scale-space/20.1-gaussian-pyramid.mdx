# 20.1 Gaussian pyramid

<Definition term="Gaussian pyramid">a stack of copies of an image, each half the width and half the height of the one before, made by blurring with a small Gaussian and then keeping every second row and column. The bottom level is the original; the top is a tiny thumbnail.</Definition>

Objects appear at different sizes: a part close to the camera is large, the same part far away is small. A detector tuned to one size misses the other. Searching a large image for a template is slow. Both problems become easier if the image exists at several resolutions at once. Burt and Adelson's pyramids (1983) [1] are the classic way to build them, and they are inside many OpenCV functions: optical flow (40.3), template matching speed-ups (32.3), feature detectors (33, 34) and blending (20.3).

## 1. An analogy

A map atlas: a world map, a country map, a city map, a street map. Each is a smaller-scale version of the next, and each leaves out details too small to show at its scale. To find a street, you start on the country map, then zoom in, instead of scanning street maps of the whole world.

## 2. Blur, then halve

A worked example on one row (the same blur runs down the columns too). Row 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80; the outputs come from pixels 0, 2, 4, 6:

| Output | Window (reflected at the border) | Weighted sum / 16 | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 30, 20, **10**, 20, 30 | (30 + 80 + 60 + 80 + 30) / 16 = 17.5 | 18 |
| 1 | 10, 20, **30**, 40, 50 | (10 + 80 + 180 + 160 + 50) / 16 = 30 | 30 |
| 2 | 30, 40, **50**, 60, 70 | 800 / 16 = 50 | 50 |
| 3 | 50, 60, **70**, 80, 70 | (50 + 240 + 420 + 320 + 70) / 16 = 68.75 | 69 |

`cv2.pyrDown` gives exactly 18, 30, 50, 69. In the middle of a straight ramp the blur changes nothing; at the borders the reflection pulls the values in.

Sizes for the 320 × 200 scene: 320 × 200, 160 × 100, 80 × 50, 40 × 25, 20 × 13 (odd sizes round up). All levels together hold 1.33 times the pixels of the original: 1 + 1/4 + 1/16 + … < 4/3. A pyramid is cheap.

## 3. Why blur first

Keeping every second pixel halves the sampling rate, so the highest frequency the new level can represent halves too (Nyquist, 5.4). Anything finer must be removed *before* subsampling, or it aliases into false patterns. On the zone plate, whose rings get finer towards the corners, the standard deviation in the top-left corner region (where the rings are finest) shows it:

| Level | Size | Blur + subsample (pyrDown) | Subsample only |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 320 × 200 | 90.2 | 90.2 |
| 1 | 160 × 100 | 8.8 | 90.4 |
| 2 | 80 × 50 | 0.1 | 92.8 |
| 3 | 40 × 25 | 0.0 | 89.5 |

With the blur, rings that are too fine for the level fade to flat grey (std near 0): correct, the detail is simply absent at that scale. Without it, they stay at full contrast as moiré rings of the wrong frequency: false detail that was never in the scene.

## 4. Coarse to fine

The main use is search. Matching a 64 × 64 template over a 1024 × 1024 image tests about a million positions. At level 3 both are 8 times smaller in each direction: about 15 000 positions with an 8 × 8 template, roughly 4 000 times less work. The best match found there is refined at level 2 in a small neighbourhood, then level 1, then level 0. The same coarse-to-fine idea is used by pyramidal Lucas–Kanade optical flow (`cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK`, 40.3), ECC registration (21.3) and many feature detectors, which detect at every level to find features of every size.

## 5. Industrial example

A pick-and-place or label-verification system must locate a part anywhere in a 5-megapixel image within a few milliseconds. It searches for the part on level 3 or 4 of a pyramid, where the image is a few hundred pixels wide, and refines the position on finer levels only around the candidate. The same pyramid gives a fast preview for the operator screen (the level that fits the display) and is reused by every stage that needs multi-scale data, so it is built once per frame.

## Code

Two examples: pyrDown by hand and with OpenCV, then a pyramid with and without the blur.

### 1. pyrDown

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

row = np.array([10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80], np.uint8)
print("pyrDown of the row:", cv2.pyrDown(np.repeat(row[None, :], 5, axis=0))[1].tolist())

def blur_row(r):                                          # 1-4-6-4-1 / 16 with BORDER_REFLECT_101
    p = np.pad(r.astype(float), 2, mode="reflect")
    return np.convolve(p, [1, 4, 6, 4, 1], mode="valid") / 16
print("by hand:", blur_row(row)[::2].tolist())

scene = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
levels = [scene]
for _ in range(4):
    levels.append(cv2.pyrDown(levels[-1]))
print("sizes (w x h):", [(l.shape[1], l.shape[0]) for l in levels])
print("total pixels / original:", round(sum(l.size for l in levels) / scene.size, 3))
```

Output:

```text
pyrDown of the row: [18, 30, 50, 69]
by hand: [17.5, 30.0, 50.0, 68.75]
sizes (w x h): [(320, 200), (160, 100), (80, 50), (40, 25), (20, 13)]
total pixels / original: 1.332
```

- NumPy's `mode="reflect"` is OpenCV's BORDER_REFLECT_101 (the edge pixel is not repeated).
- The 8-bit result rounds 17.5 up to 18 and 68.75 to 69.

### 2. Aliasing without the blur

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

zone = cv2.imread("sample-zoneplate.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
blurred, dropped = [zone], [zone]
for _ in range(3):
    blurred.append(cv2.pyrDown(blurred[-1]))
    dropped.append(dropped[-1][::2, ::2])                 # subsample only
corner = lambda x: x[:x.shape[0] // 4, :x.shape[1] // 4].std()
for i in range(4):
    print(f"level {i}: corner std with blur {corner(blurred[i]):5.1f} | without {corner(dropped[i]):5.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
level 0: corner std with blur  90.2 | without  90.2
level 1: corner std with blur   8.8 | without  90.4
level 2: corner std with blur   0.1 | without  92.8
level 3: corner std with blur   0.0 | without  89.5
```

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.pyrDown(src, dstsize=None, borderType=cv2.BORDER_DEFAULT)`**: blur with the 5 × 5 kernel and drop every second row and column. Default size ((w + 1)/2, (h + 1)/2); `dstsize` may differ by at most one pixel from that.

**`cv2.pyrUp(src, dstsize=None, ...)`**: the reverse direction, 2× larger (20.2).

**`buildPyramid`**: exists in C++ (`cv::buildPyramid`) but is not exposed in the Python bindings of 4.13 (`cv2.buildPyramid` raises AttributeError); in Python, loop over `cv2.pyrDown`.

**Types**: 8-bit, 16-bit, 16-bit signed, float32 and float64, any number of channels.

**Other factors**: for scale steps other than 2 (e.g. 1.2 for detectors), use `cv2.resize` with `INTER_AREA` after a matching Gaussian blur, or `INTER_AREA` alone for moderate steps.

## Where it fails

- **Fixed factor 2**: objects whose size falls between levels are seen at a scale up to √2 off; detectors that need finer steps build their own scale series.
- **Fine detail is gone** at coarse levels, by design: thin scratches or small text vanish after one or two levels.
- **Coarse-to-fine search can lock onto the wrong candidate** if the target is indistinct at the coarsest level; keep several candidates.
- **Not shift-invariant**: shifting the input by one pixel changes the coarse levels noticeably (subsampling phase).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Downscaling with `img[::2, ::2]`** or `INTER_NEAREST`: aliasing.
- **Mixing up coordinates between levels**: a position (x, y) at level k is (x · 2ᵏ, y · 2ᵏ) at level 0, plus up to 2ᵏ − 1 pixels of uncertainty.
- **Going too coarse**: below about 16 pixels on the object, it is no longer recognisable.
- **Odd sizes**: pyrUp of a level gives an even size; pass `dstsize` to match the level below (20.2).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The generating kernel

Burt and Adelson chose a 5-tap kernel (c, b, a, b, c) satisfying normalisation, symmetry and "equal contribution" (each input pixel contributes the same total weight to the next level). That leaves one parameter a; a = 0.375 gives 1-4-6-4-1 / 16, close to a Gaussian with σ ≈ 1 [1]. Repeated application approximates Gaussians of growing σ, which is why the levels form a discrete scale-space.

### Scale-space

A continuous scale-space is the family of images blurred with Gaussians of every σ (Witkin 1983, Koenderink 1984, Lindeberg [2]). A pyramid samples that family at σ doubling per level and saves memory by subsampling. SIFT (34.1) uses several blur levels per octave, between the halvings, to get finer scale steps.

### Shift-invariant alternatives

Undecimated ("à trous") wavelet transforms and steerable pyramids keep every level at full size or add orientation bands, trading memory for shift-invariance and direction selectivity (31.2).

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Write a `build_pyramid(img, levels)` function, and check that `hasattr(cv2, "buildPyramid")` is False in Python. Then compare level 1 with `cv2.resize(..., interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)` to half size: how large is the difference?

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

def build_pyramid(img, levels):
    pyr = [img]
    for _ in range(levels):
        pyr.append(cv2.pyrDown(pyr[-1]))
    return pyr

pyr = build_pyramid(scene, 3)
print("levels:", len(pyr), "| cv2.buildPyramid in Python:", hasattr(cv2, "buildPyramid"))
area = cv2.resize(scene, (160, 100), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)
diff = np.abs(pyr[1].astype(int) - area)
print("pyrDown vs INTER_AREA: mean abs diff", round(float(diff.mean()), 2), "| max", int(diff.max()))
```

Output:

```text
levels: 4 | cv2.buildPyramid in Python: False
pyrDown vs INTER_AREA: mean abs diff 3.23 | max 91
```

Both are reasonable half-size images, but they are not the same: they differ by 3 grey levels on average and by up to 91 at sharp edges and fine detail, where pyrDown's 5 × 5 kernel blurs much more than the 2 × 2 average of INTER_AREA (which lets more fine detail, and some aliasing, through).

</Solution>
