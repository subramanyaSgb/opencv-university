# 20.3 Blending

<Definition term="Multiband (pyramid) blending">combining two images along a seam separately for each band of detail sizes: coarse brightness and colour change gradually over a wide zone, fine texture switches over a narrow zone. Each band gets a transition as wide as its own detail size, so the seam is invisible and nothing is doubled.</Definition>

Panoramas (34.6), exposure fusion (48.1), inserting a defect into a good image for training data (58.3) and replacing a background all need two images joined without a visible seam. 14.4 blended with one mask; this chapter shows why a single mask cannot work at all scales, and how Burt and Adelson's 1983 multiresolution spline [1] solves it with the Laplacian pyramid of 20.2.

## 1. An analogy

Joining two pieces of wallpaper with different patterns and slightly different shades. Fading one into the other over a metre gives a smooth shade change but a strip where both patterns show through on top of each other. A sharp cut keeps the patterns clean but leaves a visible line where the shades jump. What you want: the shade fades over a metre, while the pattern switches at a clean line.

## 2. Blend each band separately

The trick is in GM. At level 0 the mask is still sharp, so the finest detail switches within a pixel or two. At level 3, the mask has been blurred and halved three times; enlarged back to full size, its transition is about 8 times wider, which is right for structures 8 times larger. The residual level (very coarse) uses a very soft mask, so the overall colour changes slowly across a wide zone.

Measured on the two 256 × 256 textures (steel on the left, brick on the right, mask = left half). "Colour jump": the largest step across the seam after blurring the result with σ = 8 (coarse brightness and colour). "Ghost zone": the number of columns where the fine texture matches neither A nor B, i.e. both patterns are mixed:

| Method | Colour jump | Ghost zone (columns) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hard cut | 3.23 | 5 |
| Feathered mask, σ = 2 | 3.14 | 4 |
| Feathered mask, σ = 8 | 2.31 | 6 |
| Feathered mask, σ = 32 | 0.94 | **28** |
| Pyramid, 4 levels | 2.62 | 2 |
| Pyramid, 6 levels | 1.27 | 2 |
| **Pyramid, 8 levels** | **0.51** | **2** |

A feather can be smooth (σ = 32) or clean (σ = 2), not both. The pyramid gets the smoothest colour transition *and* the narrowest mixing zone. More levels give a softer coarse transition; with 8 levels on a 256-pixel image the residual is 2 × 2 pixels, so the colour transition spans the whole image.

## 3. How many levels

The coarsest level decides how wide the slowest transition is: roughly 2^(levels) pixels. Rules of thumb:

- Enough levels that the residual is small compared with the overlap region, so differences in overall brightness are spread across it.
- Not so many that the soft transition reaches into parts of A or B that must stay unchanged.
- The mask must be defined everywhere at the coarse levels: for a small region (the circle), too many levels blur the mask into a weak grey cloud and the inserted object fades.

Brightness differences larger than the blend can hide (one image much darker) should be corrected first (gain compensation, as in stitching).

## 4. Industrial example

Panorama stitching in OpenCV (`cv2.Stitcher`, 34.6) uses multiband blending by default to hide seams between overlapping camera images; inspection systems that tile a large part (a solar panel, a printed web, a wafer) from many camera images use the same blender so that tile borders do not show up as false defects. Synthetic defect generation for training classifiers (58.3) pastes cut-out defects into good images with pyramid blending, so that the network learns the defect and not the pasting seam.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** At level 3 of the mask pyramid, the mask's transition is about 2 pixels wide. How wide is it at full resolution?

<Solution>

2 × 2³ = **16 pixels**: each level doubles the width when enlarged back.

</Solution>

**Q2.** Why is the mask pyramid Gaussian and not Laplacian?

<Solution>

At every level the mask must be a weight between 0 and 1 for that level's band. The Gaussian pyramid gives a blurred, downscaled copy of the mask; a Laplacian level of the mask would be a signed detail image, not a weight.

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: pyramid blending from scratch against a cut and a feather, then OpenCV's MultiBandBlender.

### 1. Pyramid blending

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

A = cv2.imread("sample-blend-a.png").astype(np.float32)
B = cv2.imread("sample-blend-b.png").astype(np.float32)
h, w = A.shape[:2]
M = np.zeros((h, w), np.float32)
M[:, : w // 2] = 1                                                   # take A on the left

def gaussian_pyr(x, n):
    g = [x]
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        g.append(cv2.pyrDown(g[-1]))
    return g

def laplacian_pyr(x, n):
    g = gaussian_pyr(x, n)
    return [g[i] - cv2.pyrUp(g[i + 1], dstsize=(g[i].shape[1], g[i].shape[0])) for i in range(n - 1)] + [g[-1]]

def collapse(L):
    cur = L[-1]
    for level in reversed(L[:-1]):
        cur = level + cv2.pyrUp(cur, dstsize=(level.shape[1], level.shape[0]))
    return cur

def pyramid_blend(a, b, m, n):
    La, Lb, Gm = laplacian_pyr(a, n), laplacian_pyr(b, n), gaussian_pyr(m, n)
    return collapse([g[..., None] * la + (1 - g[..., None]) * lb for la, lb, g in zip(La, Lb, Gm)])

highpass = lambda x: x - cv2.GaussianBlur(x, (0, 0), 2)
hA, hB = highpass(A), highpass(B)

def seam_report(name, R):
    low = cv2.GaussianBlur(R, (0, 0), 8)
    jump = np.abs(np.diff(low, axis=1)).mean(axis=(0, 2)).max()       # coarse colour step
    hR = highpass(R)
    eA, eB = ((hR - hA) ** 2).sum((0, 2)), ((hR - hB) ** 2).sum((0, 2))
    ghost = ((eA > 0.25 * (hA ** 2).sum((0, 2))) & (eB > 0.25 * (hB ** 2).sum((0, 2)))).sum()
    print(f"{name:12s} colour jump {jump:4.2f} | ghost columns {int(ghost):3d}")

seam_report("cut", M[..., None] * A + (1 - M[..., None]) * B)
for s in (2, 32):
    m = cv2.GaussianBlur(M, (0, 0), s)[..., None]
    seam_report(f"feather {s}", m * A + (1 - m) * B)
for n in (4, 8):
    seam_report(f"pyramid {n}", pyramid_blend(A, B, M, n))
```

Output:

```text
cut          colour jump 3.23 | ghost columns   5
feather 2    colour jump 3.14 | ghost columns   4
feather 32   colour jump 0.94 | ghost columns  28
pyramid 4    colour jump 2.62 | ghost columns   2
pyramid 8    colour jump 0.51 | ghost columns   2
```

- All arithmetic in float32; clip to 0–255 and convert to uint8 only for saving.
- The mask can be any shape and soft-edged; it should be float in 0–1.

### 2. OpenCV's MultiBandBlender

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

a8, b8 = cv2.imread("sample-blend-a.png"), cv2.imread("sample-blend-b.png")
mask_a = np.zeros((h, w), np.uint8)
mask_a[:, : w // 2] = 255
blender = cv2.detail_MultiBandBlender(0, 5)                          # try_gpu = 0, 5 bands
blender.prepare((0, 0, w, h))                                         # output rectangle x, y, w, h
blender.feed(a8.astype(np.int16), mask_a, (0, 0))                     # image, mask, top-left corner
blender.feed(b8.astype(np.int16), 255 - mask_a, (0, 0))
result, result_mask = blender.blend(None, None)
out = np.clip(result, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)
print(result.dtype, out.shape, "| differs from my 5-level blend by", 
      round(float(np.abs(out.astype(float) - np.clip(pyramid_blend(A, B, M, 6), 0, 255)).mean()), 2), "on average")
```

Output:

```text
int16 (256, 256, 3) | differs from my 5-level blend by 1.99 on average
```

- The stitching blender expects int16 images (CV_16SC3), uint8 masks and the top-left corner of each image in the output; it is designed for many overlapping, differently placed images.
- `num_bands` counts band levels, so 5 bands correspond to 6 pyramid levels including the residual. The small average difference (about 2 grey levels) comes from the blender's integer arithmetic and its own mask weighting and border handling; visually the two results are the same.

## OpenCV API notes

**Pyramid blending from primitives**: `cv2.pyrDown`, `cv2.pyrUp(dstsize=...)` and float arithmetic, as in example 1.

**`cv2.detail_MultiBandBlender(try_gpu=0, num_bands=5, weight_type=cv2.CV_32F)`**: `prepare(rect)`, `feed(img_int16, mask_uint8, top_left)` for each image, `blend(None, None)` → (int16 result, mask). Used by `cv2.Stitcher`.

**`cv2.detail_FeatherBlender(sharpness=0.02)`**: distance-based feathering, the cheaper alternative.

**`cv2.seamlessClone(src, dst, mask, center, flags)`**: Poisson blending (photo module), which matches gradients instead of pyramids: ideal for pasting an object into a different background.

## Where it fails

- **Misaligned images**: blending hides seams, not misregistration; edges that do not line up stay doubled (21).
- **Large brightness differences**: the coarse transition becomes a visible gradient; compensate gains first.
- **Small masked regions with many levels**: the coarse mask becomes too weak and the region fades.
- **Moving objects in overlap regions**: ghosts; choose the seam to avoid them (graph-cut seam finders in stitching).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Blending in uint8**: band levels are signed (20.2).
- **Building the mask pyramid as a Laplacian pyramid**.
- **Mask and images of different sizes** or pyramids with different numbers of levels.
- **Forgetting the colour channels**: the mask must be broadcast to all three.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The multiresolution spline

Burt and Adelson [1] showed that a transition zone of about one wavelength is ideal for each frequency band: narrower makes a visible step, wider makes a double image. A pyramid provides exactly that, one band per octave, at a cost of about twice a single blur.

### Poisson blending

Pérez, Gangnet and Blake (2003) [2] paste the gradients of the source into the target and solve a Poisson equation so that the result matches the target at the region border. The source's colour is changed to fit the background; good for objects, less good when the colours must stay exact. OpenCV: `cv2.seamlessClone` (NORMAL_CLONE, MIXED_CLONE).

### Seam finding

Before blending, stitching chooses where the seam runs: through areas where the two images agree (graph cut, Kwatra et al. 2003), so that moving objects and parallax errors are not cut in half. OpenCV: `cv2.detail_GraphCutSeamFinder`.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Blend with a circular mask (radius 0.3 × width, centred) using 4 and 7 levels, and report the mean of the blended mask weight inside the circle at the coarsest level, i.e. how much of A survives there.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:h, 0:w]
C = (np.hypot(xx - w / 2, yy - h / 2) < 0.3 * w).astype(np.float32)
for n in (4, 7):
    g = gaussian_pyr(C, n)[-1]
    top = cv2.resize(g, (w, h), interpolation=cv2.INTER_LINEAR)
    print(f"{n} levels: coarsest mask {g.shape}, mean weight inside the circle {top[C > 0].mean():.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
4 levels: coarsest mask (32, 32), mean weight inside the circle 0.93
7 levels: coarsest mask (4, 4), mean weight inside the circle 0.47
```

With 7 levels the coarsest mask is a 4 × 4 blur in which the circle has become a weak grey: only about half of A's overall colour survives inside it, and the inserted region looks washed out. For small regions, use fewer levels.

</Solution>
