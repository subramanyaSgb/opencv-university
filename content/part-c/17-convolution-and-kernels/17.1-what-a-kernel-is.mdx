# 17.1 What a kernel is

<Definition term="Kernel (filter mask)">a small grid of numbers, for example 3 × 3, that is placed over every pixel in turn. Each output pixel is the sum of the neighbouring input pixels multiplied by the kernel's weights. Changing the weights changes the operation: blur, sharpen, find edges.</Definition>

Point operations (Part B) look at one pixel at a time. They cannot tell a pixel on a smooth surface from one on an edge, or a noise spike from a real dark spot, because that needs the neighbours. A kernel brings in the neighbourhood. It is the most important idea in classical image processing, and it is also exactly what the first layer of a convolutional neural network learns (Part J).

## 1. An analogy

A recipe with fixed proportions, applied in every kitchen in a street: "one part from each neighbour, four parts from your own house". Every house cooks the same recipe with its own neighbours' ingredients. A smooth street gives the same dish everywhere; where the houses change, the dish changes.

A 7 × 6 test image: a dark region (20) on the left, a bright region (200) on the right, one grey spot (90) and one dark spot (30). The box kernel (all weights 1/9) at pixel (2, 2):

```text
neighbours  20  20 200      weights  1/9 1/9 1/9
            20  90 200               1/9 1/9 1/9
            20  20 200               1/9 1/9 1/9
sum = (20 + 20 + 200 + 20 + 90 + 200 + 20 + 20 + 200) / 9 = 790 / 9 = 87.8
```

The output is the neighbourhood's average: the spot is diluted, and the edge between 20 and 200 becomes a ramp (row 2 after the box: 20, 27.8, 87.8, 147.8, 181.1, …).

## 3. Reading a kernel

| Kernel | Weights | Sum | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | 1 in the centre, 0 elsewhere | 1 | nothing |
| Box | all 1/9 | 1 | average: blur, noise reduction |
| Gaussian | (1 2 1; 2 4 2; 1 2 1) / 16 | 1 | weighted average, smoother blur (18.2) |
| Sharpen | (0 −1 0; −1 5 −1; 0 −1 0) | 1 | centre minus neighbours, added back: edges stronger (19.1) |
| Sobel x | (−1 0 1; −2 0 2; −1 0 1) | 0 | brightness change from left to right (22.2) |
| Laplacian | (0 1 0; 1 −4 1; 0 1 0) | 0 | second derivative: spots and edges (22.3) |

Two rules cover most cases:

- **Weights sum to 1**: flat areas keep their value, the mean brightness is unchanged (on `sample-scene.png`: 120.3 before and after the box and the sharpen kernel).
- **Weights sum to 0**: flat areas become 0; only changes produce output, positive or negative. Results must be stored in a signed type (Sobel x on the scene ranges from −745 to 803).

## 4. Industrial example

A glass-sheet inspection looks for small bubbles: dark spots of 2–3 pixels on a bright, slightly uneven background. A 7 × 7 kernel with a positive centre region and a negative ring around it (sum 0) gives near zero on the smooth glass, whatever its brightness, and a strong response on a spot of the right size. The kernel is a hand-made "spot detector"; Laplacian-of-Gaussian and DoG filters (22.3, 20.4) are its principled versions.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** Apply the sharpen kernel (0 −1 0; −1 5 −1; 0 −1 0) to a flat area of value 100.

<Solution>

5 · 100 − 4 · 100 = **100**: flat areas are unchanged (the weights sum to 1).

</Solution>

**Q2.** What does the Sobel x kernel give on a flat area, and on a vertical edge from 20 (left) to 200 (right)?

<Solution>

Flat: **0**. On the edge (left column 20, right column 200): (−1 −2 −1)·20 + (1 + 2 + 1)·200 = −80 + 800 = **720**.

</Solution>

## Code

Two examples: kernels on the small test image, then mean brightness and value range on a real image.

### 1. Kernels on a 7 × 6 image

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

a = np.array([[20, 20, 20, 200, 200, 200, 200],
              [20, 20, 20, 200, 200, 200, 200],
              [20, 20, 90, 200, 200, 200, 200],
              [20, 20, 20, 200, 200, 30, 200],
              [20, 20, 20, 200, 200, 200, 200],
              [20, 20, 20, 200, 200, 200, 200]], np.float32)
kernels = {
    "box": np.ones((3, 3), np.float32) / 9,
    "sharpen": np.array([[0, -1, 0], [-1, 5, -1], [0, -1, 0]], np.float32),
    "sobel x": np.array([[-1, 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]], np.float32),
}
print("by hand, box at (2, 2):", round((20 + 20 + 200 + 20 + 90 + 200 + 20 + 20 + 200) / 9, 1))
for name, k in kernels.items():
    r = cv2.filter2D(a, -1, k)                              # -1: same depth as the input (float32 here)
    print(f"{name:8s} (sum {k.sum():.0f}) row 2: {r[2].astype(float).round(1).tolist()}")
```

Output:

```text
by hand, box at (2, 2): 87.8
box      (sum 1) row 2: [20.0, 27.8, 87.8, 147.8, 181.1, 181.1, 162.2]
sharpen  (sum 1) row 2: [20.0, -50.0, 190.0, 310.0, 200.0, 370.0, 200.0]
sobel x  (sum 0) row 2: [0.0, 140.0, 720.0, 580.0, -170.0, 0.0, 0.0]
```

- The box turns the 20 → 200 step into a ramp; sharpen overshoots on both sides of the edge (−50 and 310) and boosts the spots; Sobel x is zero on flat parts and large at the edge.

### 2. Brightness and range on a real image

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

img = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE).astype(np.float32)
for name, k in kernels.items():
    r = cv2.filter2D(img, -1, k)
    print(f"{name:8s} mean {img.mean():.1f} -> {r.mean():.1f} | range {r.min():.1f} to {r.max():.1f}")

u8 = cv2.filter2D(img.astype(np.uint8), -1, kernels["sobel x"])           # 8-bit output: negatives clipped to 0
print("Sobel x into uint8: negative values lost:", int((cv2.filter2D(img, -1, kernels["sobel x"]) < 0).sum()), "pixels set to 0")
```

Output:

```text
box      mean 120.3 -> 120.3 | range 7.7 to 250.9
sharpen  mean 120.3 -> 120.3 | range -492.0 to 805.0
sobel x  mean 120.3 -> 4.0 | range -745.0 to 803.0
Sobel x into uint8: negative values lost: 27943 pixels set to 0
```

- Sum-1 kernels keep the mean; the Sobel output is centred near 0 (the 4.0 comes from the image's overall left-to-right brightness gradient).
- Filtering an 8-bit image into an 8-bit result clips every negative response to 0: half of all edges disappear. Use `ddepth=cv2.CV_16S` or `cv2.CV_32F` for signed kernels.

## OpenCV API notes

**`cv2.filter2D(src, ddepth, kernel, anchor=(-1, -1), delta=0, borderType=BORDER_REFLECT_101)`**: correlation with any kernel (17.2, 17.6); `ddepth=-1` keeps the input depth, `cv2.CV_32F` / `cv2.CV_16S` for signed results.

**Ready-made kernels**: `cv2.blur` (box), `cv2.GaussianBlur`, `cv2.Sobel`, `cv2.Laplacian`, `cv2.getGaussianKernel`, `cv2.getDerivKernels`.

**Kernel dtype**: float32 (or float64) array; integer arrays are converted.

## Where it fails

- **Fixed weights everywhere**: a kernel cannot adapt to edges (it blurs them too); edge-preserving filters (median, bilateral, 18.3–18.4) are non-linear.
- **Large kernels are slow** done naively (k² multiplications per pixel); separable kernels (17.4), integral images (17.5) or the FFT (Part E) help.
- **Borders**: near the edge the kernel reaches outside the image (17.3).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Signed kernels into uint8 outputs** (negative values clipped).
- **Forgetting to normalise** a blur kernel (all ones without /9 brightens the image ninefold).
- **Integer division** when building kernels (`np.ones((3, 3)) // 9` is all zeros).
- **Thinking a kernel is applied once**: it is applied at every pixel.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Linear shift-invariant systems

A kernel operation is linear (filtering a sum equals the sum of the filtered images) and shift-invariant (shifting the input shifts the output). Every linear shift-invariant operation on images is a convolution with some kernel, its impulse response: filter an image that is 1 at one pixel and 0 elsewhere, and the output is the (flipped) kernel [1].

### Kernels in neural networks

A convolutional layer applies many kernels at once, each producing one feature map, and learns their weights from data. Early layers typically learn edge- and blob-like kernels that resemble the hand-made ones in this chapter (Part J, 51.3) [2].

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

Apply the Laplacian (0 1 0; 1 −4 1; 0 1 0) at the dark spot (5, 3) of the test image (value 30, its four neighbours 200).

<Solution>

4 · 200 − 4 · 30 = 800 − 120 = **680**: a strong positive response at a dark spot (the Laplacian is positive where a pixel is darker than its neighbours).

</Solution>

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Make a 5 × 5 "spot detector": +1 in the 3 × 3 centre, and enough negative weight on the outer ring that the sum is 0. Apply it to a flat image (200) that contains a dark 3 × 3 square (30) and a single dark pixel (30), and compare the responses.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

k = np.full((5, 5), -9 / 16, np.float32)               # 16 ring cells share -9
k[1:4, 1:4] = 1                                         # 9 centre cells
print("kernel sum:", float(k.sum()))
b = np.full((9, 11), 200, np.float32)
b[3:6, 2:5] = 30                                        # 3 x 3 dark square, centre (3, 4)
b[4, 8] = 30                                            # single dark pixel at (8, 4)
r = cv2.filter2D(b, -1, k, borderType=cv2.BORDER_REPLICATE)
y, x = np.unravel_index(np.abs(r).argmax(), r.shape)
print(f"largest |response| {r[y, x]:.1f} at (x={x}, y={y}) | single pixel: {r[4, 8]:.1f} | flat corner: {r[0, 0]:.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
kernel sum: 0.0
largest |response| -1530.0 at (x=3, y=4) | single pixel: -170.0 | flat corner: 0.0
```

The response is strongest (negative: centre darker than ring) at the centre of the 3 × 3 square, nine times stronger than for the single pixel, and zero on flat glass. A kernel matched to the defect's size responds most to that size. (On the 7 × 6 test image the large step between 20 and 200 would dominate: real spot detectors also need edge suppression.)

</Solution>
