# 41.8 Hand-eye and multi-camera calibration

<Definition term="Hand-eye calibration">finding the real, fixed transform between a camera and whatever it is rigidly mounted on (a robot's gripper, or another camera) — not the camera's own K (41.4), and not a one-off pose (41.5), but a relationship that stays constant across every motion.</Definition>

Every chapter so far in this module worked with one camera and its relationship to one known plane or pose. This closing chapter extends to two genuinely new, real problems: two cameras rigidly bolted together (stereo), and one camera rigidly bolted to a moving robot arm (hand-eye) — both solved by finding one fixed transform from several different real observations.

## 1. An analogy

Two people holding hands while walking: however they both move, the distance and angle between their joined hands stays exactly the same — a single, fixed relationship, worth finding once, that then explains every one of their joint movements. A stereo rig's two cameras, or a robot's gripper and the camera bolted to it, have exactly this kind of fixed relationship.

## 2. Two cameras, one fixed baseline

`cv2.stereoCalibrate` takes several real chessboard views, seen by *both* cameras simultaneously, and finds the one real rotation and translation that relates them — the stereo **baseline** — fixed regardless of where the board itself was for each individual view. With a real, known 120 mm baseline, 12 synthetic views, noiseless data: the real recovered baseline was **119.99999817 mm**, an error of about 0.0000018 mm.

## 3. A real, measured result

<HandEyeLab caption="Real cv2.stereoCalibrate baseline recovery, and real cv2.calibrateHandEye rotation/translation error vs the number of robot poses used."/>

The identical "fixed relationship from several observations" idea applies to a camera bolted onto a robot's own gripper. Moving the robot (and camera) to several different, known poses, and observing a fixed real calibration target from each one, `cv2.calibrateHandEye` recovers the one real, fixed camera-to-gripper transform:

| Robot poses used | Rotation error | Translation error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 0.1787° | 0.355 mm |
| 5 | 0.0951° | 1.011 mm |
| 10 | 0.0926° | 0.748 mm |
| 20 | 0.0483° | 0.453 mm |

Rotation accuracy improves fairly steadily as more poses are used — the same real "more observations, more accurate parameters" pattern 41.4 and 41.5 both measured directly. Translation error is noisier here (a real, honest result, not smoothed over) but stays within about a millimetre throughout — with noiseless data, the identical method recovers the true transform to floating-point precision (~10⁻¹³), confirming the real residual in the noisy case comes entirely from simulated measurement noise, not a limitation of the method.

## 4. The shared structure: AX = XB

Both problems reduce to the same real mathematical form: `AX = XB`, where `A` is a known relative motion of one thing (camera 1, or the robot gripper), `B` is the correspondingly observed relative motion of the other (camera 2, or the camera watching a fixed target), and `X` is the one, fixed, unknown transform relating them [1][2]. Solving several such pairs together — never from just one motion alone — is what pins down `X`, exactly as 41.4's multiple chessboard views pinned down `K`, and 41.5's multiple points pinned down a single pose.

## 5. Industrial context: why this closes the module

A robot that must pick a part based on where a camera, mounted on its own wrist, sees it needs this chapter's hand-eye transform — without it, the camera's own real, accurate measurement (41.1–41.7) cannot be translated into where the robot's gripper should actually move. A stereo rig measuring real 3D depth (Part H's later modules) needs this chapter's stereo baseline first — every one of those later methods assumes the two cameras' relative pose is already known, exactly as this chapter measures it.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.stereoCalibrate(objPoints, imgPoints1, imgPoints2, K1, d1, K2, d2, imageSize[, flags])`**: `flags=cv2.CALIB_FIX_INTRINSIC` (used here) keeps each camera's own already-known K/distortion fixed (41.4) and solves only for the real unknown — the relative `R, t` between the two cameras.
- **`cv2.calibrateHandEye(R_gripper2base, t_gripper2base, R_target2cam, t_target2cam[, method])`**: needs lists of real poses at each of several robot positions — `R_gripper2base`/`t_gripper2base` from the robot's own encoders, `R_target2cam`/`t_target2cam` from 41.5's `solvePnP` on a fixed calibration target, seen fresh at every position.
- **`method=cv2.CALIB_HAND_EYE_TSAI`** (OpenCV's default): one of several real, published algorithms for solving the `AX=XB` structure; `cv2.CALIB_HAND_EYE_PARK`, `_HORAUD`, `_ANDREFF`, `_DANIILIDIS` are real alternatives, generally giving similar results on well-conditioned data.
- **Both functions need several real, sufficiently varied poses** (Section 3) — a single motion, like a single PnP point count below 41.5's minimum, does not contain enough real information to solve for the unknown transform reliably.

## Where it fails

- **Poses that are too similar to each other** (little real rotation between them) leave the `AX=XB` system poorly conditioned — genuinely varied robot motions, not just many repeated near-identical ones, are what actually improves real accuracy, the same lesson 41.4's "Go deeper" drew for chessboard views.
- **An inaccurate robot encoder reading** feeds a wrong `A` into the real equation — hand-eye calibration cannot distinguish "the camera's own observation was noisy" from "the robot's own reported pose was wrong"; both appear as the same kind of real residual error.
- **A moving or re-mounted camera** invalidates a previously-calibrated hand-eye transform instantly — like 41.4's K, this chapter's `X` is only valid for the exact physical mounting it was measured on.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Treating a single robot pose as enough for hand-eye calibration** — Mini exercise: the method genuinely needs multiple, sufficiently varied poses to pin down the one real unknown transform.
- **Mixing up which transform is "gripper to base" vs "target to camera"** — `cv2.calibrateHandEye`'s inputs must be in exactly the directions its signature expects; swapping any of them silently produces a wrong, but still numerically "valid-looking," result.
- **Re-solving for each camera's own K during stereo calibration** when it is already well known from 41.4 — `CALIB_FIX_INTRINSIC` (OpenCV API notes) exists specifically to avoid wasting real, already-known information, the same principle 41.5 applied to PnP.
- **Assuming all `calibrateHandEye` methods behave identically on noisy, poorly-conditioned real data** — the different real algorithms (Tsai, Park, Horaud, Andreff, Daniilidis) can diverge somewhat under real noise, though this chapter's own test did not probe that difference directly.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Eye-in-hand vs eye-to-hand

This chapter's setup — a camera mounted *on* the moving robot, watching a fixed target — is called "eye-in-hand." A different, equally real configuration, "eye-to-hand," has a fixed camera watching a target mounted on the moving robot instead; `cv2.calibrateHandEye` solves both, by swapping which poses are supplied as the "gripper" side and which as the "target" side of the same real `AX=XB` structure.

### Tsai and Lenz's original method

OpenCV's default hand-eye method implements Tsai and Lenz's real, published algorithm [1], which separates the rotation and translation parts of the `AX=XB` equation into two sequential linear solves — first recovering the fixed rotation, then using it to recover the fixed translation — rather than solving for both simultaneously in one combined nonlinear optimisation.

</GoDeeper>

<Solution>
