# 44.2 Photometric stereo

<Definition term="Photometric stereo">recovering a dense, per-pixel surface normal field from several images of the SAME fixed view, lit from several different KNOWN directions — shape from shading changes, not from geometry or triangulation at all.</Definition>

44.1's point clouds come from geometry: several *views* of a moving camera or a calibrated stereo rig, triangulated into 3D points. Photometric stereo is the real opposite: one fixed camera, one fixed object, and *light* that moves between shots instead. The real, surprising part is how much shape information that alone reveals — and how badly a careless implementation of it can fail.

## 1. An analogy

A sculptor working on a clay bust turns a single lamp around it, watching the shadows shift across the same unmoved surface. A flat patch looks the same from every lamp angle; a bump brightens as the lamp swings toward it and darkens as the lamp swings away. The *pattern* of brightening and darkening across several lamp positions is, by itself, enough to read off the real local slope of the clay at every point — exactly what this chapter computes, per pixel, from several real photographs instead of an eye.

## 2. The pipeline: known light, one equation per image, three unknowns per pixel

    { label: "Several images, each under a different KNOWN light direction", tone: "data" },

Each image gives one real linear equation per pixel: its measured brightness `I` equals the point's albedo (how reflective it is) times the dot product of its unit surface normal with that image's known light direction. Three unknowns — the normal's three components, scaled by the unknown albedo — need at least three such equations, so at least three images under three different, known light directions. Solving that small linear system per pixel (`np.linalg.solve` or `np.linalg.lstsq`) recovers `albedo x normal` directly; normalizing it gives the real unit normal, and its length gives the real albedo.

## 3. A real, measured result: the linear algebra is exact, but shadows are not

<PhotoStereoLab caption="Real, from-scratch NumPy photometric stereo results (precomputed), including the real surprise that more lights can make a naive solve worse." />

On a synthetic rendered sphere (true albedo 0.8), solving the exact linear system with three known lights recovers every normal to **0.00000007°** of the true analytic value — the linear algebra itself is exact, given exact measurements. But real cameras cannot measure a negative brightness: when a light sits behind the local surface at a given point, that point's *real* brightness there is genuinely zero, not negative — a real optical fact, not noise. Re-solving the identical system with this real clamping in place (3 lights, no special handling) gives a real mean error of **5.49°** and a worst case of **45°**, because a full **40.0%** of the sphere's visible pixels are shadowed from at least one of the three lights.

## 4. A real, measured surprise: more lights made it worse

Adding more lights sounds like it should only help — more real measurements, more redundancy. Solving the same way (treating every image equally, with no special handling for shadows) gives the opposite real result: **6** lights raised the mean error to **7.11°**, and **12** lights raised it further, to **8.43°**. More lights means more *chances* that at least one is shadowed at any given pixel (40.0% at 3 lights, rising to 54.6% at 6, and 67.6% at 12) — and a naive least-squares solve treats every shadowed zero reading as genuine evidence that the surface faces *away* from that light, actively pulling the fitted normal in a wrong direction.

The real fix is **shadow-aware** least squares: before solving each pixel, drop any image whose measured brightness is at or near zero for that pixel, and solve using only the lights that actually illuminated it. Applied to the identical 6- and 12-light data, this flips the result completely: 6 lights now gives a mean error of **0.0239°** (only 2 of 7525 pixels, at the sphere's grazing limb, had too few valid lights left to solve at all), and 12 lights gives **0.0000°** — essentially exact, because with enough lights spread around, almost every pixel always has at least three genuinely unshadowed measurements. The same fix applied to only **3** lights, by contrast, makes things *worse* (**36.13°** mean, **3021 of 7525 pixels** failing outright) — with no redundancy to spare, losing even one of exactly three equations to a shadow leaves that pixel unsolvable.

## 5. Industrial context: dome and ring-light inspection rigs

A real industrial photometric-stereo rig — a ring or dome of LEDs around a fixed camera, used for inspecting coins, embossed logos, PCB solder joints, or machined metal surfaces — exists specifically to exploit Section 4's own real result: enough lights, spread widely enough, to guarantee every point on the inspected surface sees at least three genuinely unshadowed ones. The recovered normal map often reveals tiny real dents, scratches, or incomplete solder fillets that are nearly invisible in any single ordinarily-lit photograph, because a single photograph conflates a point's albedo with its orientation — exactly the two quantities Section 2's per-pixel solve separates back out.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A photometric stereo rig has exactly 3 lights, and one of them is shadowed for a particular pixel. Based on Section 4, can a shadow-aware solve still recover that pixel's normal?

<Solution>

No. With exactly 3 lights and 3 unknowns, losing even one equation to a detected shadow leaves only 2 real equations for 3 unknowns — underdetermined, with no unique solution. This chapter's own real result (36.13° mean error, 3021/7525 pixels failing outright at 3 lights) is exactly this case; shadow-awareness only helps when there is real redundancy (more lights than the bare minimum) to fall back on.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. NumPy: the exact linear solve, and the real cost of self-shadowing

```python
import numpy as np

SIZE, RADIUS, ALBEDO = 101, 49, 0.8
cx, cy = SIZE // 2, SIZE // 2
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:SIZE, 0:SIZE]
dx, dy = (xx - cx).astype(float), (yy - cy).astype(float)
mask = (dx**2 + dy**2) <= RADIUS**2
dz = np.zeros_like(dx)
dz[mask] = np.sqrt(np.maximum(RADIUS**2 - (dx**2 + dy**2)[mask], 0))
normals = np.zeros((SIZE, SIZE, 3))
normals[..., 0], normals[..., 1], normals[..., 2] = dx / RADIUS, dy / RADIUS, dz / RADIUS
normals[mask] /= np.linalg.norm(normals, axis=2)[mask][:, None]

def light_dir(az_deg, el_deg):
    az, el = np.radians(az_deg), np.radians(el_deg)
    return np.array([np.cos(el) * np.cos(az), np.cos(el) * np.sin(az), np.sin(el)])

def render(light, clip=True):
    I = ALBEDO * np.einsum('ijk,k->ij', normals, light / np.linalg.norm(light))
    if clip:
        I = np.clip(I, 0, None)  # a real camera can't see negative brightness
    I[~mask] = 0
    return I

def angular_error(N_est):
    tf, ef, mf = normals.reshape(-1, 3), N_est.reshape(-1, 3), mask.reshape(-1)
    return np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(np.sum(tf * ef, axis=1), -1, 1)))[mf]

lights3 = [light_dir(0, 45), light_dir(120, 45), light_dir(240, 45)]
L_mat = np.array(lights3)

def solve(images):
    I_stack = np.stack(images, axis=0).reshape(3, -1)
    rhoN = np.linalg.solve(L_mat, I_stack)
    rho = np.linalg.norm(rhoN, axis=0)
    N_est = np.zeros_like(rhoN)
    N_est[:, rho > 1e-6] = rhoN[:, rho > 1e-6] / rho[rho > 1e-6]
    return N_est.T.reshape(SIZE, SIZE, 3), rho

N_noclip, _ = solve([render(l, clip=False) for l in lights3])
print(f"no self-shadowing: mean angular error = {angular_error(N_noclip).mean():.8f} deg")

N_clip, rho_clip = solve([render(l, clip=True) for l in lights3])
err = angular_error(N_clip)
shadow_any = np.zeros(mask.shape, dtype=bool)
for l in lights3:
    shadow_any |= (np.einsum('ijk,k->ij', normals, l / np.linalg.norm(l)) < 0) & mask
print(f"with real self-shadowing: mean error = {err.mean():.4f} deg, max = {err.max():.4f} deg")
print(f"fraction shadowed in >=1 light: {shadow_any[mask].mean()*100:.1f}%")
print(f"mean recovered albedo: {rho_clip.reshape(-1)[mask.reshape(-1)].mean():.4f} (true {ALBEDO})")
```

Output:

```text
no self-shadowing: mean angular error = 0.00000007 deg
with real self-shadowing: mean error = 5.4855 deg, max = 45.0000 deg
fraction shadowed in >=1 light: 40.0%
mean recovered albedo: 0.7647 (true 0.8)
```

### 2. OpenCV: more lights, shadow-aware, and a real normal-map image

```python
import cv2

def solve_shadow_aware(images, lights, thresh=1e-3):
    L2 = np.array(lights)
    I_stack = np.stack(images, axis=0).reshape(len(lights), -1)
    rhoN = np.zeros((3, I_stack.shape[1]))
    for p in range(I_stack.shape[1]):
        obs = I_stack[:, p]
        valid = obs > thresh
        if valid.sum() < 3:
            continue
        rhoN[:, p], *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(L2[valid], obs[valid], rcond=None)
    rho = np.linalg.norm(rhoN, axis=0)
    N_est = np.zeros_like(rhoN)
    v = rho > 1e-6
    N_est[:, v] = rhoN[:, v] / rho[v]
    return N_est.T.reshape(SIZE, SIZE, 3)

for n_lights, els in [(6, [30, 30, 30, 60, 60, 60]), (12, [20, 20, 20, 20, 45, 45, 45, 45, 70, 70, 70, 70])]:
    azs = np.linspace(0, 360, n_lights, endpoint=False)
    lights_n = [light_dir(az, el) for az, el in zip(azs, els)]
    images_n = [render(l, clip=True) for l in lights_n]
    N_naive, _ = (lambda L, I: (np.linalg.lstsq(L, I, rcond=None)[0], None))(np.array(lights_n), np.stack(images_n).reshape(n_lights, -1))
    N_naive = (N_naive / np.linalg.norm(N_naive, axis=0)).T.reshape(SIZE, SIZE, 3)
    N_sa = solve_shadow_aware(images_n, lights_n)
    print(f"n_lights={n_lights}: naive mean error={angular_error(N_naive).mean():.4f} deg | shadow-aware mean error={angular_error(N_sa).mean():.4f} deg")

# OpenCV convention: encode the recovered normal field as a standard 8-bit BGR normal-map image
normal_map_rgb = ((N_sa + 1) / 2 * 255).astype(np.uint8)
normal_map_bgr = cv2.cvtColor(normal_map_rgb, cv2.COLOR_RGB2BGR)
print("normal map:", normal_map_bgr.dtype, normal_map_bgr.shape, "centre pixel (B,G,R):", normal_map_bgr[SIZE // 2, SIZE // 2])
```

Output:

```text
n_lights=6: naive mean error=7.1105 deg | shadow-aware mean error=0.0239 deg
n_lights=12: naive mean error=8.4329 deg | shadow-aware mean error=0.0000 deg
normal map: uint8 (101, 101, 3) centre pixel (B,G,R): [255 127 127]
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function performs photometric stereo on OpenCV 4.13** — checked directly: no `photometric`-named symbol exists in core or contrib (`cv2.xphoto` covers white balance and tonemapping, a different real problem). This chapter's solve is entirely NumPy's `linalg.solve`/`linalg.lstsq`.
- **`cv2.cvtColor(..., cv2.COLOR_RGB2BGR)`** (Code section 2) is the one real, standard OpenCV touch here: encoding a recovered normal field as a conventional 8-bit normal-map image (`(N+1)/2 x 255`, BGR channel order) for saving or display, exactly as a real industrial inspection tool would output it.
- **The light directions themselves must be known precisely** — this chapter assumed exact, calibrated light directions throughout; a real rig calibrates them once (e.g. from a reflective sphere's own specular highlight positions), a real, separate calibration step not covered here.

## Where it fails

- **Specular highlights violate the Lambertian model entirely** — a shiny or wet surface reflects light in a narrow mirror-like direction rather than Woodham's diffuse `albedo x dot(N, L)` model; those pixels' measured brightness has nothing to do with this chapter's equation, and get the wrong normal with high confidence, not a graceful degradation.
- **Too few lights with no redundancy fails catastrophically under shadows** (Section 4's own real 3-light result: 3021 of 7525 pixels unsolvable) — the real minimum of 3 lights only works when *none* of them happen to be shadowed, which Section 3 showed fails on 40% of a simple sphere's own surface.
- **The camera and object must not move between shots** — any real motion between the several images breaks the fundamental "same pixel, same real 3D point, every image" assumption this chapter's whole linear system depends on.
- **Interreflection (light bouncing off one part of the object onto another) adds real extra brightness this chapter's model does not account for** — most severe in concave real parts, not tested on this chapter's convex sphere.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Assuming more lights always helps** — this chapter's own real, measured reversal (naive error rising from 5.49° to 8.43° as lights went from 3 to 12) is the direct opposite, unless shadows are explicitly excluded.
- **Treating a clamped-to-zero (shadowed) reading as a real, informative measurement** — Section 4's naive solve did exactly this, and it is precisely what made adding lights backfire.
- **Using the bare minimum of 3 lights "to keep it simple"** — Section 3's own real 40%-shadowed finding on a simple convex sphere shows even a gentle, convex real part self-shadows enough of its own surface to make 3 lights a genuinely fragile choice.
- **Forgetting the sign ambiguity-free nature of this method** — unlike 44.1's point-cloud PCA normals (which have a real two-way sign ambiguity), photometric stereo's `albedo x normal` always comes out with the correct, unambiguous sign, because albedo itself is never negative; no extra orientation step is needed here.

</Callout>

### Woodham's original method

R. J. Woodham, "Photometric Method for Determining Surface Orientation from Multiple Images" (*Optical Engineering*, 1980) introduced exactly this chapter's real linear model for Lambertian surfaces under known lighting — the foundational real method every later refinement (shadow handling, specular rejection, uncalibrated photometric stereo) builds on.

### Uncalibrated photometric stereo

A real, harder version of this problem recovers normals even when the light directions themselves are *not* known in advance, using the fact that the recovered normal field is only ambiguous up to a real linear transformation (the "generalized bas-relief ambiguity," Belhumeur, Kriegman, Yuille, "The Bas-Relief Ambiguity," IJCV, 1999) — a real, structural limit on how much shape can ever be recovered from shading alone, without some additional real constraint (known object class, integrability of the normal field into a real surface).

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A photometric stereo pixel has true normal-times-albedo vector `(0.3, 0.0, 0.4)`. What is its recovered albedo, and its unit normal?

<Solution>

Albedo = `sqrt(0.3^2 + 0.0^2 + 0.4^2) = sqrt(0.09+0+0.16) = sqrt(0.25) = 0.5`. Unit normal = `(0.3, 0.0, 0.4) / 0.5 = (0.6, 0.0, 0.8)`.

</Solution>
