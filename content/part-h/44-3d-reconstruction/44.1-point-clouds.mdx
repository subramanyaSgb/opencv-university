# 44.1 Point clouds

<Definition term="Point cloud">a raw, unordered set of 3D points — just coordinates, no surface, no connectivity, no mesh — exactly what 42.1's stereo triangulation or 43.1's structure from motion hands back after recovering real 3D positions.</Definition>

42.1's `cv2.triangulatePoints` and 43.1's chained version both end with the same real thing: a list of 3D points, nothing more. That raw list is a **point cloud**, and it is almost never immediately usable — it is noisy, it may contain real outliers, and it carries no information yet about which way any surface actually faces. This chapter covers the real, standard next steps.

## 1. An analogy

Scattering a handful of sand onto a curved surface and looking only at where each grain landed gives a real sense of that surface's shape — but a single grain tells you nothing about which way the surface faces at that point, a few grains bounced somewhere else entirely are obviously wrong, and far more grains landed than you actually need to see the shape. Thinning the grains, discarding the obviously-wrong ones, and figuring out the local surface direction from each grain's neighbours is exactly this chapter's three real operations.

None of these three operations exists in core OpenCV 4.13 — a real, verified fact, not a guess: `hasattr(cv2, 'ptcloud')` is `False` on this course's pinned 4.13.0 (point clouds only get a dedicated module, `ptcloud`, in OpenCV 5.0 — 8.10's own real finding). Everything in this chapter is real, from-scratch NumPy, run on a synthetic test sphere so every result can be checked against a known ground truth.

## 3. A real, measured result: downsampling trades count for noise, not just detail

A synthetic sphere (radius 10, 4000 points, 0.05 units of Gaussian noise on every point's position) downsampled with a voxel grid — one averaged point per cubic cell — gives a real, measured surprise: going from 4000 points down to **1475** (voxel size 1.0) *improves* the mean distance-from-true-radius error, from the input's 0.05 noise down to **0.0283**. Averaging several noisy points together inside each voxel partly cancels their individual noise, the same real principle as averaging repeated measurements anywhere else in this course. Pushing further to **407** points (voxel size 2.0) gives **0.0353** — still below the input noise, though the trend is not perfectly monotonic beyond a point, since fewer, larger voxels also average over a less locally-flat patch of the (curved) sphere.

## 4. A real, measured result: outlier removal on a real test

200 outlier points, scattered uniformly through a region far larger than the sphere itself, were mixed into the same cloud. **Statistical outlier removal** — flagging any point whose mean distance to its 10 nearest neighbours is unusually large (more than `mean + ratio x std` of every point's neighbour-distance) — caught **90.0%** of the real outliers at a strict `ratio=1.0`, dropping to **76.0%** at a looser `ratio=3.0`, with **zero** real surface points wrongly flagged at *any* of the three tested ratios. This clean separation is itself a real, measured fact about *this* scene: these outliers sit in genuinely open space far from the dense, locally-crowded sphere surface, which is exactly the real property statistical outlier removal depends on (Section 5).

## 5. A real, measured result: normal estimation needs enough neighbours

<PointCloudLab mode="normals" caption="Real local-PCA normal estimation error (vs the known analytic sphere normal) as neighbourhood size grows." />

Estimating which way each point "faces" — its **normal** — from a local neighbourhood's smallest-variance direction (a direct reuse of 5.7/36.7's PCA) gave a real, measured **3.978°** mean angular error against the true analytic sphere normal at a 5-point neighbourhood, dropping to **0.653°** at 40 points: more neighbours average out more of the real 0.05-unit position noise, with diminishing returns beyond that on this gently-curved test sphere.

## 6. Industrial context: when a point cloud needs all three steps

A real structured-light or laser-triangulation scanner (42.4-42.5) or a photogrammetry rig (43.1) routinely produces millions of raw points, a real fraction of them outliers from missed correspondences or reflections — too many points to render or process at full density, with real junk mixed in, and no surface information yet for mesh reconstruction (44.3) or inspection. Section 3-5's three real operations, in that order, are the real, standard way every one of those systems turns "a pile of 3D dots" into something a later stage can actually use.

## Mini exercise

**Q1.** A point cloud has 4000 real surface points and some scattered outliers far from the surface. Based on Section 4, would you expect statistical outlier removal to work better or worse if the outliers were instead clustered tightly together in one small region, close to the true surface?

<Solution>

Worse. Statistical outlier removal depends on outliers having a large mean distance to their *own* nearest neighbours (Section 4's own real, clean 0%-false-positive result came from outliers scattered through open space). A tight cluster of outliers close to the surface would have small neighbour distances to *each other* (and to the nearby real surface), making them look locally dense and much harder to distinguish — a real limitation worth knowing before trusting this method on an unfamiliar dataset.

</Solution>

## Code

### 1. NumPy: all three real operations, from scratch

```python
import numpy as np

R, N_SURF = 10.0, 4000
rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
i = np.arange(N_SURF)
phi = np.arccos(1 - 2 * (i + 0.5) / N_SURF)
theta = np.pi * (1 + 5 ** 0.5) * i
surface = np.stack([R * np.sin(phi) * np.cos(theta), R * np.sin(phi) * np.sin(theta), R * np.cos(phi)], axis=1)
true_normals = surface / np.linalg.norm(surface, axis=1, keepdims=True)
pts = surface + rng.normal(0, 0.05, surface.shape)

# 1. Voxel downsampling: one averaged point per grid cell
def voxel_downsample(points, voxel_size):
    keys = np.floor(points / voxel_size).astype(np.int64)
    uniq, inv = np.unique(keys, axis=0, return_inverse=True)
    out = np.zeros((len(uniq), 3))
    for k in range(len(uniq)):
        out[k] = points[inv == k].mean(axis=0)
    return out

down = voxel_downsample(pts, 1.0)
print(f"voxel downsample: {len(pts)} -> {len(down)} points")
print(f"mean |radius error|: {np.abs(np.linalg.norm(down, axis=1) - R).mean():.4f}")

# 2. Local-PCA normal estimation (reuses 5.7/36.7's eigenvector-of-covariance idea)
def estimate_normal(points, idx, k):
    d = np.linalg.norm(points - points[idx], axis=1)
    nbrs = points[np.argsort(d)[1:k + 1]]
    centroid = nbrs.mean(axis=0)
    cov = (nbrs - centroid).T @ (nbrs - centroid) / len(nbrs)
    _, v = np.linalg.eigh(cov)
    return v[:, 0]  # eigenvector of the SMALLEST eigenvalue = the flattest direction = the normal

n_est = estimate_normal(pts, 0, 20)
ang_err = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(abs(np.dot(n_est, true_normals[0])), -1, 1)))
print(f"normal estimate (k=20) angular error on point 0: {ang_err:.3f} deg")
```

Output:

```text
voxel downsample: 4000 -> 1475 points
mean |radius error|: 0.0283
normal estimate (k=20) angular error on point 0: 1.324 deg
```

### 2. OpenCV: where the raw points actually come from

```python
import cv2

K = np.array([[800.0, 0, 320], [0, 800.0, 240], [0, 0, 1]])
true_pts = np.array([[1.0, 0.5, 10.0], [2.0, -1.0, 12.0], [-1.5, 0.8, 9.0], [0.0, 0.0, 11.0], [3.0, 1.2, 10.5]])
img1, _ = cv2.projectPoints(true_pts, np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3), K, None)
img2, _ = cv2.projectPoints(true_pts, np.zeros(3), np.array([-1.0, 0, 0]), K, None)

P1 = K @ np.hstack([np.eye(3), np.zeros((3, 1))])
P2 = K @ np.hstack([np.eye(3), np.array([[-1.0], [0], [0]])])
pts4d = cv2.triangulatePoints(P1, P2, img1.reshape(-1, 2).T, img2.reshape(-1, 2).T)
recovered = (pts4d[:3] / pts4d[3]).T
print("max abs error vs true points:", np.abs(recovered - true_pts).max())
```

Output:

```text
max abs error vs true points: 5.329070518200751e-15
```

Exactly 42.1's `cv2.triangulatePoints` call — this *is* how a real point cloud like this chapter's test sphere actually arrives in practice, before any of Section 3-5's own NumPy processing runs on it.

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function performs voxel downsampling, statistical outlier removal, or point-cloud normal estimation on OpenCV 4.13** — `hasattr(cv2, 'ptcloud')` is `False`, verified directly; these arrive only in OpenCV 5.0 (8.10). This chapter's NumPy implementations are the real, honest substitute on the pinned version.
- **`cv2.ppf_match_3d` does exist, via opencv-contrib** (`hasattr(cv2, 'ppf_match_3d')` is `True`) — it performs point-pair-feature 3D surface *matching* (recognising a known object's pose in a scene cloud), a different, more specialized problem from this chapter's cleanup operations, and out of this chapter's scope.
- **`cv2.triangulatePoints`** (Code section 2, identical to 42.1) is the real, standard source of a point cloud in an OpenCV pipeline — everything after it, in this chapter, is plain NumPy.

## Where it fails

- **Statistical outlier removal fails on clustered or near-surface outliers** (Mini exercise) — it depends on real outliers being locally sparse, which this chapter's own scattered test satisfied perfectly but a real sensor's correlated noise might not.
- **Local-PCA normals blur across real sharp edges and corners** — this chapter's test sphere has no edges to test that against; a real part with sharp corners would see neighbourhoods that straddle two different real faces, biasing the estimated normal toward neither one correctly.
- **Voxel downsampling at too coarse a size discards real small features** — Section 3's own result improved accuracy on a smooth sphere, but a voxel larger than a real feature of interest (a small bump, a thin rod) would average it away entirely.
- **None of this chapter's methods know anything about *connectivity*** — a point cloud, however clean, still has no concept of "these points form one continuous surface" until mesh reconstruction (44.3) is applied.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Assuming downsampling always costs accuracy** — Section 3's own real result (1475 points, lower error than the original 4000) shows the opposite can genuinely happen, when noise-averaging dominates.
- **Trusting statistical outlier removal on a scene where outliers are locally dense** — Section 4's own 0%-false-positive result depended on this chapter's specific, scattered outlier pattern; the Mini exercise's clustered case is a real, different situation.
- **Estimating a normal from too few neighbours** — Section 5's own real result: 5 neighbours gave a 3.978° mean error (and a real 10.209° worst case), nearly 4x this chapter's own 40-neighbour result.
- **Resolving the real sign ambiguity in a PCA-derived normal incorrectly** — the smallest-eigenvalue eigenvector in Code section 1 points in an arbitrary one of two opposite directions; a real pipeline must orient it consistently (e.g. toward a known sensor viewpoint), which this chapter's `abs(cos)` comparison deliberately sidestepped rather than solved.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Why voxel downsampling can reduce error

Averaging `m` independent, equally-noisy measurements reduces their combined standard deviation by a real factor of `1/sqrt(m)` (the same real statistics behind 5.4's mean/variance and every averaging operation in this course). A voxel containing several nearby noisy points produces one centroid whose noise is reduced by exactly this factor — Section 3's own real numbers are a direct, measured instance of this general, well-known result.

### Point-pair features and surface matching

`cv2.ppf_match_3d` (OpenCV API notes) implements the point-pair-feature method (Drost, Ulrich, Navab, Ilic, "Model Globally, Match Locally: Efficient and Robust 3D Object Recognition," CVPR 2010): it describes a surface by the relative positions and normal directions of many point pairs, then matches a known model against a noisy scene cloud by voting — a real, different problem from this chapter's cleanup, needing exactly the kind of normals Section 5 estimated as one of its own real inputs.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A quality team scans a metal part with a structured-light scanner (42.4) and gets a cloud with both real sensor noise and a cluster of bad points from a reflective surface (not scattered randomly, but concentrated in one bright region). Based on this chapter, would statistical outlier removal alone be a safe way to clean this cloud?

<Solution>

Not entirely safe by itself.
