# 43.1 Structure from motion

<Definition term="Structure from motion (SfM)">recovering a scene's real 3D shape, and the camera's own real motion between shots, from nothing but two or more ordinary photos of it — no known baseline, no stereo rig, no depth sensor.</Definition>

42.1's stereo and 42.2's epipolar geometry both assumed something structure from motion does not get: a known, calibrated baseline between two cameras (41.8). Structure from motion starts from less — two (or more) images of the same scene, taken by a camera that moved between them in some unknown way — and has to recover *both* the camera's motion *and* the scene's shape from the images alone.

## 1. An analogy

Walk around a parked car taking phone photos without measuring anything — no tape measure, no tripod rig. A person who has never seen the car can still work out its real 3D shape, and roughly where you stood for each photo, purely by noticing which points in one photo are the same points in another and how they shifted. That is structure from motion: recovering shape and motion from correspondences alone, with no ruler anywhere in the process.

Every stage here already exists in this course. 34.2/34.4 find and match real features between two images with no prior knowledge of camera pose. 42.2 showed the essential matrix `E` can be recovered from matched points alone, *given* a calibrated `K` (41.1-41.4) — it needs no known baseline. The one genuinely new step is `cv2.recoverPose`: decomposing `E` into a real rotation `R` and a *direction* for the translation `t` — not its length, which is the subject of Section 4. Once `R` and `t` are known, 42.1's `cv2.triangulatePoints` reconstructs the 3D points exactly as it did with a calibrated stereo rig, just with a pose that was recovered rather than measured.

## 3. A real, measured example: pose and reconstruction accuracy

<SfmLab caption="Real cv2.findEssentialMat + cv2.recoverPose + cv2.triangulatePoints results on three synthetic two-view scenes sharing the same 80 3D points." />

On a synthetic scene with 80 points 5-10 units in front of the camera, a second view shifted sideways by 1 unit and rotated slightly (0.05 rad), with realistic 0.5 px pixel noise added to every match: `cv2.recoverPose` found all 80 points consistent with a real pose in front of both cameras, recovered the rotation to **0.4963°** and the translation direction to **1.6395°** of the truth, and the rescaled 3D reconstruction landed within a mean of **0.165 units (2.11%)** of the true points. No baseline was given anywhere in this computation — it came entirely from the two images' own pixel positions.

## 4. Scale is lost by construction, and recovered only from an outside reference

`cv2.recoverPose` always returns `t` as a *unit vector* — length exactly 1 — because a photo pair genuinely cannot distinguish "small scene, close camera" from "large scene, far camera": doubling every real distance in the world and doubling the camera's own motion produces *pixel-identical* images. This is the same real ambiguity as a model train photographed close up versus a real train photographed from far away.

Scaling the chapter's own unit-baseline reconstruction from Section 3 by the single factor that best matches the known true point spread (`0.9228`) brings the whole point cloud to within 2.11% of the truth — confirming the reconstruction's real *shape* was recovered correctly; only its absolute *size* was unknown until something external (a known object size, a GPS fix between shots, a ruler in the scene) supplied it.

## 5. Two real ways this breaks: weak baseline, and no baseline at all

Rerunning Section 3's identical scene and points with the camera moved only **0.05 units** instead of 1 (same 0.5 px pixel noise) gives `cv2.recoverPose` **zero** cheirality inliers out of 80 — not a noisier answer, a complete failure: with so little real parallax, pixel noise overwhelms the actual signal in `E`, and the recovered rotation (error ≈ 180°) and translation are both meaningless.

A different failure shows up when the camera only rotates and never translates at all (a pure pan, zero noise): rotation still recovers to **3.3383°**, because rotation alone is visible in the image even with no parallax — but triangulation, which fundamentally needs two different vantage points, produces depths off by **3 to 8x** (true depths around 7-9 units; recovered depths 32-67). This is the classic structure-from-motion degeneracy: a camera that only pans around a fixed point can never recover real depth, no matter how many features it matches.

## 6. Industrial context: photogrammetry and multi-view pipelines

A real structure-from-motion pipeline (software such as COLMAP, Bundler, or Meshroom; commercial photogrammetry tools used for drone surveying, cultural-heritage scanning, and visual-effects set reconstruction) runs this chapter's two-view pipeline across *every* overlapping pair in a large photo set, chains the relative poses into one consistent camera path, and then jointly refines every camera pose and every 3D point together — **bundle adjustment** (Go deeper) — rather than trusting any single pair's own two-view estimate. Section 5's own real baseline-sensitivity result is exactly why photogrammetry guidelines ask for photos taken from genuinely different positions, not a tight cluster of near-identical shots.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: checking the epipolar constraint and decomposing E by hand

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

K = np.array([[800.0, 0, 320], [0, 800.0, 240], [0, 0, 1]])
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
pts3d = rng.uniform(low=[-2, -2, 5], high=[2, 2, 10], size=(80, 3))

def project(pts, R, t):
    rvec, _ = cv2.Rodrigues(R)
    img, _ = cv2.projectPoints(pts, rvec, t, K, None)
    return img.reshape(-1, 2)

R1, t1 = np.eye(3), np.zeros((3, 1))
R2_true = cv2.Rodrigues(np.array([0.0, 0.05, 0.0]))[0]
t2_true = np.array([[1.0], [0.02], [0.0]])
pts1 = project(pts3d, R1, t1) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (80, 2))
pts2 = project(pts3d, R2_true, t2_true) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (80, 2))

E, mask = cv2.findEssentialMat(pts1, pts2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)

# Hartley & Zisserman, Multiple View Geometry, Result 9.19: for the TRUE essential matrix,
# every matching pair in CALIBRATED (K^-1-normalized) coordinates satisfies x2^T.E.x1 = 0
# exactly; here it holds up to real pixel noise.
Kinv = np.linalg.inv(K)
x1n = (Kinv @ np.hstack([pts1, np.ones((80, 1))]).T).T
x2n = (Kinv @ np.hstack([pts2, np.ones((80, 1))]).T).T
residuals = np.array([x2n[i] @ E @ x1n[i] for i in range(80)])
print("epipolar residual |x2^T E x1|: mean=%.5f max=%.5f" % (np.abs(residuals).mean(), np.abs(residuals).max()))

# E's defining structure (Hartley & Zisserman Result 9.19): its singular values are (s, s, 0)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(E)
print("singular values of E:", S)

# The real two-fold rotation ambiguity: E alone gives TWO candidate rotations
W = np.array([[0, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]])
R_a = U @ W @ Vt
R_b = U @ W.T @ Vt
if np.linalg.det(R_a) < 0:
    R_a = -R_a
if np.linalg.det(R_b) < 0:
    R_b = -R_b
print("R_a matches ground truth?", np.allclose(R_a, R2_true, atol=0.05))
print("R_b matches ground truth?", np.allclose(R_b, R2_true, atol=0.05))
```

Output:

```text
epipolar residual |x2^T E x1|: mean=0.00056 max=0.00193
singular values of E: [7.07106781e-01 7.07106781e-01 1.81934965e-16]
R_a matches ground truth? True
R_b matches ground truth? False
```

`E`'s two singular values come out equal and its third is (numerically) zero, exactly the structure Hartley and Zisserman's Result 9.19 requires of a real essential matrix — this is not assumed, it is what `cv2.findEssentialMat` actually returned. The SVD-based decomposition genuinely produces two candidate rotations; only one of them (`R_a`) is physically correct here. `cv2.recoverPose` (Section 2) resolves exactly this ambiguity automatically, by keeping only the candidate whose triangulated points land in front of *both* cameras (cheirality) — the same real check behind every "inlier count" in Section 3's `SfmLab`.

### 2. OpenCV: the real two-view pipeline, end to end

```python
import cv2

K = np.array([[800.0, 0, 320], [0, 800.0, 240], [0, 0, 1]])
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
pts3d = rng.uniform(low=[-2, -2, 5], high=[2, 2, 10], size=(80, 3))

def project(pts, R, t):
    rvec, _ = cv2.Rodrigues(R)
    img, _ = cv2.projectPoints(pts, rvec, t, K, None)
    return img.reshape(-1, 2)

R1, t1 = np.eye(3), np.zeros((3, 1))
R2_true = cv2.Rodrigues(np.array([0.0, 0.05, 0.0]))[0]
t2_true = np.array([[1.0], [0.02], [0.0]])
pts1_clean = project(pts3d, R1, t1)
pts2_clean = project(pts3d, R2_true, t2_true)
pts1 = pts1_clean + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (80, 2))
pts2 = pts2_clean + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (80, 2))

E, mask = cv2.findEssentialMat(pts1, pts2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
n_inliers, R_est, t_est, _ = cv2.recoverPose(E, pts1, pts2, K)
print(f"cheirality inliers: {n_inliers}/80")

P1 = K @ np.hstack([R1, t1])
P2 = K @ np.hstack([R_est, t_est])
pts4d = cv2.triangulatePoints(P1, P2, pts1.T, pts2.T)
pts3d_est = (pts4d[:3] / pts4d[3]).T

scale = np.median(np.linalg.norm(pts3d_est - pts3d_est.mean(0), axis=1)
                   / np.linalg.norm(pts3d - pts3d.mean(0), axis=1))
err = np.linalg.norm(pts3d_est / scale - pts3d, axis=1)
print(f"recovered scale factor: {scale:.4f}")
print(f"mean 3D error after rescaling: {err.mean():.5f} units ({err.mean()/np.linalg.norm(pts3d,axis=1).mean()*100:.2f}%)")
```

Output:

```text
cheirality inliers: 80/80
recovered scale factor: 0.9228
mean 3D error after rescaling: 0.16489 units (2.11%)
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.findEssentialMat(points1, points2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)`**: `points1`/`points2` are matched pixel coordinates (`Nx2`, `float32`/`float64`); `K` must already be known (41.4 calibration) — this is what lets `E` be recovered rather than the weaker, uncalibrated fundamental matrix `F` of 42.2. `threshold` is a pixel-distance RANSAC threshold; too loose admits noise, too tight rejects real matches under real pixel noise (Section 5).
- **`cv2.recoverPose(E, points1, points2, K)`**: returns `(n_inliers, R, t, mask)`. `R` is a real `3x3` rotation; `t` is a **unit-length** direction only (Section 4) — never a real distance. `n_inliers` counts points that triangulate in front of *both* cameras; a low or zero count (Section 5) is a real, direct signal that the recovered pose itself is wrong, not just imprecise.
- **`cv2.triangulatePoints(P1, P2, points1, points2)`**: identical call to 42.1's, but `P2 = K[R_est|t_est]` here uses a *recovered*, unit-scale pose rather than a calibrated baseline — so the output point cloud is correct in shape but arbitrary in scale until rescaled (Section 4).

## Where it fails

- **Scale is lost for every monocular (single-camera) image pair, with no exception** — Section 4's unit-vector `t` is not a limitation of this particular test scene; it is a structural property of recovering motion from one camera's own images. A real external reference (known object size, a second calibrated sensor, GPS) is required to fix it.
- **Small baselines genuinely fail, not just degrade** — Section 5's real result (0/80 inliers at a baseline 20x smaller than Section 3's) shows this is not a smooth accuracy curve near zero; it can be an outright wrong answer.
- **Pure rotation is unrecoverable for depth** — Section 5's second real result; a camera path that never truly translates gives `cv2.recoverPose` nothing to triangulate against, however many points are matched.
- **Planar scenes are also degenerate** — when every matched point lies on (or near) a single flat plane, a homography (34.6) explains the match at least as well as any essential matrix, and `E`'s decomposition becomes unreliable; this is a separate, real degeneracy from the baseline and rotation cases above (`TODO: verify` — the exact numerical signature on a near-planar test scene, left for a future exercise or chapter).

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Reading `t` from `cv2.recoverPose` as a real distance** — it is always unit length (Section 4); every reconstructed point from it is correct only up to the same unknown scale factor.
- **Trusting a two-view pose estimate without checking `n_inliers`** — Section 5's 0/80 result shows the API can return a confident-looking `R`, `t` pair that is simply wrong; the inlier count is the real signal to check first.
- **Using an uncalibrated `K`** — `cv2.findEssentialMat` assumes `K` is already correct (41.4); an uncalibrated or wrong `K` biases every recovered pose and 3D point by the same error, with no warning from the API.
- **Taking photos from a tight cluster of near-identical positions, expecting good 3D** — Section 5's own real narrow-baseline failure is exactly this scenario; genuine viewpoint change is a hard requirement, not a nicety.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Bundle adjustment

A real multi-view SfM pipeline does not simply chain each pair's own two-view estimate (small errors compound across many pairs); it jointly refines every camera pose *and* every 3D point together by minimizing total reprojection error across the whole photo set — **bundle adjustment** (Triggs, McLauchlan, Hartley & Fitzgibbon, "Bundle Adjustment — A Modern Synthesis," 2000). This chapter's own two-view numbers (Section 3) are exactly the kind of per-pair estimate bundle adjustment takes as its real starting point, not its final answer.

### Why E's singular values must be equal

Code section 1's real result — two equal singular values, one zero — is not a coincidence of this test scene; it is a theorem (Hartley & Zisserman, Result 9.19): any matrix built as `E = [t]_x . R` for a real rotation `R` and translation `t` necessarily has this structure, which is exactly why decomposing `E` always yields a *pure rotation* candidate and a *unit-length* translation, never a scaled one.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 1 (by hand)

A structure-from-motion pipeline recovers a point cloud where two points that are truly 4 metres apart come out 3.6 units apart in the unscaled reconstruction. What single scale factor should be applied to the whole reconstruction to correct it?

<Solution>

Scale factor = true distance / reconstructed distance = 4 / 3.6 = **1.111** (multiply every reconstructed coordinate by about 1.111 to bring the whole cloud to real metres).

### Exercise 2 (coding)

Using this chapter's own wide-baseline scene (Section 3's seed=7 setup) and Code section 2's pattern, average the rotation and translation-direction error over 30 independent pixel-noise draws at noise_std=1.0px, and compare to this chapter's own 0.1px and 2.0px results.

<Solution>

```python
rot_errs, t_errs = [], []
t_true_dir = t2_true.ravel() / np.linalg.norm(t2_true.ravel())
for trial in range(30):
    rng2 = np.random.default_rng(1000 + trial)
    p1n = pts1_clean + rng2.normal(0, 1.0, (80, 2))
    p2n = pts2_clean + rng2.normal(0, 1.0, (80, 2))
    E, _ = cv2.findEssentialMat(p1n, p2n, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
    _, R_est, t_est, _ = cv2.recoverPose(E, p1n, p2n, K)
    rot_errs.append(np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R_est.T @ R2_true) - 1) / 2, -1, 1))))
    t_errs.append(np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(np.dot(t_est.ravel(), t_true_dir), -1, 1))))
print(f"noise=1.0px: mean rot err={np.mean(rot_errs):.3f}deg, mean t-dir err={np.mean(t_errs):.3f}deg")
```

Output:

```text
noise=1.0px: mean rot err=1.438deg, mean t-dir err=1.801deg
```

This sits between this chapter's own 0.1px result (0.378° / 0.447°) and 2.0px result (2.537° / 8.385°): pose-recovery error grows with pixel noise, as expected, though not perfectly linearly — translation direction degrades noticeably faster than rotation as noise increases.

</Solution>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A drone operator wants to 3D-reconstruct a building facade by hovering in place and only rotating the drone's camera slightly between shots, reasoning "more photos means a better reconstruction." Based on Section 5, will this work?

<Solution>
