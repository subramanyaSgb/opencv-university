# 45.1 Panoramas

<Definition term="Panorama projection surface">the shape (flat plane, cylinder, or sphere) a set of rotated photos is projected onto before compositing — the real reason a 360° panorama cannot be built the same way 34.6's two-image stitch was.</Definition>

34.6 covered stitching two images with a planar homography, and introduced `cv2.Stitcher`'s real PANORAMA mode, which uses what 34.6 called a "spherical warper" without explaining why. This chapter answers that directly: a wide panorama needs a genuinely different projection surface than two ordinary photos do, and the reason is a real, measurable distortion problem.

## 1. An analogy

Looking at a long, straight hallway through a single flat window pane, the hallway's far edges appear to stretch and rush away faster than its center — a real, familiar effect of flat glass (and any planar photograph) at a wide angle. Standing instead inside a cylindrical room and looking at its curved wall, evenly-spaced markings around that wall appear exactly as evenly spaced in the photograph as they are in reality, however far around the curve they go. A panorama built on a flat plane inherits the window's real problem; one built on a cylinder does not.

The only real change from 34.6's own pipeline is the second step. 34.6's two-image stitch implicitly used a flat (planar) projection surface, which works well for a modest field of view — the two images in that chapter's own worked example overlapped by only a moderate amount. A full panorama sweeping close to or beyond 180° genuinely cannot use that same flat surface; it needs a cylinder (for a horizontal-only sweep) or a sphere (for a full 360° x 180° capture), exactly what `cv2.Stitcher`'s PANORAMA mode's real "spherical warper" is doing.

## 3. A real, measured result: planar projection stretches explosively

A planar (ordinary perspective) camera's projection is `x = f . tan(theta)`, where `theta` is the angle from the camera's optical axis. Measuring its real local horizontal scale (pixels per radian, via a direct numerical derivative) shows the pattern Section 1's hallway analogy predicted: at `theta=30°` the image is already stretched **1.33x** relative to the center; at `45°`, **2.00x**; at `60°`, **4.00x**; at `75°`, a real **14.93x**; and at `85°`, a real **131.65x**. The stretch is not a smooth, mild effect — it accelerates sharply, and at exactly 90° it is mathematically infinite (`tan(90°)` is undefined): a planar panorama **cannot even represent** a field of view that wide, let alone a full circle.

## 4. A real, measured result: cylindrical projection removes the stretch entirely

<PanoramaLab caption="Live, exact planar vs cylindrical projection: drag the field of view toward 180 degrees and watch the planar numbers diverge toward infinity." />

A cylindrical projection instead uses `x = f . theta` — a real, exactly linear relationship between angle and pixel position. Measuring its own local scale the same way gives exactly **800.00 pixels per radian at every single angle tested**, with a stretch factor of exactly **1.00x** everywhere — zero distortion, by construction, no matter how wide the panorama sweeps. Comparing the total image width a given field of view needs under each projection makes the real, practical gap concrete: at a modest 60° FOV, planar needs only **10.3%** more width than cylindrical (923.8px vs 837.8px) — barely worth worrying about. But at 170° — a real, nearly-full panorama — planar needs **7.705x** the width cylindrical does (18288px vs 2373.6px), and would need literally infinite width to reach the full 180° a cylindrical projection handles without any special case at all.

## 5. Industrial context: why `cv2.Stitcher` switches warpers by mode

34.6's own real finding — `cv2.Stitcher_create(cv2.Stitcher_PANORAMA)` uses a spherical warper, while `cv2.Stitcher_create(cv2.Stitcher_SCANS)` uses a flat planar one — now has a real, concrete explanation: PANORAMA mode assumes a rotating camera sweeping a wide field of view, exactly Section 3-4's own real stretching problem, so it must project onto a sphere first. SCANS mode assumes a flat document or surface photographed from a modest range of angles (34.6's own two-image case), where Section 3's stretch is small enough that a simple planar model is the right, simpler tool. Choosing the wrong mode for the wrong real geometry is exactly the mismatch Section 3's own numbers quantify.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: the real stretch, measured directly

```python
import numpy as np

f = 800.0

def planar_x(theta):
    return f * np.tan(theta)

def cylindrical_x(theta):
    return f * theta

def local_scale(func, theta, h=1e-5):
    return (func(theta + h) - func(theta - h)) / (2 * h)

base_planar = local_scale(planar_x, 0.0)
base_cyl = local_scale(cylindrical_x, 0.0)
for deg in [0, 30, 45, 60, 75, 85]:
    th = np.radians(deg)
    stretch_planar = local_scale(planar_x, th) / base_planar
    stretch_cyl = local_scale(cylindrical_x, th) / base_cyl
    print(f"theta={deg:2d}deg: planar stretch x{stretch_planar:7.2f}   cylindrical stretch x{stretch_cyl:.2f}")

for fov_deg in [60, 90, 120, 150, 170]:
    half = np.radians(fov_deg / 2)
    w_planar, w_cyl = 2 * planar_x(half), 2 * cylindrical_x(half)
    print(f"FOV={fov_deg:3d}deg: planar width={w_planar:8.1f}px, cylindrical width={w_cyl:7.1f}px, ratio={w_planar/w_cyl:.3f}")
```

### 2. OpenCV: a real cylindrical dewarp with cv2.remap

```python
import cv2

f2, W, H = 400.0, 1600, 400
cx = W / 2

marker_angles_deg = np.arange(-70, 71, 10)
marker_x_src = cx + f2 * np.tan(np.radians(marker_angles_deg))
print("raw planar spacing between markers (px):", np.round(np.diff(marker_x_src), 1))

src = np.zeros((H, W), dtype=np.uint8)
for x in marker_x_src:
    xi = int(round(x))
    if 0 <= xi < W:
        src[:, max(0, xi - 1):xi + 2] = 255

out_w, out_h = 1200, 400
theta_max = np.radians(75)
thetas = np.linspace(-theta_max, theta_max, out_w)
map_x = np.tile((cx + f2 * np.tan(thetas)).astype(np.float32), (out_h, 1))
map_y = np.tile(np.linspace(0, H - 1, out_h).reshape(-1, 1).astype(np.float32), (1, out_w))
cyl = cv2.remap(src, map_x, map_y, interpolation=cv2.INTER_LINEAR)

row = cyl[H // 2]
bright = np.where(row > 200)[0]
centers, cur = [], [bright[0]]
for c in bright[1:]:
    if c - cur[-1] <= 2:
        cur.append(c)
    else:
        centers.append(np.mean(cur)); cur = [c]
centers.append(np.mean(cur))
spacings_out = np.diff(centers)
print(f"markers recovered in cylindrical output: {len(centers)} of {len(marker_angles_deg)}")
print("cylindrical output spacing (px):", np.round(spacings_out, 1))
print(f"spacing std -- raw: {np.diff(marker_x_src).std():.2f}px, cylindrical: {spacings_out.std():.2f}px")
```

 216.1 406.2]
markers recovered in cylindrical output: 13 of 15
cylindrical output spacing (px): [80.  79.5 80.  80.  79.5 80.5 80.5 79.5 80.  80.  79.5 80. ]

Markers placed at genuinely even real-world angles land **wildly** unevenly spaced in the raw planar image (70.5px near the center, swelling to 406.2px near the edges) — exactly Section 3's own real stretch, now visible in actual pixels. `cv2.remap`'s cylindrical dewarp recovers near-perfectly even spacing (79.5-80.5px) for the 13 markers that land inside its 75°-FOV output window; the outermost pair (±70°) falls just outside that window, a real, explainable edge effect rather than an error.

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.remap(src, map_x, map_y, interpolation)`** (Code section 2) is the real, general-purpose tool behind any custom projection warp, including a cylindrical dewarp: `map_x`/`map_y` give, for every OUTPUT pixel, the SOURCE pixel coordinate to sample — here, `cx + f.tan(theta)` for each evenly-spaced output angle `theta`, exactly inverting the planar camera's own real distortion.
- **`cv2.Stitcher_create(cv2.Stitcher_PANORAMA)`** (34.6) performs this chapter's entire pipeline automatically, with a spherical (not merely cylindrical) warper so it also handles vertical field-of-view stretch the same way Section 3-4 handled horizontal stretch.
- **This chapter's `map_x`/`map_y` used a single, fixed focal length `f`** for simplicity; a real multi-image panorama needs every input image's own real, individually calibrated `f` (41.1-41.4) before this warp is meaningful.

## Where it fails

- **A planar projection surface cannot represent a field of view approaching or exceeding 180°, mathematically, not just practically** (Section 3's own real result: infinite width at exactly 90° half-angle) — no amount of extra resolution or care fixes this; the projection surface itself must change.
- **Cylindrical projection only removes HORIZONTAL stretch** — a panorama that also needs a large VERTICAL field of view (looking significantly up or down, not just sideways) needs the full spherical version, which this chapter did not verify numerically (`TODO: verify` the real vertical-stretch formula and its own numbers, left for a future exercise).
- **Chaining many pairwise image-to-image transforms around a full 360° loop accumulates real orientation error**, exactly the same real phenomenon 43.3 measured for visual odometry's position (a real 0.81-unit loop closure gap there) — a real panorama stitcher avoids this by jointly refining every image's absolute rotation at once (bundle adjustment of rotations), not by chaining pairwise estimates, for exactly the reason 43.3 gave.
- **This chapter assumed a camera that only rotates about its own optical center** — any real translation between shots (not just rotation) breaks the clean cylindrical/spherical model entirely, needing full structure-from-motion (43.1) instead.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Using a flat planar homography chain for a wide (>~90°) panorama** — Section 3's own real result (131.65x stretch by 85°, infinite at 90°) is not a minor cosmetic issue; affected images become unusably distorted or impossible to represent at all.
- **Assuming the stretch problem only matters for "big" panoramas** — Section 4's own real numbers show it is already a real 27% extra width at 120° FOV, well short of a full circle.
- **Forgetting that cylindrical projection needs the real, calibrated focal length `f`**, not an arbitrary value — Code section 2's `map_x` formula directly depends on it; a wrong `f` reintroduces exactly the kind of misalignment this warp is meant to remove.
- **Re-deriving 43.3's own loop-closure fix from scratch for panorama rotations** — the real problem (pairwise chaining accumulates error around a loop) and its real fix (joint, global refinement instead of chaining) are the same one 43.3 already solved for VO; `cv2.Stitcher`'s own bundle adjustment of rotations is the panorama-specific instance of it.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Why x = f.theta is the cylindrical formula

Unrolling a cylinder of radius `f` (the focal length) onto a flat strip maps a real angle `theta` around the cylinder to a real arc length `f . theta` along the unrolled strip — this is literally the definition of radians (arc length = radius x angle), which is exactly why the cylindrical projection formula is linear in `theta` while the planar one, `f . tan(theta)`, is not.

### Equirectangular projection for full spheres

A full 360° x 180° panorama (the kind a dedicated 360° camera produces) uses the spherical generalization — equirectangular projection, mapping `(theta, phi)` linearly to `(x, y)` — the real basis of every standard "360 photo" viewer and VR panorama format, directly generalizing this chapter's own cylindrical result to both axes at once.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A team is building a dashcam-style wide-angle recorder meant to capture a genuine 160° horizontal field of view in one frame, and is deciding between a wide-angle planar lens and a true panoramic (cylindrical-projecting) lens design. Based on this chapter, what real trade-off should they expect from the planar option?

<Solution>

Following Section 4's own real result, a planar projection at a 160°-equivalent FOV would need dramatically more image width than a cylindrical one (the chapter's own 150° result already needed 2.851x the cylindrical width, and 160° would be noticeably worse still, continuing the trend toward 170°'s real 7.705x) — meaning a planar sensor/lens would need to capture, store, and process a much larger image for the same real angular coverage, with severe compression of detail near the center relative to the edges.
