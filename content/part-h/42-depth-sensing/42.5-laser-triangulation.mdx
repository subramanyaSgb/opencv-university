# 42.5 Laser triangulation

<Definition term="Laser triangulation">recovering real height from where a laser line (projected at a known, fixed angle) appears in a camera's image — a third real way to get depth, using one known-geometry light source instead of a second camera (42.1) or several sequential patterns (42.4).</Definition>

42.1 used two cameras; 42.4 used one camera and several projected patterns. This chapter uses one camera and a single, continuously-on laser line, at a known fixed angle — a different real trade-off, common in high-speed industrial profile measurement.

## 1. An analogy

Shining a flashlight at a fixed, known angle across a table and watching where its bright line lands tells you exactly how high the table's surface is at every point the line crosses — raise an object under the beam, and the bright line visibly shifts, by an amount that depends only on the known beam angle and how far it moved. A laser triangulation sensor is exactly this, with a real camera reading the shift precisely instead of an eye estimating it.

For a real laser-camera baseline `B = 150 mm` and laser angle `theta = 20°`, a surface at height offsets from −30 mm to +60 mm (true `Z` from 470 to 560 mm) produces real pixel positions from **284.14 px down to 243.11 px** — the laser spot moves steadily across the image as height changes. Inverting the formula, `Z = B / (x_norm + tan(theta))` where `x_norm = (u − cx)/fx`, recovered every one of those true heights to within **10⁻¹³ mm** — exact, given the known geometry.

## 3. A real, measured baseline-sensitivity relationship

<LaserTriangulationLab caption="The real laser triangulation formula, baseline-sensitivity, and shadow-length relationships, all live and exact."/>

Exactly like 42.1's stereo baseline, a wider laser-camera baseline makes laser triangulation more precise for the identical pixel measurement error:

| Baseline | Height error from a 1 px measurement error |
| --- | --- |
| 50 mm | 6.250 mm |
| 100 mm | 3.125 mm |
| 150 mm | 2.083 mm |
| 300 mm | 1.042 mm |

Doubling the baseline exactly halves the real error — the identical geometric trade-off 42.1 measured for stereo, because both methods are, at their core, the same triangulation idea, just using a known angle instead of a second camera's own image.

## 4. A real, measured cost: shadows

A laser at a shallow angle casts a real, long shadow behind any step or ridge on the surface — a region the laser line simply cannot reach, and therefore cannot measure:

| Laser angle | Shadow behind a 5mm step |
| --- | --- |
| 10° | 28.36 mm |
| 20° | 13.74 mm |
| 45° | 5.00 mm |
| 70° | 1.82 mm |

A shallow 10° angle casts a shadow over **5.6× longer** than the step itself; a steep 70° angle shrinks it to barely a third of the step's own height. This is a real, geometric trade-off against Section 3's baseline-sensitivity relationship — a real system cannot simply maximise both baseline and angle freely without consequence.

## 5. Industrial context: why laser triangulation earns its place

A fast-moving conveyor needing a continuous height profile, line after line, benefits from laser triangulation's single, always-on measurement — no multi-frame pattern sequence (42.4) to stay synchronised with a moving part, and no second camera's own calibration (41.8) and matching cost (42.3) to maintain. The real cost, measured directly in Sections 3–4, is a genuine trade-off between precision (wider baseline, steeper angle) and real coverage (shallower angle sees around more obstructions, but a wider baseline and steeper angle both increase self-occlusion risk on a complex part shape) — a real engineering choice, not a free upgrade over the other two methods.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: exact triangulation and its inverse, verified

```python
import numpy as np

B, theta_deg, Z0 = 150.0, 20.0, 500.0
theta = np.deg2rad(theta_deg)
fx, cx = 800.0, 320.0

for delta_h in [-30, 0, 60]:
    Z_true = Z0 + delta_h
    X_laser = B - Z_true * np.tan(theta)
    u = fx * X_laser / Z_true + cx                 # forward: real projection
    x_norm = (u - cx) / fx
    Z_recovered = B / (x_norm + np.tan(theta))      # inverse: real triangulation
    print(f"Z_true={Z_true}, u={u:.2f}px, Z_recovered={Z_recovered:.6f}")
```

Output:

```text
Z_true=470.0, u=284.14px, Z_recovered=470.000000
Z_true=500.0, u=268.82px, Z_recovered=500.000000
Z_true=560.0, u=243.11px, Z_recovered=560.000000
```

### 2. OpenCV: the same forward projection, verified independently

```python
import cv2

pt3d = np.array([[X_laser, 0.0, Z_true]])          # X_laser, Z_true: this chapter's own last test case
img, _ = cv2.projectPoints(pt3d, np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3),
                            np.array([[fx, 0, cx], [0, fx, cx], [0, 0, 1]]), None)
print("cv2.projectPoints pixel:", img.reshape(-1)[0])
```

Output:

```text
cv2.projectPoints pixel: 243.1095268727524
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **No dedicated `cv2` function performs laser-line triangulation directly** — the real geometry (Code section 1) is plain trigonometry built on the same `K`, `cv2.projectPoints` primitives 41.1–42.1 already established.
- **Finding the real laser line's pixel position in a real image** typically uses a simple brightness-weighted centroid per row/column (the laser line is usually by far the brightest feature when the right optical filter and exposure are used) — a different, simpler problem from 42.3's full block-matching search.
- **`cv2.triangulatePoints`** (42.1) still applies conceptually once the laser's own known geometry is expressed as a second "camera" — some real systems do implement laser triangulation exactly this way, reusing stereo infrastructure with one "camera" replaced by the laser's known projection geometry.

## Where it fails

- **Shadows behind steps or ridges** (Section 4) are a real, geometric blind spot, not a measurement-noise problem — no amount of signal processing recovers a surface the laser line never actually reached.
- **A shiny or very dark surface** can fail to produce a clean, detectable laser line at all, the same real physical problem 42.4's structured light faces with reflective or absorbent surfaces.
- **Ambient light or a second reflective surface** can introduce a spurious, bright line the real detector might mistake for the true laser line — real systems often use a narrow optical bandpass filter matched to the laser's own wavelength to reject this.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Maximising baseline or angle without checking the real shadow/occlusion cost** — Section 4's own measured trade-off shows a real, physical limit, not just a tuning preference.
- **Assuming a steeper angle is always better** — it reduces shadowing (Section 4) but is not free; a real system's complete trade-off includes self-occlusion from the part's own shape at steep angles too, not modelled in this chapter's simplified flat-surface test.
- **Measuring the laser line's position with less than real sub-pixel precision** — Section 3's own real sensitivity numbers (2-6mm of height error per pixel of measurement error) assume whatever real detection precision is actually achieved; a cruder, whole-pixel-only detector inherits the full real error this chapter measured.
- **Forgetting that this chapter's formula assumes a perfectly flat reference geometry** — a real laser plane's actual shape (and any real lens distortion, 41.3) needs its own calibration for true industrial accuracy.

</Callout>

### Laser line scanners in practice

A real industrial laser profile sensor sweeps (or the part itself moves under a fixed line) to build up a full 2D height map, one 1D profile line at a time — exactly 2.10's line-scan camera idea (an earlier module), combined with this chapter's real triangulation, to turn a moving part into a complete 3D surface measurement without ever needing a second camera.

### Comparing the three real depth methods of this module

Passive stereo (42.1–42.3) needs real scene texture but only ambient light; structured light (42.4) needs an active projector and several patterns but works on featureless surfaces; laser triangulation (this chapter) needs only one active, known-angle light source and one camera, trading real coverage (shadows) for simplicity and speed. None of the three is universally best — each solves a genuinely different combination of real constraints.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A line is inspecting parts with deep, narrow slots. A colleague suggests a very steep laser angle (close to 90°) "to avoid shadows entirely." Based on Section 4-5, is that achievable, and what else should they check?

<Solution>

A steeper angle does reduce shadow length (Section 4's own real, verified trend: shadow shrinks toward zero as theta approaches 90°) but a 90° angle itself would mean the laser plane is parallel to the camera's view direction, geometrically degenerate for triangulation (no real baseline-like leverage left to measure depth from, an extreme case this chapter's formula does not handle gracefully). Beyond that geometric limit, Section 5 notes a real, separate concern at steep angles: self-occlusion from the part's own shape (a deep, narrow slot can block the camera's view of the laser line even without a shadow, simply because the slot's own walls are in the way) — the colleague should check real line-of-sight geometry for their specific slot shape and camera position, not just the shadow-length formula alone.

</Solution>
