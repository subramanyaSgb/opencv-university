# 42.6 Time of flight

<Definition term="Time of flight (ToF)">recovering real distance from how long light actually takes to travel to a surface and back, using the one real, exactly-known constant every other method in this module never needed: the speed of light.</Definition>

42.1 through 42.5 all measured depth through triangulation — an angle between two known viewpoints, or a known-angle light source. Time of flight needs none of that: a single light source and a single detector, timing a real round trip, is enough on its own.

## 1. An analogy

Shouting across a canyon and timing the echo tells you the canyon's real width, using nothing but the known speed of sound and a stopwatch — no second listener on the far side needed. Time-of-flight depth sensing is exactly this, with light instead of sound, and real electronics standing in for the stopwatch.

## 2. Direct ToF: a real, extremely short round trip

Light travels at a real, exactly-known `c = 299,792,458 m/s`. For a surface just 1 metre away, the real round-trip time is **6.671 nanoseconds** — for 10 metres, **66.713 nanoseconds**. These are genuinely, extraordinarily short real durations, which is exactly why direct ToF sensors need equally extraordinary real timing electronics.

## 3. How much timing precision does real depth precision actually need?

Rearranging `distance = c.t/2` shows exactly how much timing precision a given real depth precision demands:

| Target depth precision | Required timing precision |
| --- | --- |
| 1 mm | 6.67 picoseconds |
| 10 mm | 66.71 picoseconds |
| 100 mm | 667.13 picoseconds |

Millimetre-level depth precision needs **picosecond**-level timing precision — a real, extremely demanding requirement, explaining why many practical ToF depth cameras use a different real approach for everyday ranges instead of trying to time individual pulses directly.

## 4. Phase-based ToF, and a real, measured failure mode

<TimeOfFlightLab caption="Both real ToF formulas, live. Push the distance slider past the max unambiguous range and watch the phase-recovered distance wrap to a real, wrong, closer-looking value."/>

Continuous-wave (phase-based) ToF modulates the light's brightness at a known frequency and measures the real phase shift of the returning signal instead of timing one pulse directly — a far less demanding real electronics problem. At a real `20 MHz` modulation frequency, the real maximum unambiguous range is `c / (2.f_mod) = 7.495 m`. Within that range, real distances recover exactly: `1.0 m → 1.0000 m`, `3.0 m → 3.0000 m`, `6.0 m → 6.0000 m`. Just past it, at `7.5 m`, the phase wraps and naively recovers **0.0052 m** — essentially zero. At `9.0 m`, it wraps to **1.5052 m**: a real, confidently wrong answer that looks exactly like a genuine nearby object, not an error or a warning.

## 5. Industrial context: ToF's real niche

A scene where triangulation's baseline or known-angle geometry (42.1–42.5) would be impractical — very long range, a single small sensor package, or a scene where no second viewpoint can physically fit — is where ToF earns its real place, at the real cost of either extreme timing precision (direct ToF) or a real, bounded unambiguous range that must be chosen to safely exceed the scene's actual maximum real distance (phase ToF, Section 4). A system that might see something beyond its chosen modulation frequency's real range needs either a lower frequency (a longer real range, at the cost of precision, since the same phase measurement precision now covers more real distance) or a second, different modulation frequency to resolve the real ambiguity directly.

## Code

### 1. NumPy: real direct-ToF timing and the required precision

```python
import numpy as np

c = 299792458.0  # m/s, exact

for d in [0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0]:
    t_ns = (2 * d / c) * 1e9
    print(f"d={d}m: round-trip time = {t_ns:.3f} ns")

for depth_precision_mm in [1, 10, 100]:
    d = depth_precision_mm / 1000.0
    t_ps = (2 * d / c) * 1e12
    print(f"depth precision {depth_precision_mm}mm needs timing precision: {t_ps:.2f} ps")
```

Output:

```text
d=0.5m: round-trip time = 3.336 ns
d=1.0m: round-trip time = 6.671 ns
d=5.0m: round-trip time = 33.356 ns
d=10.0m: round-trip time = 66.713 ns
depth precision 1mm needs timing precision: 6.67 ps
depth precision 10mm needs timing precision: 66.71 ps
depth precision 100mm needs timing precision: 667.13 ps
```

### 2. OpenCV/NumPy: real phase-based distance, and the real ambiguity

```python
f_mod = 20e6  # 20 MHz
max_range = c / (2 * f_mod)
print(f"max unambiguous range: {max_range:.3f} m")

for d in [1.0, 3.0, 6.0, 7.5, 9.0]:
    phase = (4 * np.pi * f_mod * d / c) % (2 * np.pi)
    d_recovered = phase * c / (4 * np.pi * f_mod)
    print(f"d={d}m: recovered={d_recovered:.4f}m")
```

Output:

```text
max unambiguous range: 7.495 m
d=1.0m: recovered=1.0000m
d=3.0m: recovered=3.0000m
d=6.0m: recovered=6.0000m
d=7.5m: recovered=0.0052m
d=9.0m: recovered=1.5052m
```

Once any real depth value exists — from ToF, stereo (42.1–42.3), structured light (42.4), or laser triangulation (42.5) — `cv2` works with it identically; the sensing *method* this module covers is entirely separate from the downstream image processing.

## OpenCV API notes

- **No `cv2` function implements time-of-flight sensing** — the real physics (Code sections 1-2) is pure, exact arithmetic on a known constant (`c`) and a measured time or phase; a real ToF *camera* (hardware) produces a depth map directly, which `cv2` can then read and process like any other depth image, exactly as 42.1's own stereo output would be used downstream.
- **`cv2.rgbd`** (contrib) and related modules work with depth images from any real source (stereo, structured light, or a real ToF camera) once they exist — the sensing *method* (this module's whole focus) is separate from what `cv2` does with the resulting depth map afterward.

## Where it fails

- **Direct ToF's real picosecond timing requirement** (Section 3) genuinely limits how cheaply and precisely it can be built — many consumer ToF products use the phase-based method specifically to avoid this real cost.
- **Phase-based ToF's real ambiguity** (Section 4) is not a software bug to patch — it is a real, physical consequence of measuring a value (phase) that genuinely repeats every `2π`; a scene that can exceed the chosen modulation frequency's real range needs a real design change (lower frequency, or multiple frequencies), not a software fix alone.
- **Strong ambient light or a highly reflective/absorbent surface** can overwhelm or starve a real ToF sensor's own emitted signal, the same real physical problem structured light (42.4) and laser triangulation (42.5) both face.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Assuming a higher modulation frequency is always better for phase-based ToF** — Section 4's own real formula shows a higher frequency *reduces* the maximum unambiguous range; it trades range for precision, not a free improvement.
- **Treating a confidently-returned ToF distance as automatically correct** — Section 4's own real demonstration shows a genuinely wrong, wrapped distance looks exactly like a normal, valid reading, with no built-in warning.
- **Expecting direct ToF to reach millimetre precision cheaply** — Section 3's own real picosecond timing requirement is a genuine, expensive engineering problem, not a software tuning knob.
- **Forgetting ToF needs no baseline at all**, then designing a ToF system with unnecessary triangulation-style geometric constraints left over from stereo or laser-triangulation thinking (42.1, 42.5).

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### Resolving phase ambiguity with multiple frequencies

A real, common fix for phase-based ToF's ambiguity (Section 4) is measuring phase at two or more different modulation frequencies: each frequency wraps at a different real range, and combining the two real phase measurements can uniquely determine the true distance over a much longer real span than either frequency alone could manage unambiguously — a real, practical technique used in several commercial ToF depth cameras.

### SPAD-based direct ToF

Modern direct-ToF sensors increasingly use Single-Photon Avalanche Diodes (SPADs), real detectors sensitive enough to register individual photons with real sub-nanosecond timing resolution, directly addressing Section 3's own extreme precision requirement at the hardware level — a genuinely different real engineering approach from the phase-based method this chapter's Section 4 covers in depth.

</GoDeeper>
