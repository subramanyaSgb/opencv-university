# 40.3 Optical flow

<Definition term="Optical flow">the apparent motion of brightness patterns between two frames, estimated as a vector (direction and distance) at a point or at every pixel — not just "did this pixel change" (40.1), but "which way, and how far, did it move."</Definition>

40.1 and 40.2 answered "where is something different." Optical flow answers a sharper question: for a given point, where did it actually *go* between one frame and the next? That one extra piece of information — a real velocity, not just a presence/absence mask — is exactly what the tracking methods later in this module (40.4 onward) need.

## 1. An analogy

Differencing is noticing a chess piece is no longer where it was. Optical flow is watching the hand move it and reading off exactly which square it landed on — the same event, but with the actual motion captured, not just its before-and-after evidence.

## 2. Two ways to estimate it

**Lucas-Kanade** [1] tracks a small set of chosen points (often `cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack`'s corners, 33.3) by assuming brightness is locally constant and solving a small least-squares problem over each point's neighbourhood; `cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK` does this across an image pyramid (20.1) so it can handle larger motions than a single-resolution search could. **Farneback** [2] instead fits a local polynomial approximation around *every* pixel and estimates a dense flow field — one vector per pixel, at a real cost in precision, as Section 3 measures directly.

## 3. A real, measured accuracy comparison

<FlowLab caption="Real cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK (sparse, red) and calcOpticalFlowFarneback (dense, blue) results. Switch to the real sample-ball clip to see the flow field around an actual moving object."/>

Shifting a real scene image by a known, exact (3.0, −1.0) px and estimating the motion back with both methods:

| Method | Mean recovered shift | Std |
| --- | --- | --- |
| True shift | (3.000, −1.000) | — |
| Lucas-Kanade (sparse, 25 tracked corners) | (2.9999, −1.0000) | (0.0005, 0.0006) |
| Farneback (dense, central region) | (2.751, −0.911) | (0.251, 0.088) |

Lucas-Kanade recovers the true shift to within a few ten-thousandths of a pixel on this clean test. Farneback, estimating a vector at *every* pixel rather than a handful of well-chosen ones, is real and useful, but measurably less precise — a genuine, quantified trade-off between coverage (every pixel) and precision (fewer, better-chosen points).

## 4. Tracking a real moving object across many real frames

A single point placed on 40.1/40.2's own moving uniform object, tracked frame-to-frame with Lucas-Kanade across 20 real frames of that synthetic video:

| Frame | Tracked x | True x |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 17.00 | 17 |
| 5 | 31.97 | 32 |
| 10 | 47.15 | 47 |
| 15 | 62.07 | 62 |

The tracked position stays within a fraction of a pixel of the true position across the whole sequence — this is exactly the real capability 40.4's MeanShift/CamShift and 40.5's Kalman filter will build on: a point (or region) that can genuinely be followed frame to frame, not just flagged as "different."

## 5. Industrial context: when flow earns its cost over differencing

A part moving steadily down a conveyor, where the *speed* itself is the measurement (parts/minute, or flagging a part moving abnormally fast or slow), needs optical flow's actual velocity — 40.1's blob detections alone say "something is here," never "how fast." A simple presence/absence alarm (something entered a zone) rarely needs flow at all; the extra computation only pays for itself when the motion's direction or speed is itself part of the answer.

### 2. OpenCV: real Lucas-Kanade and Farneback on a known shift

```python
# Needs a timing-sensitive run: Farneback's last few digits vary slightly between machines
import cv2

base = cv2.imread("sample-scene.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
H, W = base.shape
matrix = np.float32([[1, 0, 3.0], [0, 1, -1.0]])
shifted = cv2.warpAffine(base, matrix, (W, H), flags=cv2.INTER_LINEAR, borderMode=cv2.BORDER_REFLECT)

p0 = cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack(base, maxCorners=40, qualityLevel=0.1, minDistance=10)
p1, status, err = cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(base, shifted, p0, None)
good = status.flatten() == 1
disp = (p1[good] - p0[good]).reshape(-1, 2)
print("LK mean displacement:", disp.mean(axis=0))

flow = cv2.calcOpticalFlowFarneback(base, shifted, None, 0.5, 3, 15, 3, 5, 1.2, 0)
margin = 20
central = flow[margin:H - margin, margin:W - margin]
print("Farneback mean (central region):", central.reshape(-1, 2).mean(axis=0))
```

Typical output (one machine):

```text
LK mean displacement: [ 2.9998627 -1.0000175]
Farneback mean (central region): [ 2.7507453 -0.9111326]
```

## OpenCV API notes

- **`cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(prev, next, p0, None[, winSize, maxLevel, criteria])`**: returns `(p1, status, err)`; `status[i] == 1` means point `i` was tracked successfully — always filter by `status` (Code section 2) before trusting a point's new location.
- **`cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack`** (33.3) is the standard way to choose the points to track in the first place — corners and well-textured points give Lucas-Kanade a real, locally-unambiguous pattern to lock onto (Go deeper).
- **`cv2.calcOpticalFlowFarneback(prev, next, None, pyr_scale, levels, winsize, iterations, poly_n, poly_sigma, flags)`**: returns one `(dx, dy)` vector per pixel, `shape (H, W, 2)` — no point selection needed, at the real precision cost Section 3 measured.
- **Both methods assume brightness constancy**: a tracked point's surroundings are assumed to keep roughly the same brightness between frames — real lighting changes or reflections violate this assumption directly.

## Where it fails

- **The aperture problem**: a point on a straight edge, or inside a uniform region, does not have enough local information to pin down motion *along* that edge/region — only motion perpendicular to it is actually constrained; `goodFeaturesToTrack`'s whole purpose is picking points (corners, texture) that avoid this (Go deeper).
- **Large motions need the pyramid** (`maxLevel`, 20.1) — searching only at full resolution, a motion bigger than the search window is simply missed; the pyramid solves progressively coarser-to-finer so a large motion is caught at a coarse level and refined at finer ones.
- **Dense flow is real but approximate everywhere at once** (Section 3) — useful for a whole-scene impression of motion, less suited to a measurement that needs sub-pixel precision at one specific point, which sparse tracking (Section 3's LK result) delivers instead.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">

- **Not checking `status` after `calcOpticalFlowPyrLK`** and using every returned point regardless of whether tracking actually succeeded for it.
- **Tracking points on a flat, textureless region** — the aperture problem (Where it fails) means these points have no reliable motion estimate to give, however confident the function's return value looks.
- **Treating dense Farneback output as exact** — Section 3's own real measurement shows a genuine, nonzero error even on a clean, idealised test; never assume per-pixel flow values are ground truth.
- **Forgetting the brightness-constancy assumption** — a scene with real lighting changes between frames (14.5, 40.2) violates the core assumption both methods depend on.

</Callout>

## 🔍 Go deeper

<GoDeeper>

### The aperture problem, concretely

Looking at a moving edge through a small window, only the component of motion *perpendicular* to the edge is actually observable — any amount of sliding *along* the edge looks identical through that window. A corner (two edges at different orientations) removes this ambiguity, which is exactly why `goodFeaturesToTrack` (33.3) selects points with strong gradients in more than one direction, rather than points on a single straight edge.

### Lucas-Kanade's underlying least-squares solve

For a small window around a point, Lucas-Kanade assumes every pixel in that window shares the same local motion `(u, v)`, and solves the resulting overdetermined system of brightness-constancy equations by least squares [1] — the system is well-conditioned exactly when the window contains gradients in more than one direction (the same corner-vs-edge distinction as the aperture problem above), which is why a poorly-chosen point can produce a numerically unstable, unreliable estimate even when the function returns successfully.

</GoDeeper>

### Exercise 3 (thinking)

A colleague wants to measure the exact speed of a single screw moving down a conveyor, to one tenth of a pixel per frame. Based on Section 3's own real numbers, should they use sparse Lucas-Kanade tracking on a chosen point, or dense Farneback flow averaged over the screw's region?

<Solution>

Sparse Lucas-Kanade on a well-chosen point (a real corner or textured feature of the screw, following `goodFeaturesToTrack`'s own selection criteria) is the better choice for this precision target — Section 3's own measurement showed LK accurate to a few ten-thousandths of a pixel, comfortably within a tenth-of-a-pixel requirement, while Farneback's real measured error (around a quarter of a pixel) would already exceed the required precision even before accounting for any additional real-world noise.

</Solution>
